# Job
When it happened: The book gives no date. Job's long life, wealth counted in flocks and herds, and his offering sacrifices for his own family resemble the age of the patriarchs, so many place the story in the second millennium BC.
When it was written: Proposed dates range from the time of Solomon to after the exile; one Jewish tradition credits Moses.
Where: "the land of Uz" (Job 1:1), probably east or southeast of Israel near Edom (compare Lamentations 4:21).
The world at the time: Job is not tied to any event in Israel's history. Raiding Sabeans and Chaldeans attack his herds, and his friends come from Teman, Shuah, and Naamath.

**Job 1-2** — In the land of Uz lived a man named Job, who was "perfect and upright, and one that feared God, and eschewed evil." He had seven sons, three daughters, and great wealth. When the sons of God came before the LORD, Satan claimed Job served God only because God had blessed him, and the LORD allowed Satan to test him. In one day Job lost his oxen, asses, camels, sheep, and servants, and all ten of his children. Job tore his robe, fell to the ground, and worshipped: "Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither: the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD." Then Satan struck Job with sore boils from head to foot. His wife told him to "curse God, and die," but "In all this did not Job sin with his lips." Three friends—Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar—came to comfort him and sat with him on the ground in silence for seven days and seven nights, for they saw that his grief was very great.

**Job 3-31** — Then Job cursed the day he was born. Round after round, his friends insisted that God always punishes the wicked and blesses the righteous, so Job must have sinned. Job called them "miserable comforters," held to his innocence, protested that the righteous suffer while the wicked prosper, and longed to bring his case before God. He asked, "If a man die, shall he live again?" yet declared, "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him," and still hoped: "For I know that my redeemer liveth." Searching for where wisdom can be found, he concluded that "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom." Job closed with a solemn oath that he had not committed any of the sins his friends implied, and "The words of Job are ended."

**Job 32-37** — A younger man named Elihu, the son of Barachel the Buzite, had waited for his elders to finish. He was angry with Job for justifying himself rather than God, and with the three friends for condemning Job without answering him. He argued that God speaks through dreams and suffering to warn and correct, that God cannot do wrong, and that God is great beyond human understanding, pointing to the thunder and the storm.

**Job 38-41** — Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind: "Gird up now thy loins like a man," and asked, "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?... when the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?" He questioned Job about the sea, the light, the stars, and the weather, about wild creatures—the lion, the wild ass, the ostrich, the horse, the hawk—and about behemoth and leviathan, displaying his wisdom and power over all creation. Job answered, "I will lay mine hand upon my mouth." God never explained the test in heaven; He revealed Himself instead.

**Job 42** — Job answered, "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." The LORD rebuked the three friends, because they had not spoken of him what was right, as Job had, and told them to offer sacrifices while Job prayed for them. "The LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends," and gave Job twice as much as he had before, along with seven sons and three daughters, Jemima, Kezia, and Kerenhappuch, the fairest women in the land, who received an inheritance with their brothers. Job lived another 140 years and saw four generations.

# Psalms
When it happened: The psalms span many centuries—from Psalm 90, titled "A Prayer of Moses," through the many psalms of David (about 1000 BC), to songs from the Babylonian exile (Psalm 137) and after.
When it was written: The 150 psalms were written over many centuries. Their titles name David most often (73 psalms), along with Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Heman, and Ethan; many are untitled. They were gathered over time into five books, likely completed after the exile.
Where: Many are tied to worship at Jerusalem and the temple; some to moments in David's life, such as the wilderness of Judah (Psalm 63); and one to the rivers of Babylon (Psalm 137).
The world at the time: The psalms were Israel's hymnbook and prayer book. David organized Levite singers and musicians for worship (1 Chronicles 25), and the psalms were sung at the temple and on pilgrimages to Jerusalem.

**The Righteous Find Refuge in God (Psalms 1:1–10:18)** — The opening psalms contrast righteousness with wickedness, introduce God's chosen king, and express confidence that the Lord hears those who seek Him.

Psalm 1 sets two paths before the reader: the blessed man who delights in God's law is “like a tree planted by the rivers of water,” while the ungodly are like chaff the wind drives away. Psalm 2 shows the nations raging against the Lord and His anointed, to whom God says, “Thou art my Son; this day have I begotten thee,” a verse the New Testament applies to Christ. Psalm 3 is titled as David's prayer when he fled from Absalom. Psalm 8 marvels, “O LORD our Lord, how excellent is thy name in all the earth,” and asks, “What is man, that thou art mindful of him?”

**David Seeks Protection, Justice, and Guidance (Psalms 11:1–20:9)** — David's prayers move between danger and confidence, emphasizing God's righteousness, reliable word, and care for those who trust Him.

“The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God,” begins Psalm 14, while Psalm 16 trusts that God will not leave His holy one to see corruption, words Peter later applied to the resurrection. Psalm 18 repeats David's song of deliverance from 2 Samuel 22. Psalm 19 declares, “The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork,” praises God's perfect law, and closes, “Let the words of my mouth, and the meditation of my heart, be acceptable in thy sight, O LORD, my strength, and my redeemer.” Psalm 20 adds, “Some trust in chariots, and some in horses: but we will remember the name of the LORD our God.”

**Suffering Gives Way to Praise and Trust (Psalms 21:1–30:12)** — These psalms celebrate God's protection of His king, express deep suffering, and present the Lord as a shepherd who faithfully guides His people.

Psalm 22 opens with the cry Jesus spoke from the cross, “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” and describes pierced hands and feet and enemies who “part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture,” before turning to praise. Psalm 23 follows: “The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want... Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me.” Psalm 24 proclaims, “The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof,” and Psalm 27 asks, “The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?” Psalm 30 promises, “weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning.”

**Confession, Deliverance, and God's Faithful Care (Psalms 31:1–41:13)** — David seeks refuge, celebrates forgiveness, and reflects on trust amid illness, betrayal, and opposition.

Psalm 31 contains the words Jesus prayed at His death, “Into thine hand I commit my spirit.” Psalm 32 rejoices, “Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered,” after David stopped hiding his sin. Psalm 34 invites, “O taste and see that the LORD is good,” and Psalm 37 counsels, “Delight thyself also in the LORD; and he shall give thee the desires of thine heart.” Psalm 40 begins, “I waited patiently for the LORD; and he inclined unto me, and heard my cry,” and Psalm 41 laments a familiar friend who lifted up his heel against him, a verse Jesus applied to Judas. The first book of Psalms closes with “Amen, and Amen.”

**Longing for God During Trouble and Exile (Psalms 42:1–49:20)** — Worshipers long for God's presence while celebrating His protection, kingship, and power over nations.

The second book opens with the sons of Korah, far from the temple: “As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God,” with the refrain, “Why art thou cast down, O my soul?... hope thou in God.” Psalm 45 is a royal wedding song, and Psalm 46 declares, “God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble,” and commands, “Be still, and know that I am God.” Psalms 47 and 48 celebrate God as King over all the earth and Zion as His city, and Psalm 49 warns that wealth cannot redeem anyone from death.

**God Desires Sincere Worship and Repentance (Psalms 50:1–60:12)** — God calls His people to genuine obedience while David's prayers show confession, humility, and dependence in conflict.

In Psalm 50, God reminds His people that He needs no sacrifices, for every beast is His, and “the cattle upon a thousand hills.” Psalm 51 is titled as David's prayer after Nathan confronted him over Bathsheba: “Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me,” for “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit.” Several following psalms are tied to David's flight from Saul, and Psalm 55, written in the pain of a friend's betrayal, urges, “Cast thy burden upon the LORD, and he shall sustain thee.”

**The Soul Finds Rest in God's Strength (Psalms 61:1–72:20)** — David's prayers emphasize trust and worship, while later songs celebrate God's justice and a righteous king.

David prays, “lead me to the rock that is higher than I,” and in Psalm 62 says his soul waits on God alone. Psalm 63, written in the wilderness of Judah, begins, “O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee.” Psalms 65 to 68 praise God for harvest, deliverance, and His victorious march, and Psalm 69 cries out from deep waters. Psalm 72, for Solomon, prays for a king who defends the poor and whose dominion reaches the ends of the earth. The second book ends, “The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended.”

**The Prosperity of the Wicked Challenges Faith (Psalms 73:1–83:18)** — Asaph and other worshipers wrestle with injustice, remember Israel's history, and call on God to defend His people.

Psalm 73 begins the third book with Asaph's confession: “I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked,” “Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end.” He concludes, “God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever.” Psalms 74 and 79 mourn the destruction of the sanctuary and Jerusalem. Psalm 78 retells Israel's history from Egypt to David as a warning not to repeat their fathers' unbelief, and Psalm 80 pleads, “Turn us again, O God,” for the vine God brought out of Egypt.

**God's Dwelling Place and Covenant Faithfulness (Psalms 84:1–89:52)** — These psalms celebrate worship in God's presence, seek restoration, and struggle with the apparent failure of promises to David.

“How amiable are thy tabernacles, O LORD of hosts!” sing the sons of Korah in Psalm 84, for “a day in thy courts is better than a thousand. I had rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God, than to dwell in the tents of wickedness.” Psalm 88 is the darkest of the psalms, ending in darkness without a turn to praise. Psalm 89 celebrates God's covenant with David, then laments that the crown has been cast to the ground: “Lord, where are thy former lovingkindnesses, which thou swarest unto David in thy truth?” The third book ends there.

**The Eternal God Rules Over Human Generations (Psalms 90:1–100:5)** — The collection turns toward God's everlasting nature, protection, and authority as King over the earth.

The fourth book opens with the only psalm credited to Moses: “LORD, thou hast been our dwelling place in all generations,” with the prayer, “So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.” Psalm 91 promises, “He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty,” the psalm Satan quoted when tempting Jesus. Psalms 93 to 99 proclaim that the Lord reigns. Psalm 95 calls, “O come, let us worship and bow down: let us kneel before the LORD our maker,” and Psalm 100 invites, “Make a joyful noise unto the LORD, all ye lands... Enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise.”

**God's Mercy Endures Through Human Weakness (Psalms 101:1–107:43)** — Worshipers reflect on righteous leadership, suffering, forgiveness, creation, and God's repeated rescue in distress.

Psalm 103 begins, “Bless the LORD, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name,” and rejoices, “As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us.” Psalm 104 celebrates God's care for all creation, and Psalms 105 and 106 retell Israel's history, first as God's faithfulness and then as Israel's repeated rebellion; the fourth book ends with Psalm 106. Psalm 107 opens the fifth book with stories of the lost, the imprisoned, the sick, and “They that go down to the sea in ships,” each crying to the Lord in trouble and being delivered.

**Praise, Prayer, and Confidence in God's Victory (Psalms 108:1–118:29)** — David's prayers seek deliverance, while songs celebrate God's rule, care for the needy, and lasting mercy.

Psalm 110, the psalm most quoted in the New Testament, says, “The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool,” and, “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek.” Psalms 113 to 118 praise God for lifting the poor and bringing Israel out of Egypt; Psalm 117 is the shortest chapter in the Bible. Psalm 118 declares, “The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner,” words Jesus applied to Himself, and, “This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it.”

**God's Word Guides the Faithful Through Every Trial (Psalms 119:1–176)** — An extended meditation celebrates God's law and promises as wisdom, comfort, guidance, and hope.

The longest psalm and the longest chapter in the Bible, Psalm 119 has one hundred seventy-six verses in twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, beginning with Aleph. Nearly every verse names God's word by terms such as law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, and judgments. The psalmist says, “Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee,” and, “Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path.”

**The Pilgrims Look to God for Help (Psalms 120:1–134:3)** — Songs of Ascents celebrate trust, unity, worship, and God's protection as people look toward Jerusalem.

Each of these fifteen psalms is titled “A Song of degrees” in the King James Version, and they are often linked with pilgrims going up to Jerusalem. “I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help,” begins Psalm 121, and Psalm 122 rejoices, “I was glad when they said unto me, Let us go into the house of the LORD.” Psalm 126 promises, “They that sow in tears shall reap in joy,” Psalm 127 warns, “Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it,” and Psalm 130 cries, “Out of the depths have I cried unto thee, O LORD.” Psalm 133 celebrates, “Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!”

**God's Greatness Is Remembered Through History (Psalms 135:1–139:24)** — Worshipers praise God's authority, remember mercy through history, and reflect on His complete knowledge of every person.

Psalm 136 retells creation and Israel's history with the same refrain in all twenty-six verses: “for his mercy endureth for ever.” Psalm 137 remembers the exile: “By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion... How shall we sing the LORD'S song in a strange land?” and ends with a bitter cry for judgment on Edom and Babylon. In Psalm 139, David marvels that God knows him completely, asking, “Whither shall I go from thy spirit?” and praising, “I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made,” before praying, “Search me, O God, and know my heart.”

**David Seeks Deliverance and Teaches Dependence on God (Psalms 140:1–145:21)** — David seeks rescue and guidance before turning to praise for God's goodness and everlasting kingdom.

David prays for protection from violent and deceitful men and asks, “Set a watch, O LORD, before my mouth.” Psalm 142 is titled as his prayer when he was in the cave, hiding from Saul. Psalm 143 pleads for mercy, for no living person is righteous before God, and Psalm 144 blesses the Lord who trains his hands for war. Psalm 145, the last psalm credited to David, declares, “Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised,” and, “The LORD is nigh unto all them that call upon him.”

**All Creation Is Called to Praise the Lord (Psalms 146:1–150:6)** — The final songs celebrate God's justice, care, power, and greatness, calling every living creature to praise Him.

Each of the last five psalms begins and ends with “Praise ye the LORD.” They warn against trusting in princes, praise the God who “healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds,” and call on the angels, the sun and moon, the sea creatures, the mountains, and all peoples to praise Him. The book ends with trumpet, harp, timbrel, and cymbals: “Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD. Praise ye the LORD.”

# Proverbs
When it happened: Most of the proverbs come from King Solomon's reign (about 970–930 BC). Chapters 25–29 were copied out by "the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" (Proverbs 25:1) around 700 BC.
When it was written: The book was gathered in stages: Solomon's proverbs (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the words of the wise" (22:17; 24:23), the collection copied by Hezekiah's men (chapters 25–29), and the words of Agur (chapter 30) and King Lemuel (chapter 31). It reached its final form in or after Hezekiah's time.
Where: The royal court at Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Collections of wise sayings were common across the ancient Near East, especially in Egypt. 1 Kings 4:30–32 says Solomon's wisdom "excelled the wisdom of all the children of the east country, and all the wisdom of Egypt."

**Wisdom Begins with Reverence for God (Proverbs 1:1–2:22)** — Solomon introduces wisdom as righteous living that begins with reverence for the Lord. Wisdom warns against violence and folly, guards the upright, and leads away from destructive paths.

The book's theme comes in its first chapter: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise wisdom and instruction.” A father urges his son, “My son, if sinners entice thee, consent thou not,” warning that those who lie in wait for others' blood end up ambushing themselves. Wisdom cries out in the streets, warning that those who refuse her call will one day seek her and not find her. Chapter 2 promises that those who search for wisdom as for hidden treasure will understand the fear of the Lord.

**Trusting God Shapes a Wise Life (Proverbs 3:1–4:27)** — Trust in the Lord, accept correction, value wisdom above riches, help neighbors, and guard the heart. The righteous path grows brighter while the wicked stumble in darkness.

The best-known counsel in the book comes here: “Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths.” The son is told not to despise God's chastening, “for whom the LORD loveth he correcteth.” Chapter 4 promises that “the path of the just is as the shining light, that shineth more and more unto the perfect day,” and urges, “Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life.”

**Wisdom Warns Against Adultery and Destructive Desires (Proverbs 5:1–7:27)** — Solomon warns that adultery and uncontrolled desires destroy life, while faithfulness in marriage protects it. He also warns about rash pledges, laziness, and violent or dishonest behavior.

Instead of the strange woman whose steps lead to death, the son is told to “rejoice with the wife of thy youth.” The sluggard is sent to school: “Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise.” Chapter 6 lists six things the Lord hates, yea, seven, from a proud look to one who sows discord among brethren. Chapter 7 tells of a young man void of understanding who follows an adulteress home “as an ox goeth to the slaughter.”

**Wisdom Calls Everyone to Choose Life (Proverbs 8:1–9:18)** — Wisdom and Folly are pictured as competing invitations. Wisdom offers truth, justice, understanding, and life; Folly offers secrecy and stolen pleasure but leads toward death.

Wisdom speaks at the city gates and declares that she was present before creation: “The LORD possessed me in the beginning of his way, before his works of old,” rejoicing beside Him as He set the heavens and the seas in place. In chapter 9 Wisdom has built her house and set her table, for “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom.” Folly calls from her doorway that “Stolen waters are sweet,” but her guests are in the depths of hell.

**Everyday Choices Reveal Wisdom or Foolishness (Proverbs 10:1–12:28)** — Short sayings contrast righteous and wicked conduct in speech, work, honesty, generosity, correction, and care for others. They present general patterns rather than guarantees for every circumstance.

Solomon's collection of two-line sayings begins, “A wise son maketh a glad father.” They warn that “A false balance is abomination to the LORD,” that “When pride cometh, then cometh shame,” and that lying lips are an abomination, while truthful lips endure forever. Most contrast two kinds of people in a single verse: the diligent and the slothful, the generous and the stingy, the one who loves correction and the one who hates it.

**Wise Words and Humble Hearts Preserve Relationships (Proverbs 13:1–15:33)** — These sayings praise discipline, diligence, gentle speech, patience, wise companions, and humility. God sees hearts and motives, and wisdom grows through counsel and reverence.

These chapters observe that “Hope deferred maketh the heart sick,” and warn, “There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death.” They teach that “A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger,” and that “Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people.” Walking with wise companions makes a person wise, and the eyes of the Lord are in every place, beholding the evil and the good.

**God Values Justice, Humility, and Self-Control (Proverbs 16:1–18:24)** — Human plans remain subject to God. The sayings commend justice, humility, self-control, careful listening, faithful friendship, and trust in the Lord over wealth or pride.

“A man's heart deviseth his way: but the LORD directeth his steps,” and even the lot cast into the lap is decided by the Lord. Here are some of the book's most familiar lines: “Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall,” “A friend loveth at all times,” and “A merry heart doeth good like a medicine.” Chapter 18 adds, “The name of the LORD is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe,” and speaks of “a friend that sticketh closer than a brother.”

**Integrity Matters More Than Wealth or Status (Proverbs 19:1–21:31)** — Integrity, compassion, responsibility, justice, and wise planning are valued above wealth and ambition. Preparation matters, but victory belongs to the Lord.

“He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD,” and many devices fill a person's heart, but the Lord's counsel stands. “Wine is a mocker,” and whoever is deceived by it is not wise. Even rulers are under God's hand: “The king's heart is in the hand of the LORD.” The section ends, “The horse is prepared against the day of battle: but safety is of the LORD.”

**Wise Living Requires Discipline and Compassion (Proverbs 22:1–24:34)** — A good name, fairness, self-control, wise friendships, diligence, and care for the vulnerable are commended. The neglected field shows how ruin grows through laziness.

“A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches,” and parents are told, “Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it.” A new collection, “the words of the wise,” warns against robbing the poor, befriending an angry man, and envying sinners. The section closes with the field of the slothful, overgrown with thorns and its wall broken down, and the warning that “a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep” brings poverty like an armed man.

**Hezekiah's Men Preserve More of Solomon's Wisdom (Proverbs 25:1–27:27)** — Hezekiah's men copy further proverbs about leadership, humility, conflict, friendship, self-control, careful speech, and responsible care for household resources.

“A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver,” and kindness answers hostility: “If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat,” a saying Paul quotes in Romans. Chapter 26 sets two proverbs side by side, “Answer not a fool according to his folly” and “Answer a fool according to his folly,” showing that wisdom must judge the situation. Chapter 27 warns, “Boast not thyself of to morrow,” and teaches that “Iron sharpeneth iron,” as one friend sharpens another.

**Righteous Leadership Protects the Vulnerable (Proverbs 28:1–29:27)** — Righteous leadership, honest work, confession, correction, justice for the poor, and trust in the Lord are contrasted with corruption, greed, hardened hearts, and fear of people.

“The wicked flee when no man pursueth: but the righteous are bold as a lion.” Confession brings mercy: “He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy.” Chapter 29 warns, “Where there is no vision, the people perish,” and, “The fear of man bringeth a snare,” but whoever trusts in the Lord shall be safe.

**Agur Reflects on Human Limits and Contentment (Proverbs 30:1–33)** — Agur humbly acknowledges human limits, affirms God's pure word, asks for neither poverty nor riches, and draws wisdom from nature while warning against pride and anger.

Agur the son of Jakeh confesses that he has not learned wisdom and asks who has ascended into heaven or gathered the wind in his fists. He affirms, “Every word of God is pure: he is a shield unto them that put their trust in him.” He prays, “give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me,” lest he grow full and deny God or grow poor and steal. Then he gives numbered sayings, “three things... yea, four,” drawn from ants, conies, locusts, spiders, and the way of an eagle in the air.

**King Lemuel Learns Justice and Self-Control (Proverbs 31:1–9)** — Lemuel's mother warns him against destructive pleasures and urges him to use authority with self-control, righteous judgment, and defense of the poor and needy.

King Lemuel records the words his mother taught him. She warns him not to give his strength to women who destroy kings, and says, “it is not for kings to drink wine,” lest they forget the law and pervert justice. Instead he must “Open thy mouth for the dumb,” judging righteously and pleading the cause of the poor and needy.

**The Virtuous Woman Is Honored for Her Wisdom (Proverbs 31:10–31)** — The final poem honors a capable woman known for diligence, generosity, wisdom, kindness, faithfulness, and reverence for the Lord.

The book closes with an acrostic poem, each verse beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet: “Who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies.” She works willingly with her hands, buys a field and plants a vineyard, reaches out to the poor, and speaks with wisdom and kindness, and her husband and children praise her. The poem ends, “Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised.”

# Ecclesiastes
When it happened: The book reflects on life rather than telling events. Its speaker, the Preacher, calls himself "the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:1), traditionally Solomon, about 950 BC.
When it was written: Many scholars date the book later than Solomon, in the Persian or Greek period, because of its style of Hebrew, and see the Preacher's royal voice as a literary role.
Where: Jerusalem.
The world at the time: The Preacher writes from the position of a king with every advantage—wisdom, wealth, great building projects, and every pleasure—and tests whether any of it gives lasting meaning.

**The Search for Meaning in a Changing World (Ecclesiastes 1:1–18)** — The Preacher examines life's cycles and declares all is vanity: human effort, memory, and even wisdom cannot answer every problem beneath the sun.

The book opens with its refrain: “Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity.” Generations come and go, the sun rises and sets, the wind circles, and the rivers run to the sea, yet the sea is never full. “There is no new thing under the sun,” and no one remembers those who came before. The Preacher set his heart to search out wisdom but found that “in much wisdom is much grief.”

**Pleasure, Wealth, and Achievement Cannot Last (Ecclesiastes 2:1–26)** — Pleasure, wealth, accomplishments, and wisdom cannot overcome death or guarantee lasting satisfaction. Enjoyment of labor is received as a gift from God, not permanent security.

The Preacher tried laughter and wine, built houses, planted vineyards and gardens, made pools, and gathered servants, silver, gold, and singers, denying himself nothing. When he looked at it all, “all was vanity and vexation of spirit.” Wisdom is better than folly, yet the wise man dies just as the fool does, and his labor goes to someone who did not work for it. He concludes that eating, drinking, and enjoying one's labor is good, and that this too is from the hand of God.

**God Appoints a Time for Every Purpose (Ecclesiastes 3:1–22)** — Life has changing seasons under God's authority. People cannot discover all His work, so they are encouraged to do good and receive their labor as God's gift while revering Him.

The famous poem begins, “To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: A time to be born, and a time to die,” and continues through planting and plucking up, weeping and laughing, mourning and dancing, war and peace. God “hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart,” so that people cannot find out the whole of His work. What God does endures forever, and He will judge the righteous and the wicked.

**Oppression and Competition Leave People Empty (Ecclesiastes 4:1–16)** — The Preacher sees oppression, envy, loneliness, and unstable public honor. He values modest peace and praises companionship as strength in a troubled world.

The Preacher saw the tears of the oppressed with no one to comfort them, and labor driven by envy of one's neighbor. He watched a man work endlessly with no one to share his wealth. “Two are better than one,” he says, for if one falls the other lifts him up, and “a threefold cord is not quickly broken.” Even a king's popularity fades, as the crowds turn to the next young ruler.

**Approaching God with Reverence and Honesty (Ecclesiastes 5:1–20)** — Careless worship and unfulfilled vows are warned against. Wealth cannot satisfy or follow a person into death, yet the ability to enjoy honest work and possessions is God's gift.

The Preacher urges caution in worship, for “God is in heaven, and thou upon earth: therefore let thy words be few,” and it is better not to vow than to vow and not pay. “He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver,” and the laborer sleeps sweetly while the rich man's abundance keeps him awake. Naked a person comes from the womb and naked returns, taking nothing of his labor, yet the power to enjoy one's portion is the gift of God.

**Riches and Long Life Cannot Guarantee Satisfaction (Ecclesiastes 6:1–12)** — Wealth, honor, children, and long life cannot guarantee enjoyment. Human desires remain unsatisfied, and people cannot know every answer or what is truly good.

A man may have riches, wealth, and honor, a hundred children, and a long life, yet if he cannot enjoy them, a stillborn child is better off than he. All a person's labor is for his mouth, yet his appetite is never filled. No one can contend with the One who is mightier, and no one can tell what is good for a person in this vain life or what will come after him under the sun.

**Wisdom Faces Sorrow, Injustice, and Uncertainty (Ecclesiastes 7:1–29)** — Sorrow, correction, patience, and humility can teach wisdom, but righteous people still suffer and complete understanding remains beyond human reach.

“A good name is better than precious ointment,” and the house of mourning teaches more than the house of feasting, for death is the end of every person. The Preacher counsels patience over pride and receiving both prosperity and adversity from God's hand. He admits, “there is not a just man upon earth, that doeth good, and sinneth not,” and concludes, “God hath made man upright; but they have sought out many inventions.”

**Human Authority Remains Under God's Judgment (Ecclesiastes 8:1–17)** — Wisdom guides responses to rulers, but no authority controls death or guarantees immediate justice. The Preacher maintains reverence for God while admitting His work cannot be fully discovered.

The wise keep the king's command, yet no one has power over the day of death. Because sentence against an evil work is not carried out speedily, people's hearts are set to do evil; still, it will be well with those who fear God. The Preacher saw the righteous treated as the wicked deserve and the wicked as the righteous deserve, and he commended joy, since no one, however wise, can find out all the work of God.

**Death Comes to Everyone, but Life Still Matters (Ecclesiastes 9:1–18)** — Death comes to all and success is unpredictable, but those alive should enjoy God's gifts and work wisely. Wisdom can deliver even when it is later forgotten.

The same end comes to the righteous and the wicked, yet a living dog is better than a dead lion. So the Preacher urges, eat your bread with joy, live joyfully with your wife, and “Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might,” for there is no work in the grave. He observes that “the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong,” but time and chance happen to all. He tells of a poor wise man who saved his city, yet “no man remembered that same poor man.”

**Foolishness Can Undo Great Wisdom (Ecclesiastes 10:1–20)** — Small foolishness damages honor. The Preacher warns about poor leadership, careless speech, laziness, and decisions that spread harm.

“Dead flies cause the ointment of the apothecary to send forth a stinking savour,” and a little folly does the same to a man known for wisdom. The Preacher has seen fools set in high places and servants riding while princes walk. Through laziness a building decays, and through idle hands the house leaks. He warns against cursing the king even in private, for “a bird of the air shall carry the voice.”

**Act Wisely Despite an Uncertain Future (Ecclesiastes 11:1–10)** — Uncertainty should not prevent generosity and diligent work. People should enjoy life responsibly, remembering that God brings actions into judgment.

“Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it after many days,” and give to many, since you do not know what evil may come. Whoever waits for perfect weather will never sow or reap, so sow in the morning and in the evening. Young people are told to rejoice in their youth, but to know that for all these things God will bring them into judgment.

**Remember the Creator Before Life Fades (Ecclesiastes 12:1–8)** — Poetic images of aging and death urge people to remember the Creator before strength fades, dust returns to earth, and the spirit returns to God.

“Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth,” before the evil days come. The Preacher pictures old age as a house falling silent: trembling keepers, few grinders, darkened windows, and the sound of birds fading, until the silver cord is loosed and the golden bowl broken. “Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.” The Preacher's refrain returns once more: all is vanity.

**The Final Duty of Humanity Is Revealed (Ecclesiastes 12:9–14)** — The conclusion calls humanity to fear God and keep His commandments, for God will judge every work. Life is brief and understanding limited, but God's gifts are to be received gratefully and responsibly.

The Preacher taught the people knowledge and set many proverbs in order, seeking acceptable and upright words given from one shepherd. He warns, “of making many books there is no end; and much study is a weariness of the flesh.” Then comes the conclusion of the whole matter: “Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man,” for God will bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether good or evil.

# Song of Solomon
When it happened: The poem does not describe historical events. It is associated with the era of Solomon (about 950 BC).
When it was written: The title "The song of songs, which is Solomon's" (Song of Solomon 1:1) can mean it was written by Solomon or dedicated to him. Its date is debated.
Where: Jerusalem and the countryside of Israel—vineyards and gardens, Lebanon, Sharon, En-gedi, Gilead, and Tirzah.
The world at the time: Love poetry was well known in the ancient world. Jewish readers have long read the Song at Passover as a picture of God's love for Israel, and many Christians have read it as a picture of Christ and the church, as well as a celebration of married love.

**A Young Woman Expresses Her Love (Song of Solomon 1:1–17)** — A young woman and the man she loves exchange poetic expressions of affection, beauty, fragrance, and the joy of being together.

The song opens with the woman's longing: “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth: for thy love is better than wine.” Darkened by the sun from keeping her brothers' vineyards, she tells the daughters of Jerusalem, “I am black, but comely.” She asks where her beloved feeds his flock, and he praises her beauty, comparing her eyes to doves, as they delight in their green bed beneath cedar beams.

**Love Awakens Among the Flowers (Song of Solomon 2:1–17)** — The lovers celebrate affection through spring and vineyard imagery. The woman warns the daughters of Jerusalem not to awaken love before its proper time.

She says, “I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys,” and rejoices that at his banqueting house “his banner over me was love.” She charges the daughters of Jerusalem “that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please,” a refrain repeated later in the song. Her beloved comes leaping over the hills to call her away, “For, lo, the winter is past, the rain is over and gone,” and they guard against “the little foxes, that spoil the vines.” She declares, “My beloved is mine, and I am his.”

**The Woman Searches for Her Beloved (Song of Solomon 3:1–11)** — The woman describes searching for her beloved at night before the poem turns to Solomon's magnificent wedding procession and royal carriage.

“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth,” she says, and she searches the city streets until she finds him and will not let him go. Then a procession rises from the wilderness like pillars of smoke: Solomon's bed surrounded by sixty valiant men, and a chariot of cedar, silver, and gold. The daughters of Zion are called to see King Solomon wearing the crown his mother gave him on the day of his wedding.

**The Man Celebrates His Beloved's Beauty (Song of Solomon 4:1–16)** — The man praises the woman through poetic images of nature and precious things, calling her a treasured garden and sealed fountain; she welcomes him to enjoy its fruits.

The man describes her beauty from head to toe in images of doves, flocks of goats, pomegranates, and the tower of David, and says, “Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee.” He calls her “A garden inclosed is my sister, my spouse; a spring shut up, a fountain sealed,” full of spices and fruits. She answers by inviting the north and south winds to blow on her garden and her beloved to come into it.

**Affection Gives Way to Longing and Separation (Song of Solomon 5:1–16)** — After shared delight, the woman searches for her absent beloved and describes him as outstanding among thousands, both her beloved and her friend.

The woman says, “I sleep, but my heart waketh,” and hears her beloved knocking, but by the time she opens the door, he has gone. Searching for him in the city at night, she is beaten by the watchmen, and she asks the daughters of Jerusalem to tell him she is sick with love. When they ask what makes him better than others, she describes him as “the chiefest among ten thousand” and concludes, “This is my beloved, and this is my friend.”

**The Lovers Renew Their Expressions of Devotion (Song of Solomon 6:1–13)** — The woman declares mutual belonging, while the man praises her as uniquely beautiful and admired, using images of cities, gardens, morning, moon, and sun.

The woman knows her beloved has gone down to his garden and says, “I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine.” He praises her as beautiful as Tirzah and Jerusalem, his one undefiled dove among many queens. Others ask who she is who looks forth as the morning, “fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners,” and they call to the Shulamite to return.

**Their Love Is Celebrated with Joy and Desire (Song of Solomon 7:1–13)** — The man praises the woman's beauty, and she responds with confident affection, inviting him into fields and vineyards where blooming plants continue the poem's celebration of love.

He praises her from her feet to her head, comparing her stature to a palm tree. She answers, “I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me,” and invites him to go out to the fields and lodge in the villages, to see whether the vines have budded and the pomegranates bloomed, where she will give him her love.

**Love Is Strong and Cannot Be Bought (Song of Solomon 8:1–14)** — The final exchanges celebrate love's proper timing, enduring strength, and value beyond wealth. Love is strong as death and cannot be extinguished or purchased; the lovers call to one another at the close.

The woman asks, “Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death,” and declares, “Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it: if a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned.” Her brothers speak of their little sister, and the woman says her own vineyard is hers to give. The song ends with her call, “Make haste, my beloved.”
