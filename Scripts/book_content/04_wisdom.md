# Job
When it happened: The book gives no date. Job's long life, wealth counted in flocks and herds, and his offering sacrifices for his own family resemble the age of the patriarchs, so many place the story in the second millennium BC.
When it was written: Proposed dates range from the time of Solomon to after the exile; one Jewish tradition credits Moses.
Where: "the land of Uz" (Job 1:1), probably east or southeast of Israel near Edom (compare Lamentations 4:21).
The world at the time: Job is not tied to any event in Israel's history. Raiding Sabeans and Chaldeans attack his herds, and his friends come from Teman, Shuah, and Naamath.

**Job 1-2** — In the land of Uz lived a man named Job, who was "perfect and upright, and one that feared God, and eschewed evil." He had seven sons, three daughters, and great wealth. When the sons of God came before the LORD, Satan claimed Job served God only because God had blessed him, and the LORD allowed Satan to test him. In one day Job lost his oxen, asses, camels, sheep, and servants, and all ten of his children. Job tore his robe, fell to the ground, and worshipped: "the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD." Then Satan struck Job with sore boils from head to foot. His wife told him to "curse God, and die," but "In all this did not Job sin with his lips." Three friends—Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar—came to comfort him and sat with him on the ground in silence for seven days.

**Job 3-31** — Then Job cursed the day he was born. Round after round, his friends insisted that God always punishes the wicked and blesses the righteous, so Job must have sinned. Job held to his innocence, protested that the righteous suffer while the wicked prosper, and longed to bring his case before God—yet still hoped: "For I know that my redeemer liveth." Searching for where wisdom can be found, he concluded that "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom."

**Job 32-37** — A younger man named Elihu, angry with both Job and the friends, spoke at length. He argued that God uses suffering to warn and correct, and that God is great beyond human understanding.

**Job 38-41** — Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind: "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?" He questioned Job about the sea, the light, the stars, and the weather, about wild creatures—the lion, the wild ass, the ostrich, the horse, the hawk—and about behemoth and leviathan, displaying his wisdom and power over all creation.

**Job 42** — Job answered, "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." The LORD rebuked the three friends, because they had not spoken of him what was right, as Job had, and told them to offer sacrifices while Job prayed for them. The LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before, along with seven sons and three daughters, and Job lived another 140 years.

# Psalms
When it happened: The psalms span many centuries—from Psalm 90, titled "A Prayer of Moses," through the many psalms of David (about 1000 BC), to songs from the Babylonian exile (Psalm 137) and after.
When it was written: The 150 psalms were written over many centuries. Their titles name David most often (73 psalms), along with Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Heman, and Ethan; many are untitled. They were gathered over time into five books, likely completed after the exile.
Where: Many are tied to worship at Jerusalem and the temple; some to moments in David's life, such as the wilderness of Judah (Psalm 63); and one to the rivers of Babylon (Psalm 137).
The world at the time: The psalms were Israel's hymnbook and prayer book. David organized Levite singers and musicians for worship (1 Chronicles 25), and the psalms were sung at the temple and on pilgrimages to Jerusalem.

**Psalms 1-2** — The Psalms are the songs and prayers of Israel, gathered over centuries. The collection opens with two gateways: the blessed man whose "delight is in the law of the LORD," who is "like a tree planted by the rivers of water," and the LORD's anointed King, set on his holy hill of Zion despite the raging of the nations.

**Psalms 3-41** — The first book is almost entirely psalms of David. Many come from times of danger—the first is titled for the time he fled from his son Absalom—and they move from lament to trust. Here are "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want," "The heavens declare the glory of God," and the cry "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"

**Psalms 42-72** — The second book opens with the sons of Korah: "As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God." It includes Psalm 51, David's prayer of repentance after Nathan confronted him about Bath-sheba—"Create in me a clean heart, O God"—and ends with a psalm for Solomon and the note, "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended."

**Psalms 73-89** — The third book, mostly psalms of Asaph and the sons of Korah, wrestles with hard questions: why the wicked prosper, and why God has let enemies destroy his sanctuary. It closes with Psalm 89, which recalls God's covenant with David and asks how long his anger will last.

**Psalms 90-106** — The fourth book begins with the prayer of Moses: "from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God." Its psalms declare that "The LORD reigneth," call all the earth to "Make a joyful noise unto the LORD," and retell Israel's history of God's faithfulness and the people's unfaithfulness.

**Psalms 107-150** — The fifth book gathers thanksgiving and praise. It includes Psalm 119, the longest chapter in the Bible, an acrostic in praise of God's word ("Thy word is a lamp unto my feet"); the Songs of Degrees sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem; a lament from the exile, "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion"; and David's "I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made." The book ends in a crescendo of praise: "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD. Praise ye the LORD."

# Proverbs
When it happened: Most of the proverbs come from King Solomon's reign (about 970–930 BC). Chapters 25–29 were copied out by "the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" (Proverbs 25:1) around 700 BC.
When it was written: The book was gathered in stages: Solomon's proverbs (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the words of the wise" (22:17; 24:23), the collection copied by Hezekiah's men (chapters 25–29), and the words of Agur (chapter 30) and King Lemuel (chapter 31). It reached its final form in or after Hezekiah's time.
Where: The royal court at Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Collections of wise sayings were common across the ancient Near East, especially in Egypt. 1 Kings 4:30–32 says Solomon's wisdom "excelled the wisdom of all the children of the east country, and all the wisdom of Egypt."

**Proverbs 1-9** — "The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel" begin with their purpose: to give wisdom, instruction, and understanding, especially to the young. "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge." In a series of talks, a father urges his son to listen, to avoid violent men and the flattering adulteress, and to "Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding." The lazy man is told, "Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise." Wisdom herself is pictured as a woman calling out in the streets, present with God at creation, inviting the simple into her house, while the foolish woman calls from her own door.

**Proverbs 10-22** — Then come hundreds of short sayings of Solomon about everyday life: work and laziness, speech, money, friendship, family, justice, and pride. "A soft answer turneth away wrath." "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." "Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it."

**Proverbs 22-24** — A section of "the words of the wise" gives longer sayings, including warnings against moving the ancient landmark, envying sinners, and lingering long at the wine.

**Proverbs 25-29** — More "proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out" follow, full of vivid comparisons: "As cold waters to a thirsty soul, so is good news from a far country." "If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat."

**Proverbs 30-31** — The book closes with the words of Agur, who marvels at things too wonderful for him, and the words of King Lemuel, taught by his mother to speak up for the poor and needy. Its final poem, an acrostic, praises the virtuous woman whose "price is far above rubies," who works with willing hands, cares for her household, and reaches out to the poor. It ends, "a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised."

# Ecclesiastes
When it happened: The book reflects on life rather than telling events. Its speaker, the Preacher, calls himself "the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:1), traditionally Solomon, about 950 BC.
When it was written: Many scholars date the book later than Solomon, in the Persian or Greek period, because of its style of Hebrew, and see the Preacher's royal voice as a literary role.
Where: Jerusalem.
The world at the time: The Preacher writes from the position of a king with every advantage—wisdom, wealth, great building projects, and every pleasure—and tests whether any of it gives lasting meaning.

**Ecclesiastes 1-2** — "The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem" begin: "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher... all is vanity." Generations come and go, the sun rises and sets, and "there is no new thing under the sun." The Preacher set out to search out everything done under heaven. He tried wisdom; then pleasure, laughter, and wine; great building projects, gardens, servants, silver and gold, and music—"and, behold, all was vanity and vexation of spirit." The wise man and the fool both die and are forgotten.

**Ecclesiastes 3-6** — "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: A time to be born, and a time to die." God "hath made every thing beautiful in his time" and set the world in men's hearts, yet no one can find out his work from beginning to end. The Preacher saw oppression, labour driven by envy, and the loneliness of the man with no companion—"Two are better than one." He saw that whoever loves money is never satisfied, urged caution in worship and vows, and concluded that to enjoy food, drink, and one's work is the gift of God.

**Ecclesiastes 7-10** — He gathered sayings on wisdom and folly: a good name is better than precious ointment; there is not a just man on earth who does good and never sins; time and chance happen to everyone. "Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might."

**Ecclesiastes 11-12** — "Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it after many days." He called the young to rejoice in their youth, knowing that God will bring them into judgment: "Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth," before old age comes, the silver cord is loosed, and "the spirit shall return unto God who gave it." The book ends, "Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man."

# Song of Solomon
When it happened: The poem does not describe historical events. It is associated with the era of Solomon (about 950 BC).
When it was written: The title "The song of songs, which is Solomon's" (Song of Solomon 1:1) can mean it was written by Solomon or dedicated to him. Its date is debated.
Where: Jerusalem and the countryside of Israel—vineyards and gardens, Lebanon, Sharon, En-gedi, Gilead, and Tirzah.
The world at the time: Love poetry was well known in the ancient world. Jewish readers have long read the Song at Passover as a picture of God's love for Israel, and many Christians have read it as a picture of Christ and the church, as well as a celebration of married love.

**Song of Solomon 1-2** — "The song of songs, which is Solomon's" is a love poem told in the voices of a woman, her beloved, and the "daughters of Jerusalem." The woman, darkened by the sun from keeping the vineyards, longs for her beloved, and the two praise each other's beauty. She says, "I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys," and "his banner over me was love." He calls her to come away, for "the winter is past... The flowers appear on the earth." She charges the daughters of Jerusalem not to stir up love until it pleases.

**Song of Solomon 3-5** — At night she seeks the one her soul loves through the streets of the city and finds him. King Solomon's wedding procession comes up out of the wilderness. The beloved praises his bride: "Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee," and calls her "A garden inclosed." Later she is slow to open the door when he knocks, and when she does, he is gone. She searches the city, is struck by the watchmen, and tells the daughters of Jerusalem that her beloved is "the chiefest among ten thousand."

**Song of Solomon 6-8** — The lovers find each other again and delight in each other. Near the end comes the poem's great declaration: "for love is strong as death... Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it." The song closes with the lovers calling to one another.
