# Job
When it happened: The book gives no date. Job's long life, wealth counted in flocks and herds, and his offering sacrifices for his own family resemble the age of the patriarchs, so many place the story in the second millennium BC.
When it was written: One Jewish tradition credits Moses. Other proposed dates range from the time of Solomon to after the exile.
Where: "the land of Uz" (Job 1:1), probably east or southeast of Israel near Edom (compare Lamentations 4:21).
The world at the time: Job is not tied to any event in Israel's history. Raiding Sabeans and Chaldeans attack his herds, and his friends come from Teman, Shuah, and Naamath.

**Job Is Tested and Keeps His Integrity (Job 1:1–2:13)** — Satan is allowed to test Job, who loses his children, his wealth, and his health but does not curse God.

“There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job; and that man was perfect and upright, and one that feared God, and eschewed evil.” To eschew means to avoid. Job has seven sons, three daughters, and huge herds of sheep, camels, oxen, and donkeys. He is “the greatest of all the men of the east.” He even offers sacrifices for his children in case they have sinned.

Then the scene moves to heaven. The angels, called “the sons of God,” come before the LORD, and Satan comes with them. God says, “Hast thou considered my servant Job, that there is none like him in the earth?” Satan answers, “Doth Job fear God for nought?” God has put a hedge of protection around him. “But put forth thine hand now, and touch all that he hath, and he will curse thee to thy face.” God allows Satan to take everything Job has, but not to touch Job himself.

On one terrible day, messengers arrive one after another, each interrupting the last. Raiders have stolen the oxen and donkeys and killed the servants. Fire from heaven has burned up the sheep. Chaldeans have taken the camels. Then the worst news: a great wind struck the house where all ten of his children were feasting, and it collapsed on them. They are all dead. Job tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground, and worships: “Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither: the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD.” “In all this Job sinned not, nor charged God foolishly.”

Satan comes before God again and says, “Skin for skin, yea, all that a man hath will he give for his life.” If God touches Job's body, he will curse him. God allows it, but Satan must spare his life. Satan strikes Job with painful boils from the soles of his feet to the top of his head. Job sits in the ashes, scraping his sores with a piece of broken pottery. His wife says, “Dost thou still retain thine integrity? curse God, and die.” Job answers, “What? shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?” “In all this did not Job sin with his lips.”

Three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar, hear what happened and come to comfort him. When they see him from a distance, they hardly recognize him. They weep, tear their robes, and throw dust on their heads. Then they sit with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights without saying a word, “for they saw that his grief was very great.” Job never learns about the conversation in heaven.

**Job and His Friends Argue About Suffering (Job 3:1–31:40)** — Job's friends insist he must have sinned, while Job holds to his innocence and longs to meet God.

Finally Job breaks the silence and curses the day he was born: “Let the day perish wherein I was born.” He wishes he had died at birth. “Why is light given to a man whose way is hid?” What he feared most has happened to him.

Then begins a long debate in poetry, in three rounds. Each friend speaks, and Job answers each one. Their message is simple: God always punishes the wicked and blesses the good, so Job must have done something terrible. Eliphaz asks, “who ever perished, being innocent?” He admits, “man is born unto trouble, as the sparks fly upward,” but tells Job to confess and God will restore him. Bildad asks, “Doth God pervert judgment?” He suggests Job's children died for their sins. Zophar is the harshest. He says Job deserves even worse than he is getting, and asks, “Canst thou by searching find out God?”

Job refuses to admit to sins he did not commit. He knows God is great, but he cannot understand why God is treating him like an enemy. “How should man be just with God?” He wishes there were a “daysman,” a referee, between him and God. He describes how short and painful life is: “Man that is born of a woman is of few days, and full of trouble.” He asks, “If a man die, shall he live again?” Yet even in his anger, he holds on to God: “Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him.” He calls his friends “miserable comforters.” In his darkest hour, his faith rises to its highest point: “For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth: And though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God.”

Job longs to find God and argue his case: “Oh that I knew where I might find him!” He cannot find him anywhere, “But he knoweth the way that I take: when he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold.” He points out that in real life, wicked people often prosper and die peacefully. In chapter 28, he describes how people dig deep into the earth for silver and gold, but no one can mine wisdom or buy it. God alone knows where it is found: “Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom; and to depart from evil is understanding.”

Job remembers his past life, when he was honored at the city gate and helped the needy: “I was eyes to the blind, and feet was I to the lame.” Now young men mock him and spit in his face. In his last speech, he swears a solemn oath that he has not been sexually immoral, dishonest, cruel to his servants, unkind to the poor, or a worshiper of the sun and moon. “I made a covenant with mine eyes; why then should I think upon a maid?” He cries, “Oh that one would hear me!” and dares God to answer him. Then the narrator says, “The words of Job are ended.”

**Elihu Answers Job and His Friends (Job 32:1–37:24)** — A young man named Elihu speaks up and points to God's greatness in the storm.

Now a young man named Elihu, who has been listening all along, can no longer stay quiet. He is angry with Job for defending himself rather than God, and angry with the three friends for condemning Job without proving him wrong. He begins, “I am young, and ye are very old; wherefore I was afraid.” But he says wisdom does not come only with age: “The Spirit of God hath made me, and the breath of the Almighty hath given me life.”

Elihu tells Job that God does speak to people, through dreams and visions and even through pain and sickness, to turn them away from pride and save them from death. “Surely God will not do wickedly, neither will the Almighty pervert judgment.” He points to the wonders of nature, the rain, the lightning, the thunder, and the snow, and asks Job to consider how far beyond human understanding God is. As a storm seems to gather, Elihu concludes, “Touching the Almighty, we cannot find him out: he is excellent in power, and in judgment.” Elihu is never answered, by Job or by God.

**God Answers Job out of the Whirlwind (Job 38:1–41:34)** — God does not explain Job's suffering but shows him the wonders of creation.

“Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind.” At last God speaks, but he does not explain why Job suffered. Instead, he asks Job questions. “Who is this that darkeneth counsel by words without knowledge? Gird up now thy loins like a man; for I will demand of thee, and answer thou me.” “Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding.” Who measured it, and laid its cornerstone, “When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?”

God takes Job on a tour of creation. Who set the boundaries of the sea, saying, “Hitherto shalt thou come, but no further: and here shall thy proud waves be stayed”? Has Job ever commanded the morning to dawn, or walked in the depths of the sea? Has he entered the storehouses of the snow? “Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades, or loose the bands of Orion?” Then God turns to the animals. Who feeds the lions and the ravens? Who knows when the mountain goats give birth? Who set the wild donkey free? God describes the silly ostrich, the fearless warhorse that “saith among the trumpets, Ha, ha,” and the hawk and eagle soaring high. Job has no answers.

God asks, “Shall he that contendeth with the Almighty instruct him?” Job answers, “Behold, I am vile; what shall I answer thee? I will lay mine hand upon my mouth.” But God speaks again from the whirlwind: “Wilt thou condemn me, that thou mayest be righteous?” If Job can rule the world and humble every proud person, then God will admit he can save himself. Then God describes two enormous creatures. Behemoth is a mighty beast with bones like bars of iron. Leviathan is a terrifying sea monster: “Canst thou draw out leviathan with an hook?” No one dares stir it up, so who can stand before the God who made it?

**Job Repents and Is Restored (Job 42:1–17)** — Job humbles himself, prays for his friends, and receives twice as much as before.

Job answers the LORD, “I know that thou canst do every thing, and that no thought can be withholden from thee.” He admits that he spoke about “things too wonderful for me, which I knew not.” Then he says, “I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes.” Job still does not know why he suffered, but he has met God, and that is enough.

Then God turns to Eliphaz: “My wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends: for ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath.” The friends' neat explanations were wrong. Job's honest cries were closer to the truth. God tells them to offer sacrifices and have Job pray for them. “And the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends: also the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before.”

Job's brothers, sisters, and old friends come to comfort him and bring gifts. “So the LORD blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning.” He receives fourteen thousand sheep, six thousand camels, a thousand yoke of oxen, and a thousand female donkeys. He has seven more sons and three more daughters, Jemima, Kezia, and Kerenhappuch, the most beautiful women in the land, and Job gives them an inheritance along with their brothers, which was unusual in that time. Job lives another 140 years and sees four generations of his family. “So Job died, being old and full of days.”

# Psalms
When it happened: The psalms span many centuries—from Psalm 90, titled "A Prayer of Moses," through the many psalms of David (about 1000 BC), to songs from the Babylonian exile (Psalm 137) and after.
When it was written: The 150 psalms were written over many centuries. Their titles name David most often (73 psalms), along with Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Heman, and Ethan; many are untitled. They were gathered over time into five books, likely completed after the exile.
Where: Many are tied to worship at Jerusalem and the temple; some to moments in David's life, such as the wilderness of Judah (Psalm 63); and one to the rivers of Babylon (Psalm 137).
The world at the time: The psalms were Israel's hymnbook and prayer book. David organized Levite singers and musicians for worship (1 Chronicles 25), and the psalms were sung at the temple and on pilgrimages to Jerusalem.

**The Righteous Find Refuge in God (Psalms 1:1–10:18)** — The opening psalms contrast righteousness with wickedness, introduce God's chosen king, and express confidence that the Lord hears those who seek him.

The book of Psalms is Israel's songbook and prayer book. Its 150 songs were written over many centuries by David, Asaph, the sons of Korah, Moses, Solomon, and others. They are divided into five books, and each book ends with a short burst of praise. The psalms cover every human feeling: joy, fear, anger, guilt, grief, and hope.

Psalm 1 opens the whole collection by showing two paths. “Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor standeth in the way of sinners, nor sitteth in the seat of the scornful. But his delight is in the law of the LORD.” That person is “like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season.” But the ungodly “are like the chaff which the wind driveth away.”

Psalm 2 shows the nations plotting against God and his anointed king. God laughs at them and says to his king, “Thou art my Son; this day have I begotten thee.” The New Testament applies these words to Jesus. Psalm 3 is David's prayer when he fled from his son Absalom: “But thou, O LORD, art a shield for me.” In Psalm 4 he says, “I will both lay me down in peace, and sleep: for thou, LORD, only makest me dwell in safety.”

Psalm 8 looks up at the night sky in wonder: “O LORD our Lord, how excellent is thy name in all the earth!” “When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; What is man, that thou art mindful of him?” Yet God has made humans “a little lower than the angels” and crowned them with glory. Psalms 9 and 10 praise God as “a refuge for the oppressed,” but also ask the hard question, “Why standest thou afar off, O LORD?”

**David Seeks Protection, Justice, and Guidance (Psalms 11:1–20:9)** — David's prayers move between danger and confidence, emphasizing God's righteousness, reliable word, and care for those who trust him.

These psalms, most of them David's, swing between danger and trust. Psalm 11 asks, “If the foundations be destroyed, what can the righteous do?” The answer: “The LORD is in his holy temple.” In Psalm 13, David cries, “How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever?” But by the end he says, “I will sing unto the LORD, because he hath dealt bountifully with me.”

“The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God,” begins Psalm 14. Psalm 15 asks who may live with God, and answers: the one who “walketh uprightly” and “speaketh the truth in his heart.” In Psalm 16, David says God will not let his Holy One see corruption, words Peter later used about Jesus' resurrection. “In thy presence is fulness of joy; at thy right hand there are pleasures for evermore.”

Psalm 18 is David's victory song after God rescued him from Saul: “The LORD is my rock, and my fortress, and my deliverer.” Psalm 19 says God speaks through both creation and his word: “The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork.” “The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul.” It ends with a prayer many still pray: “Let the words of my mouth, and the meditation of my heart, be acceptable in thy sight, O LORD, my strength, and my redeemer.” Psalm 20, a prayer for the king before battle, says, “Some trust in chariots, and some in horses: but we will remember the name of the LORD our God.”

**Suffering Gives Way to Praise and Trust (Psalms 21:1–30:12)** — These psalms celebrate God's protection of his king, express deep suffering, and present the Lord as a shepherd who faithfully guides his people.

Psalm 22 begins with a cry of total abandonment: “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” Jesus spoke these exact words from the cross. The psalm describes a man surrounded and mocked, whose enemies say, “He trusted on the LORD that he would deliver him.” “They pierced my hands and my feet.” “They part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture.” Each of these details came true at Jesus' crucifixion. But the psalm ends in triumph, with all the nations turning to the LORD.

Right after it comes the best-known psalm of all. “The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. He maketh me to lie down in green pastures: he leadeth me beside the still waters. He restoreth my soul: he leadeth me in the paths of righteousness for his name's sake. Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me. Thou preparest a table before me in the presence of mine enemies: thou anointest my head with oil; my cup runneth over. Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life: and I will dwell in the house of the LORD for ever.” David had been a shepherd himself.

Psalm 24 celebrates God as King: “The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof.” It asks, “Who is this King of glory? The LORD of hosts, he is the King of glory.” Psalm 27 declares, “The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?” and ends, “Wait on the LORD: be of good courage, and he shall strengthen thine heart.” Psalm 29 describes “the voice of the LORD” in a thunderstorm. Psalm 30 thanks God for healing: “weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning.”

**Confession, Deliverance, and God's Faithful Care (Psalms 31:1–41:13)** — David seeks refuge, celebrates forgiveness, and reflects on trust amid illness, betrayal, and opposition.

In Psalm 31, David prays, “Into thine hand I commit my spirit,” words Jesus prayed as he died. Psalm 32 tells what it is like to hide sin and then confess it: “When I kept silence, my bones waxed old through my roaring all the day long.” But when he confessed, God forgave him. “Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered.”

Psalm 34 was written after David pretended to be insane in front of the Philistine king. It invites, “O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him.” “The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart.” Psalm 37 is wisdom for when the wicked seem to win: “Fret not thyself because of evildoers.” “Delight thyself also in the LORD; and he shall give thee the desires of thine heart. Commit thy way unto the LORD; trust also in him.” “But the meek shall inherit the earth,” a line Jesus repeated in the Sermon on the Mount. An old David adds, “I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken.”

Psalm 39 asks, “LORD, make me to know mine end, and the measure of my days.” Psalm 40 begins, “I waited patiently for the LORD; and he inclined unto me, and heard my cry. He brought me up also out of an horrible pit, out of the miry clay, and set my feet upon a rock.” In Psalm 41, David grieves that “mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me.” Jesus quoted this about Judas. Book One ends, “Blessed be the LORD God of Israel from everlasting, and to everlasting. Amen, and Amen.”

**Longing for God During Trouble and Exile (Psalms 42:1–49:20)** — Worshipers long for God's presence while celebrating His protection, kingship, and power over nations.

Book Two begins with a group of psalms by the sons of Korah, a family of temple singers. In Psalm 42, the writer is far from the temple, longing for God: “As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God.” A hart is a deer. He talks to himself: “Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God.” Psalm 45 is a song for a royal wedding. It even calls the king God: “Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever.” Hebrews 1 applies this to Jesus.

Psalm 46 is a song of confidence in the middle of disaster: “God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble. Therefore will not we fear, though the earth be removed, and though the mountains be carried into the midst of the sea.” Then God Himself speaks: “Be still, and know that I am God.” Martin Luther based his hymn A Mighty Fortress Is Our God on this psalm.

Psalm 47 calls, “O clap your hands, all ye people; shout unto God with the voice of triumph.” Psalm 48 praises Jerusalem: “Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised in the city of our God.” Psalm 49 warns that no one can buy his way out of death, no matter how rich he is.

**God Desires Sincere Worship and Repentance (Psalms 50:1–60:12)** — God calls His people to genuine obedience while David's prayers show confession, humility, and dependence in conflict.

In Psalm 50, God says He does not need animal sacrifices, “For every beast of the forest is mine, and the cattle upon a thousand hills.” What He wants is thankful, obedient hearts.

Psalm 51 is David's prayer after the prophet Nathan confronted him about Bathsheba. It is the Bible's great prayer of repentance. “Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness.” “Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin.” “Against thee, thee only, have I sinned.” He asks for more than forgiveness. He asks for a new heart: “Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me. Cast me not away from thy presence; and take not thy holy spirit from me. Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation.” He knows what God really wants: “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.”

Several psalms in this section have titles that connect them to David's life on the run from Saul, like Psalm 52 after Doeg betrayed him and Psalm 57 when he hid in a cave. In Psalm 55, betrayed by a close friend, he wishes, “Oh that I had wings like a dove! for then would I fly away, and be at rest.” He decides, “Cast thy burden upon the LORD, and he shall sustain thee.” Psalm 56 says, “What time I am afraid, I will trust in thee.”

**The Soul Finds Rest in God's Strength (Psalms 61:1–72:20)** — David's prayers emphasize trust and worship, while later songs celebrate God's justice and a righteous king.

David prays in Psalm 61, “lead me to the rock that is higher than I.” In Psalm 62 he waits quietly for God: “Truly my soul waiteth upon God: from him cometh my salvation. He only is my rock.” Psalm 63 was written in the desert of Judah: “O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee... in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is.” “Because thy lovingkindness is better than life, my lips shall praise thee.”

Psalm 67 asks God to bless Israel so that all nations will know Him: “God be merciful unto us, and bless us; and cause his face to shine upon us.” Psalm 68 praises God as “A father of the fatherless, and a judge of the widows.” In Psalm 69, a suffering man says, “in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink,” another detail that happened at the cross. In Psalm 71, an old man prays, “now also when I am old and grayheaded, O God, forsake me not.”

Book Two ends with Psalm 72, a prayer for Solomon. It describes the perfect king who defends the poor and rules justly: “He shall have dominion also from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth.” All nations will call him blessed. The book closes with the note, “The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended.”

**The Prosperity of the Wicked Challenges Faith (Psalms 73:1–83:18)** — Asaph and other worshipers wrestle with injustice, remember Israel's history, and call on God to defend His people.

Book Three begins with psalms by Asaph, one of David's chief musicians. In Psalm 73, Asaph admits that he almost lost his faith: “But as for me, my feet were almost gone... For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked.” They seem healthy, rich, and carefree, while he suffers. Then everything changes: “Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end.” Their success will not last. He ends, “Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever.”

Several of these psalms come from Israel's darkest days. Psalm 74 mourns the destruction of the temple by enemies. In Psalm 77, the writer asks, “Will the Lord cast off for ever?” Then he remembers how God led His people through the Red Sea, and he finds hope. Psalm 78 retells Israel's history so that “the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born.”

Psalm 80 pictures Israel as a vine God brought out of Egypt and planted, now torn down. Three times it prays, “Turn us again, O God, and cause thy face to shine; and we shall be saved.” Psalm 82 shows God judging unjust rulers. It says, “I have said, Ye are gods,” a verse Jesus quoted when people accused Him of claiming to be God (John 10:34).

**God's Dwelling Place and Covenant Faithfulness (Psalms 84:1–89:52)** — These psalms celebrate worship in God's presence, seek restoration, and struggle with the apparent failure of promises to David.

Psalm 84 is a pilgrim's song of longing for the temple: “How amiable are thy tabernacles, O LORD of hosts!” Even the birds have made nests near God's altar. “For a day in thy courts is better than a thousand. I had rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God, than to dwell in the tents of wickedness.” Psalm 85 prays for revival and gives a beautiful picture: “Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other.”

Psalm 87 imagines people from enemy nations, Egypt, Babylon, and Philistia, being counted as born in Zion. Psalm 88 is the darkest psalm in the Bible. It is a prayer of someone who has suffered since youth, and it ends with no hope at all: “and mine acquaintance into darkness.” Its place in the Bible shows that God welcomes even our most desperate prayers.

Book Three ends with Psalm 89. It begins, “I will sing of the mercies of the LORD for ever.” It recalls God's promise to David: “Once have I sworn by my holiness that I will not lie unto David. His seed shall endure for ever.” But then it mourns that David's kingdom has fallen and his crown is in the dust, and it cries, “Lord, where are thy former lovingkindnesses?” Even so, it ends with praise.

**The Eternal God Rules Over Human Generations (Psalms 90:1–100:5)** — The collection turns toward God's everlasting nature, protection, and authority as King over the earth.

Book Four opens with Psalm 90, “A Prayer of Moses the man of God,” likely the oldest psalm. “Lord, thou hast been our dwelling place in all generations.” God is eternal, but human life is short: “For a thousand years in thy sight are but as yesterday when it is past.” “The days of our years are threescore years and ten,” seventy years. So Moses prays, “So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.”

Psalm 91 is a song of protection: “He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty.” “Under his wings shalt thou trust.” “For he shall give his angels charge over thee, to keep thee in all thy ways.” The devil quoted this verse when he tempted Jesus. Psalm 92 is “A Psalm or Song for the sabbath day”: “It is a good thing to give thanks unto the LORD.”

Several psalms declare, “The LORD reigneth.” Psalm 95 calls, “O come, let us sing unto the LORD,” and then warns, “To day if ye will hear his voice, Harden not your heart,” a warning quoted in Hebrews. Psalm 96 says, “O sing unto the LORD a new song.” Psalm 100 is a short song of thanksgiving: “Make a joyful noise unto the LORD, all ye lands. Serve the LORD with gladness.” “Know ye that the LORD he is God: it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture. Enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise.”

**God's Mercy Endures Through Human Weakness (Psalms 101:1–107:43)** — Worshipers reflect on righteous leadership, suffering, forgiveness, creation, and God's repeated rescue in distress.

In Psalm 102, a suffering man feels his life fading like smoke, but he takes comfort that God never changes: “But thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end.”

Psalm 103 is a joyful song of David counting God's blessings: “Bless the LORD, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name.” God is the one “Who forgiveth all thine iniquities; who healeth all thy diseases.” His forgiveness is complete: “As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us. Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust.”

Psalm 104 is a hymn about creation. It describes God stretching out the heavens like a curtain, setting the earth on its foundations, and giving water, food, and homes to every creature, from wild donkeys to sea creatures. Psalms 105 and 106 retell Israel's history, first God's faithfulness and then the people's failures. Psalm 107 begins Book Five: “O give thanks unto the LORD, for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever. Let the redeemed of the LORD say so.” It tells of travelers lost in the desert, prisoners in chains, sick people near death, and sailors in a storm, “They that go down to the sea in ships.” Each cries to God, and each is rescued. After each rescue comes the refrain, “Oh that men would praise the LORD for his goodness.”

**Praise, Prayer, and Confidence in God's Victory (Psalms 108:1–118:29)** — David's prayers seek deliverance, while songs celebrate God's rule, care for the needy, and lasting mercy.

Psalm 110 is the psalm quoted most often in the New Testament. David writes, “The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool.” Jesus asked the Pharisees how David's son could also be David's Lord. The psalm also says, “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek,” which Hebrews applies to Christ. Psalm 111 teaches that “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom.”

Psalms 113 through 118 were sung at Passover. Jesus and His disciples likely sang them after the Last Supper (Matthew 26:30). Psalm 114 remembers the Exodus: “The sea saw it, and fled.” Psalm 115 mocks idols: “They have mouths, but they speak not: eyes have they, but they see not.” In Psalm 116, someone rescued from death says, “Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints.” Psalm 117 is the shortest chapter in the Bible, just two verses calling all nations to praise the LORD.

Psalm 118 is a song of victory. “The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner.” Jesus applied this verse to Himself. It continues, “This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it.” And the crowd's shout on Palm Sunday comes from this psalm: “Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the LORD.”

**God's Word Guides the Faithful Through Every Trial (Psalms 119:1–176)** — An extended meditation celebrates God's law and promises as wisdom, comfort, guidance, and hope.

Psalm 119 is the longest chapter in the Bible, with 176 verses. It is a love song for God's word. It is written as an acrostic: it has twenty-two sections, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and every one of the eight verses in each section begins with that letter. Almost every verse mentions God's word using a different term: law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, judgments, or word.

Some of its lines are among the best-known in Scripture. “Wherewithal shall a young man cleanse his way? by taking heed thereto according to thy word.” “Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee.” “Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path.” “The entrance of thy words giveth light.” The writer has known suffering, but he sees good in it: “It is good for me that I have been afflicted; that I might learn thy statutes.” His love is personal: “O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day.” The psalm ends with a humble prayer: “I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek thy servant; for I do not forget thy commandments.”

**The Pilgrims Look to God for Help (Psalms 120:1–134:3)** — Songs of Ascents celebrate trust, unity, worship, and God's protection as people look toward Jerusalem.

Psalms 120 through 134 each carry the title “A Song of degrees,” or ascents. Pilgrims likely sang them as they walked up to Jerusalem for the great feasts, since the city sits high in the hills. Psalm 121 is a traveler's prayer: “I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help. My help cometh from the LORD, which made heaven and earth.” God never stops watching: “he that keepeth Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep.” Psalm 122 celebrates arriving: “I was glad when they said unto me, Let us go into the house of the LORD.” “Pray for the peace of Jerusalem.”

Psalm 124 asks where Israel would be “If it had not been the LORD who was on our side.” Psalm 126 remembers the joy of coming home from exile: “They that sow in tears shall reap in joy.” Psalm 127, by Solomon, teaches, “Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it.” It also says, “children are an heritage of the LORD.”

Psalm 130 cries, “Out of the depths have I cried unto thee, O LORD,” and waits for God “more than they that watch for the morning.” Psalm 131 compares a quiet, trusting soul to a weaned child resting with its mother. Psalm 133 celebrates God's people together: “Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!”

**God's Greatness Is Remembered Through History (Psalms 135:1–139:24)** — Worshipers praise God's authority, remember mercy through history, and reflect on His complete knowledge of every person.

Psalm 136 retells the story of creation and of Israel's rescue from Egypt. After every line, the people answer with the same refrain: “for his mercy endureth for ever.” It repeats twenty-six times.

Psalm 137 comes from the Babylonian exile. “By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion. We hanged our harps upon the willows.” Their captors demand a happy song, but they ask, “How shall we sing the LORD'S song in a strange land?” “If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning.” The psalm ends with a shocking cry for revenge against Babylon for what it did to Jerusalem's children: “Happy shall he be, that taketh and dasheth thy little ones against the stones.” The psalms honestly show the raw pain of God's people, even when it is ugly.

Psalm 139 marvels that God knows everything about us. “O LORD, thou hast searched me, and known me.” No one can escape His presence: “Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there.” God formed the writer in his mother's womb: “I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made.” It ends with a prayer: “Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts.”

**David Seeks Deliverance and Teaches Dependence on God (Psalms 140:1–145:21)** — David seeks rescue and guidance before turning to praise for God's goodness and everlasting kingdom.

These are some of David's last psalms. He prays for protection from violent and lying people, and asks God to guard his own words: “Set a watch, O LORD, before my mouth; keep the door of my lips.” He prays, “Let my prayer be set forth before thee as incense.” Psalm 142 was written while he hid in a cave from Saul, feeling that “no man cared for my soul.” In Psalm 143 he asks, “Cause me to hear thy lovingkindness in the morning,” and “Teach me to do thy will.”

Psalm 145 is David's great song of praise, another acrostic. “Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised; and his greatness is unsearchable.” “One generation shall praise thy works to another.” “The LORD is gracious, and full of compassion; slow to anger, and of great mercy.” God cares for every creature: “The eyes of all wait upon thee; and thou givest them their meat in due season. Thou openest thine hand, and satisfiest the desire of every living thing.”

**All Creation Is Called to Praise the Lord (Psalms 146:1–150:6)** — The final songs celebrate God's justice, care, power, and greatness, calling every living creature to praise Him.

The book of Psalms ends with five songs of pure praise. Each one begins and ends with “Praise ye the LORD,” which in Hebrew is Hallelujah. Psalm 146 warns, “Put not your trust in princes,” and praises the God who feeds the hungry, frees prisoners, and opens the eyes of the blind. Psalm 147 says, “He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds. He telleth the number of the stars; he calleth them all by their names.”

In Psalm 148, everything in creation is called to praise God: angels, the sun, moon, and stars, sea creatures, fire, hail, snow, mountains, trees, animals, kings, young men and women, old people and children. Psalm 149 calls God's people to “sing unto the LORD a new song” and praise Him with dancing.

The last psalm turns everything into one great song. God should be praised with the trumpet, harp, tambourine, strings, and loud cymbals. Then comes the final line of the whole book: “Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD. Praise ye the LORD.”

# Proverbs
When it happened: Most of the proverbs come from King Solomon's reign (about 970–930 BC). Chapters 25–29 were copied out by "the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" (Proverbs 25:1) around 700 BC.
When it was written: The book was gathered in stages: Solomon's proverbs (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the words of the wise" (22:17; 24:23), the collection copied by Hezekiah's men (chapters 25–29), and the words of Agur (chapter 30) and King Lemuel (chapter 31). It reached its final form in or after Hezekiah's time.
Where: The royal court at Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Collections of wise sayings were common across the ancient Near East, especially in Egypt. 1 Kings 4:30–32 says Solomon's wisdom "excelled the wisdom of all the children of the east country, and all the wisdom of Egypt."

**Wisdom Begins with Reverence for God (Proverbs 1:1–2:22)** — Solomon introduces wisdom as righteous living that begins with reverence for the Lord. Wisdom warns against violence and folly, guards the upright, and leads away from destructive paths.

“The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel; To know wisdom and instruction.” A proverb is a short, wise saying that is easy to remember. The book says its purpose is to give good sense to the simple and understanding to the young. Then it states its main idea: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise wisdom and instruction.” To fear the LORD means to honor and trust Him with deep respect.

The first nine chapters are mostly a father's speeches to his son. “My son, hear the instruction of thy father, and forsake not the law of thy mother.” The father's first warning is about bad company: “My son, if sinners entice thee, consent thou not.” He describes a gang that plans to ambush and rob innocent people. But their plan backfires: “they lay wait for their own blood.”

Then Wisdom herself appears like a woman shouting in the busy streets and at the city gates: “How long, ye simple ones, will ye love simplicity?” Those who ignore her will one day call for her, and she will not answer. In chapter 2, the father tells his son to search for wisdom as hard as people dig for treasure: “If thou seekest her as silver, and searchest for her as for hid treasures; Then shalt thou understand the fear of the LORD.” “For the LORD giveth wisdom.” Wisdom will protect him from violent men and from the seductive woman who has abandoned her marriage.

**Trusting God Shapes a Wise Life (Proverbs 3:1–4:27)** — Trust in the Lord, accept correction, value wisdom above riches, help neighbors, and guard the heart. The righteous path grows brighter while the wicked stumble in darkness.

The father keeps teaching. Chapter 3 contains some of the most loved verses in the book: “Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths.” “Be not wise in thine own eyes.” “Honour the LORD with thy substance, and with the firstfruits of all thine increase.” Even God's correction is a sign of love: “My son, despise not the chastening of the LORD... For whom the LORD loveth he correcteth; even as a father the son in whom he delighteth.”

Wisdom is worth more than anything: “Happy is the man that findeth wisdom.” She is more valuable than silver, gold, or rubies. “Her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace.” The father also gives practical advice: “Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it.”

In chapter 4, he tells his son how his own father taught him. “Wisdom is the principal thing; therefore get wisdom.” He describes two roads. “But the path of the just is as the shining light, that shineth more and more unto the perfect day.” The road of the wicked is deep darkness. Then he gives one of the book's key commands: “Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life.” Watch your mouth, look straight ahead, and do not turn to the right or the left.

**Wisdom Warns Against Adultery and Destructive Desires (Proverbs 5:1–7:27)** — Solomon warns that adultery and uncontrolled desires destroy life, while faithfulness in marriage protects it. He also warns about rash pledges, laziness, and violent or dishonest behavior.

The father warns his son about adultery. The “strange woman,” a woman who is not his wife, speaks words sweeter than honey, “But her end is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a twoedged sword.” Instead, he should be faithful to his own wife: “Drink waters out of thine own cistern,” “and rejoice with the wife of thy youth.”

Chapter 6 gives other warnings. Do not guarantee another person's debts. Learn from tiny creatures: “Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise.” The ant has no boss, yet it stores food in summer. “Yet a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep: So shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth.” Then comes a list: “These six things doth the LORD hate: yea, seven are an abomination unto him: A proud look, a lying tongue, and hands that shed innocent blood, An heart that deviseth wicked imaginations, feet that be swift in running to mischief, A false witness that speaketh lies, and he that soweth discord among brethren.” About adultery, the father asks, “Can a man take fire in his bosom, and his clothes not be burned?”

Chapter 7 tells a story. From his window, the father sees a foolish young man walking near a certain woman's house at twilight. She comes out to meet him, dressed like a prostitute. She grabs him, kisses him, and says she has perfumed her bed and that her husband is away on a long trip. “He goeth after her straightway, as an ox goeth to the slaughter.” The father warns, “Her house is the way to hell, going down to the chambers of death.”

**Wisdom Calls Everyone to Choose Life (Proverbs 8:1–9:18)** — Wisdom and Folly are pictured as competing invitations. Wisdom offers truth, justice, understanding, and life; Folly offers secrecy and stolen pleasure but leads toward death.

Wisdom speaks again, calling out at the crossroads and the city gates to anyone who will listen. “For wisdom is better than rubies; and all the things that may be desired are not to be compared to it.” By wisdom kings rule and judges decide what is right. “I love them that love me; and those that seek me early shall find me.”

Then Wisdom describes how she was with God before the world began. “The LORD possessed me in the beginning of his way, before his works of old.” Before the mountains were formed, she was there. “When he prepared the heavens, I was there.” “Then I was by him, as one brought up with him: and I was daily his delight, rejoicing always before him.” Many Christians have seen in these verses a picture of Christ, through whom God made all things. Wisdom ends, “For whoso findeth me findeth life, and shall obtain favour of the LORD.”

Chapter 9 compares two women who invite people to dinner. Wisdom has built her house with seven pillars, prepared a feast, and sent out her servants to invite the simple to come and live. “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding.” Folly, on the other hand, is a loud, foolish woman who sits at her door calling to people passing by: “Stolen waters are sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant.” “But he knoweth not that the dead are there.”

**Everyday Choices Reveal Wisdom or Foolishness (Proverbs 10:1–12:28)** — Short sayings contrast righteous and wicked conduct in speech, work, honesty, generosity, correction, and care for others. They present general patterns rather than guarantees for every circumstance.

Chapter 10 begins a new section: “The proverbs of Solomon.” From here on, most proverbs are short two-line sayings. Usually the second line contrasts with the first, setting the wise beside the foolish, the righteous beside the wicked, and the hardworking beside the lazy. They do not tell a story. Each one is a small picture of life.

Many are about family and words: “A wise son maketh a glad father: but a foolish son is the heaviness of his mother.” “Hatred stirreth up strifes: but love covereth all sins.” “In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin: but he that refraineth his lips is wise.”

Others are about honesty, pride, and good advice: “A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight.” “When pride cometh, then cometh shame: but with the lowly is wisdom.” “Where no counsel is, the people fall: but in the multitude of counsellors there is safety.” Some are surprising or funny: “As a jewel of gold in a swine's snout, so is a fair woman which is without discretion.” Others praise kindness and good character: “He that winneth souls is wise.” “A virtuous woman is a crown to her husband.” “A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast,” meaning he takes care of his animals.

**Wise Words and Humble Hearts Preserve Relationships (Proverbs 13:1–15:33)** — These sayings praise discipline, diligence, gentle speech, patience, wise companions, and humility. God sees hearts and motives, and wisdom grows through counsel and reverence.

More of Solomon's sayings: “Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life.” Deferred means delayed. “The way of transgressors is hard.” “He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed.” Some teach about raising children: “He that spareth his rod hateth his son: but he that loveth him chasteneth him betimes,” or early.

Others warn about thinking we know best: “There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death.” Some speak about whole nations: “Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people.”

Chapter 15 gives practical wisdom for daily life. “A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger.” “The eyes of the LORD are in every place, beholding the evil and the good.” Happiness does not come from money: “Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith.” A stalled ox was a fattened calf, the finest meat. “A merry heart maketh a cheerful countenance.”

**God Values Justice, Humility, and Self-Control (Proverbs 16:1–18:24)** — Human plans remain subject to God. The sayings commend justice, humility, self-control, careful listening, faithful friendship, and trust in the Lord over wealth or pride.

These proverbs show that God is in charge even when people make their own plans: “A man's heart deviseth his way: but the LORD directeth his steps.” “The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD.” Then comes one of the most quoted proverbs of all: “Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall.”

Self-control is greater than strength: “He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city.” Kind words heal: “Pleasant words are as an honeycomb, sweet to the soul, and health to the bones.” “A merry heart doeth good like a medicine: but a broken spirit drieth the bones.”

Several speak of friendship: “A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity.” “There is a friend that sticketh closer than a brother.” Others are about words: “Even a fool, when he holdeth his peace, is counted wise.” “Death and life are in the power of the tongue.” And one about God's protection: “The name of the LORD is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe.” Also, “Whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing, and obtaineth favour of the LORD.”

**Integrity Matters More Than Wealth or Status (Proverbs 19:1–21:31)** — Integrity, compassion, responsibility, justice, and wise planning are valued above wealth and ambition. Preparation matters, but victory belongs to the Lord.

Kindness to the poor is like making a loan to God Himself: “He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD; and that which he hath given will he pay him again.” Drunkenness is foolish: “Wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging: and whosoever is deceived thereby is not wise.” Character shows early: “Even a child is known by his doings, whether his work be pure, and whether it be right.”

God rules even over kings: “The king's heart is in the hand of the LORD, as the rivers of water: he turneth it whithersoever he will.” And He cares more about how we live than about religious ceremonies: “To do justice and judgment is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice.”

Some proverbs are humorous but true: “It is better to dwell in a corner of the housetop, than with a brawling woman in a wide house.” Others are sobering: “Whoso stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also shall cry himself, but shall not be heard.” The section ends with a reminder that no matter how well people prepare, “The horse is prepared against the day of battle: but safety is of the LORD.”

**Wise Living Requires Discipline and Compassion (Proverbs 22:1–24:34)** — A good name, fairness, self-control, wise friendships, diligence, and care for the vulnerable are commended. The neglected field shows how ruin grows through laziness.

“A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches.” Rich and poor are equal before God: “The rich and poor meet together: the LORD is the maker of them all.” This section contains a famous verse about raising children: “Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it.” It also warns about debt: “the borrower is servant to the lender.”

Then comes a new collection called “the words of the wise,” about thirty sayings. Some are similar to an ancient Egyptian book of wisdom. They warn against stealing land by moving a neighbor's boundary stone, against greed, and against envying sinners. One passage paints a vivid picture of a drunkard: “Who hath woe? who hath sorrow? who hath contentions?... They that tarry long at the wine.” “Look not thou upon the wine when it is red... At the last it biteth like a serpent, and stingeth like an adder.”

Other sayings teach courage, “If thou faint in the day of adversity, thy strength is small,” and kindness even toward enemies: “Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth.” The section ends with a lesson from a lazy man's field. The writer walks past and sees it overgrown with thorns and weeds, with its stone wall broken down. “Yet a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep: So shall thy poverty come.”

**Hezekiah's Men Preserve More of Solomon's Wisdom (Proverbs 25:1–27:27)** — Hezekiah's men copy further proverbs about leadership, humility, conflict, friendship, self-control, careful speech, and responsible care for household resources.

“These are also proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out.” About 250 years after Solomon, King Hezekiah's scribes gathered more of his sayings. “It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter.” Words spoken at the right time are precious: “A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver.”

These proverbs use many vivid comparisons. Good news is refreshing: “As cold waters to a thirsty soul, so is good news from a far country.” Kindness can win over enemies: “If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat... For thou shalt heap coals of fire upon his head,” a verse Paul quoted in Romans. Fools are hard to deal with. Two proverbs side by side seem to disagree: “Answer not a fool according to his folly,” and “Answer a fool according to his folly.” Wisdom means knowing which one fits the moment. “As a dog returneth to his vomit, so a fool returneth to his folly.”

Chapter 27 warns about overconfidence: “Boast not thyself of to morrow; for thou knowest not what a day may bring forth.” It praises honest friends: “Faithful are the wounds of a friend.” “Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend.” And it urges hard work: “Be thou diligent to know the state of thy flocks.”

**Righteous Leadership Protects the Vulnerable (Proverbs 28:1–29:27)** — Righteous leadership, honest work, confession, correction, justice for the poor, and trust in the Lord are contrasted with corruption, greed, hardened hearts, and fear of people.

These proverbs focus on justice and leadership. “The wicked flee when no man pursueth: but the righteous are bold as a lion.” Hiding sin never works: “He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy.” Generosity is rewarded: “He that giveth unto the poor shall not lack.”

A nation's leaders shape its life: “When the righteous are in authority, the people rejoice: but when the wicked beareth rule, the people mourn.” “Where there is no vision, the people perish.” Here vision means a message from God. Rash speech is dangerous: “Seest thou a man that is hasty in his words? there is more hope of a fool than of him.” And worrying about what others think is a trap: “The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the LORD shall be safe.”

**Agur Reflects on Human Limits and Contentment (Proverbs 30:1–33)** — Agur humbly acknowledges human limits, affirms God's pure word, asks for neither poverty nor riches, and draws wisdom from nature while warning against pride and anger.

Chapter 30 contains “The words of Agur,” a wise man we know nothing else about. He begins humbly: “Surely I am more brutish than any man,” admitting that he does not have all the answers. He asks questions about God that only God can answer: “Who hath ascended up into heaven, or descended?... what is his name, and what is his son's name, if thou canst tell?” Then he says, “Every word of God is pure: he is a shield unto them that put their trust in him. Add thou not unto his words.”

Agur asks God for two things: “Remove far from me vanity and lies: give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: Lest I be full, and deny thee... or lest I be poor, and steal.”

The rest of the chapter uses numbered lists to point out wonders of the world. “There be three things which are too wonderful for me, yea, four which I know not: The way of an eagle in the air; the way of a serpent upon a rock; the way of a ship in the midst of the sea; and the way of a man with a maid.” He describes four small creatures that are very wise: ants, which store food; conies, or rock badgers, which make homes in the rocks; locusts, which march in order without a king; and spiders, which live even in kings' palaces.

**King Lemuel Learns Justice and Self-Control (Proverbs 31:1–9)** — Lemuel's mother warns him against destructive pleasures and urges him to use authority with self-control, righteous judgment, and defense of the poor and needy.

These are “The words of king Lemuel, the prophecy that his mother taught him.” We do not know who Lemuel was, but his mother gave him wise advice about being a good king. She tells him not to waste his strength on women. She warns, “It is not for kings, O Lemuel, it is not for kings to drink wine,” because drinking might make him forget the law and fail to judge fairly. Instead, a king should use his power to help those who cannot help themselves: “Open thy mouth for the dumb in the cause of all such as are appointed to destruction. Open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy.”

**The Virtuous Woman Is Honored for Her Wisdom (Proverbs 31:10–31)** — The final poem honors a capable woman known for diligence, generosity, wisdom, kindness, faithfulness, and reverence for the Lord.

The book ends with a poem about the ideal wife. Like some of the psalms, it is an acrostic, with each verse starting with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. “Who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies. The heart of her husband doth safely trust in her.”

She works hard and is wise in business. She buys wool and flax and makes clothing. She gets up before dawn to provide food for her family. “She considereth a field, and buyeth it,” and plants a vineyard with her own earnings. She makes and sells linen garments. Her family is not afraid of winter, because they all have warm clothes. And she is generous: “She stretcheth out her hand to the poor.”

“Strength and honour are her clothing; and she shall rejoice in time to come. She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and in her tongue is the law of kindness.” Her family praises her: “Her children arise up, and call her blessed; her husband also, and he praiseth her.” He says, “Many daughters have done virtuously, but thou excellest them all.” The book ends where it began, with the fear of the LORD: “Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised.”

# Ecclesiastes
When it happened: The book reflects on life rather than telling events. Its speaker, the Preacher, calls himself "the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:1), traditionally Solomon, about 950 BC.
When it was written: Many scholars date the book later than Solomon, in the Persian or Greek period, because of its style of Hebrew, and see the Preacher's royal voice as a literary role.
Where: Jerusalem.
The world at the time: The Preacher writes from the position of a king with every advantage—wisdom, wealth, great building projects, and every pleasure—and tests whether any of it gives lasting meaning.

**The Search for Meaning in a Changing World (Ecclesiastes 1:1–18)** — The Preacher examines life's cycles and declares all is vanity: human effort, memory, and even wisdom cannot answer every problem beneath the sun.

“The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem.” The Preacher is traditionally Solomon. He begins with a startling cry: “Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity.” The Hebrew word for vanity means vapor or breath, something that quickly disappears and cannot be held.

He asks, “What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?” Life seems to go around in circles. “One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever.” The sun rises and sets and hurries back to rise again. The wind blows south, then north, and around again. “All the rivers run into the sea; yet the sea is not full.” People are never satisfied: “the eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing.” And nothing is really new: “there is no new thing under the sun.”

The Preacher decides to study everything with all his wisdom. But he finds that “that which is crooked cannot be made straight.” Wisdom itself brings pain: “For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow.”

**Pleasure, Wealth, and Achievement Cannot Last (Ecclesiastes 2:1–26)** — Pleasure, wealth, accomplishments, and wisdom cannot overcome death or guarantee lasting satisfaction. Enjoyment of labor is received as a gift from God, not permanent security.

Next the Preacher tries pleasure. “I said of laughter, It is mad: and of mirth, What doeth it?” He tries wine, while keeping his mind guided by wisdom. He builds great projects: houses, vineyards, gardens, orchards, and pools to water the trees. He gathers servants, flocks, silver, gold, singers, and every luxury. “And whatsoever mine eyes desired I kept not from them.” He becomes greater than anyone before him in Jerusalem.

But when he looks at everything he has done, “behold, all was vanity and vexation of spirit, and there was no profit under the sun.” Vexation of spirit means chasing the wind. Wisdom is better than foolishness, “as far as light excelleth darkness.” Yet in the end, the wise man and the fool both die and are forgotten. “And how dieth the wise man? as the fool.” He even comes to hate all his hard work, because he must leave it to someone else, who may be a fool.

Yet the chapter ends with a hint of hope. “There is nothing better for a man, than that he should eat and drink, and that he should make his soul enjoy good in his labour. This also I saw, that it was from the hand of God.” Simple pleasures can be enjoyed as gifts from God.

**God Appoints a Time for Every Purpose (Ecclesiastes 3:1–22)** — Life has changing seasons under God's authority. People cannot discover all His work, so they are encouraged to do good and receive their labor as God's gift while revering Him.

Chapter 3 contains the Preacher's most famous poem. “To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: A time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted; A time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to break down, and a time to build up; A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance.” It goes on through fourteen pairs of opposites, ending, “A time to love, and a time to hate; a time of war, and a time of peace.”

God has a plan and a timing for everything, even if we cannot see it. “He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart.” The word translated “world” can also mean eternity. People long to understand the big picture, but they cannot fully grasp what God is doing from beginning to end. So the Preacher concludes again that it is good to enjoy life and work as God's gift.

He also sees injustice in the courts, where wickedness sits in the place of judgment. But he trusts that “God shall judge the righteous and the wicked.” Then he notices that people and animals both die: “all are of the dust, and all turn to dust again.” From his view “under the sun,” he cannot be sure what happens after death.

**Oppression and Competition Leave People Empty (Ecclesiastes 4:1–16)** — The Preacher sees oppression, envy, loneliness, and unstable public honor. He values modest peace and praises companionship as strength in a troubled world.

The Preacher looks at all the oppression in the world. He sees the tears of the oppressed, “and they had no comforter.” Their oppressors have power, but no one comforts the victims either. He sees that much hard work is driven by envy of one's neighbor. Then he gives some balance: “Better is an handful with quietness, than both the hands full with travail and vexation of spirit.”

He describes a man who works endlessly but has no son or brother to share his wealth with. Why work so hard? This leads him to praise friendship: “Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up.” Two can keep each other warm and defend each other. “And a threefold cord is not quickly broken.”

Finally he notes that popularity does not last: “Better is a poor and a wise child than an old and foolish king, who will no more be admonished.” Even a wise young ruler who rises from prison to the throne will be forgotten by the next generation.

**Approaching God with Reverence and Honesty (Ecclesiastes 5:1–20)** — Careless worship and unfulfilled vows are warned against. Wealth cannot satisfy or follow a person into death, yet the ability to enjoy honest work and possessions is God's gift.

The Preacher gives advice about worship. “Keep thy foot when thou goest to the house of God,” meaning be careful how you approach Him. It is better to listen than to offer the careless sacrifices of fools. “Be not rash with thy mouth... for God is in heaven, and thou upon earth: therefore let thy words be few.” Keep your promises to God: “When thou vowest a vow unto God, defer not to pay it.”

Then he turns to money. “He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver.” The more you have, the more people come to use it up. And wealth brings worry: “The sleep of a labouring man is sweet, whether he eat little or much: but the abundance of the rich will not suffer him to sleep.” Riches can be lost in a bad business deal. And no one takes anything with him at death: “As he came forth of his mother's womb, naked shall he return.”

So again the Preacher concludes that it is good to enjoy food, drink, and the rewards of work during the few days God gives. To have riches and the ability to enjoy them, “this is the gift of God.”

**Riches and Long Life Cannot Guarantee Satisfaction (Ecclesiastes 6:1–12)** — Wealth, honor, children, and long life cannot guarantee enjoyment. Human desires remain unsatisfied, and people cannot know every answer or what is truly good.

The Preacher describes a sad situation: a man whom God gives riches, wealth, and honor, so that he lacks nothing he wants, “yet God giveth him not power to eat thereof.” He cannot enjoy what he has, and a stranger enjoys it instead. Even if a man has a hundred children and lives many years but never finds satisfaction, the Preacher says a stillborn baby is better off than he is.

“All the labour of man is for his mouth, and yet the appetite is not filled.” People keep working and wanting, but they are never satisfied. It is better to be content with what is in front of you than to keep wandering after more desires. The chapter ends with questions no one on earth can answer: “For who knoweth what is good for man in this life... for who can tell a man what shall be after him under the sun?”

**Wisdom Faces Sorrow, Injustice, and Uncertainty (Ecclesiastes 7:1–29)** — Sorrow, correction, patience, and humility can teach wisdom, but righteous people still suffer and complete understanding remains beyond human reach.

This chapter contains surprising proverbs that turn ordinary thinking upside down. “A good name is better than precious ointment; and the day of death than the day of one's birth. It is better to go to the house of mourning, than to go to the house of feasting.” Funerals make people think seriously about life. “Sorrow is better than laughter.” A fool's laughter is “as the crackling of thorns under a pot,” loud but quickly gone.

He warns against living in the past: “Say not thou, What is the cause that the former days were better than these? for thou dost not enquire wisely concerning this.” In good times and bad, God is in control: “In the day of prosperity be joyful, but in the day of adversity consider: God also hath set the one over against the other.”

The Preacher warns against both pride in one's own goodness and reckless sin: “Be not righteous over much.” No one is perfect: “For there is not a just man upon earth, that doeth good, and sinneth not.” He searches for wisdom and finds it hard to find. He finds that a woman who sets traps is more bitter than death. He has found only one upright man among a thousand, and no upright woman among that many. In the end, he concludes that the problem is with all humanity: “God hath made man upright; but they have sought out many inventions,” many schemes of their own.

**Human Authority Remains Under God's Judgment (Ecclesiastes 8:1–17)** — Wisdom guides responses to rulers, but no authority controls death or guarantees immediate justice. The Preacher maintains reverence for God while admitting His work cannot be fully discovered.

Wisdom brings light: “a man's wisdom maketh his face to shine.” The Preacher advises obeying the king, because “Where the word of a king is, there is power.” But no one has power over the day of death.

He notices that wicked people often get away with evil, and that this encourages more evil: “Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil.” He sees wicked people buried with honor and righteous people treated as if they were wicked. Still, he believes God will make things right: “yet surely I know that it shall be well with them that fear God.”

So he recommends joy: “Then I commended mirth, because a man hath no better thing under the sun, than to eat, and to drink, and to be merry.” He has tried night and day to understand all God's work, but he admits that no one, not even the wisest, can fully figure it out.

**Death Comes to Everyone, but Life Still Matters (Ecclesiastes 9:1–18)** — Death comes to all and success is unpredictable, but those alive should enjoy God's gifts and work wisely. Wisdom can deliver even when it is later forgotten.

The same fate comes to everyone, good and bad, clean and unclean: they all die. Yet being alive is still better, “for a living dog is better than a dead lion.” “For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing.” So the Preacher urges, “Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with a merry heart.” “Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest all the days of the life of thy vanity.” “Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave.”

He notes that success is not guaranteed even to the most talented: “the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise... but time and chance happeneth to them all.” No one knows when hard times will come.

Then he tells a short story. A powerful king attacked a small city with few men. But there was a poor, wise man in the city who saved it with his wisdom. “Yet no man remembered that same poor man.” Wisdom is better than strength, but people often ignore the poor. “Wisdom is better than weapons of war: but one sinner destroyeth much good.”

**Foolishness Can Undo Great Wisdom (Ecclesiastes 10:1–20)** — Small foolishness damages honor. The Preacher warns about poor leadership, careless speech, laziness, and decisions that spread harm.

A little foolishness can ruin a lot of wisdom: “Dead flies cause the ointment of the apothecary to send forth a stinking savour: so doth a little folly him that is in reputation for wisdom and honour.” An apothecary was a perfume maker. The Preacher sees fools put in high positions, “servants upon horses, and princes walking as servants.”

He gives proverbs about the risks of everyday work. “He that diggeth a pit shall fall into it.” Whoever splits logs may get hurt. If an axe is dull, you have to work harder, so wisdom helps you succeed. Fools talk too much and wear themselves out. A nation suffers when its leaders are childish and spend their mornings feasting. Laziness lets a house fall apart. The chapter ends with a warning about careless words: “Curse not the king, no not in thy thought... for a bird of the air shall carry the voice.”

**Act Wisely Despite an Uncertain Future (Ecclesiastes 11:1–10)** — Uncertainty should not prevent generosity and diligent work. People should enjoy life responsibly, remembering that God brings actions into judgment.

Since no one knows the future, the Preacher urges bold generosity and hard work. “Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it after many days.” “Give a portion to seven, and also to eight; for thou knowest not what evil shall be upon the earth.” Do not wait for perfect conditions: “He that observeth the wind shall not sow.” Just as you cannot understand how a baby grows in the womb, you cannot understand all of God's work. “In the morning sow thy seed, and in the evening withhold not thine hand.”

“Truly the light is sweet, and a pleasant thing it is for the eyes to behold the sun.” People should enjoy every year of their lives, while remembering that dark days will come. He speaks to the young: “Rejoice, O young man, in thy youth; and let thy heart cheer thee.” Follow your heart's desires, “but know thou, that for all these things God will bring thee into judgment.”

**Remember the Creator Before Life Fades (Ecclesiastes 12:1–8)** — Poetic images of aging and death urge people to remember the Creator before strength fades, dust returns to earth, and the spirit returns to God.

“Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth, while the evil days come not, nor the years draw nigh, when thou shalt say, I have no pleasure in them.” Then the Preacher describes old age in a poem full of pictures. The body is like a house falling apart. “The keepers of the house shall tremble,” the hands shake. “The grinders cease because they are few,” the teeth are gone. “Those that look out of the windows be darkened,” the eyes grow dim. Sleepers wake at the sound of a bird, but can barely hear music. They are afraid of heights and of danger in the street. “The almond tree shall flourish,” a picture of white hair. Even a grasshopper feels heavy, and desire fades. Then “man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets.”

Life ends “Or ever the silver cord be loosed, or the golden bowl be broken, or the pitcher be broken at the fountain.” Then “shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.” The poem ends where the book began: “Vanity of vanities, saith the preacher; all is vanity.”

**The Final Duty of Humanity Is Revealed (Ecclesiastes 12:9–14)** — The conclusion calls humanity to fear God and keep His commandments, for God will judge every work. Life is brief and understanding limited, but God's gifts are to be received gratefully and responsibly.

In the epilogue, someone describes the Preacher's work. He was wise and taught the people knowledge. He carefully studied and arranged many proverbs, and “sought to find out acceptable words,” words that were both pleasing and true. “The words of the wise are as goads,” like the sharp sticks used to drive cattle, and they come “from one shepherd.” He adds a warning that students today still quote: “of making many books there is no end; and much study is a weariness of the flesh.”

Then comes the final answer to all the Preacher's questions: “Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man. For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil.” Life “under the sun” may seem empty, but it has meaning when it is lived for God, who will one day judge everything.

# Song of Solomon
When it happened: The poem does not describe historical events. It is associated with the era of Solomon (about 950 BC).
When it was written: The title "The song of songs, which is Solomon's" (Song of Solomon 1:1) can mean it was written by Solomon or dedicated to him. Its date is debated.
Where: Jerusalem and the countryside of Israel—vineyards and gardens, Lebanon, Sharon, En-gedi, Gilead, and Tirzah.
The world at the time: Love poetry was well known in the ancient world. Jewish readers have long read the Song at Passover as a picture of God's love for Israel, and many Christians have read it as a picture of Christ and the church, as well as a celebration of married love.

**A Young Woman Expresses Her Love (Song of Solomon 1:1–17)** — A young woman and the man she loves exchange poetic expressions of affection, beauty, fragrance, and the joy of being together.

“The song of songs, which is Solomon's.” The title means the greatest of all songs. It is a love poem between a man and a woman, told mostly through their own voices, with a group called the daughters of Jerusalem sometimes joining in. It celebrates romantic love and marriage as a good gift from God. Jewish teachers have also read it as a picture of God's love for Israel, and Christians as a picture of Christ's love for the church.

The woman speaks first: “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth: for thy love is better than wine.” She is self-conscious about her skin, darkened by working in the sun. “I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem.” Comely means lovely. Her brothers made her work in the vineyards, so she could not care for her own appearance. She asks her beloved where he takes his flocks to rest at noon.

He answers with praise, comparing her to a beautiful mare among Pharaoh's chariots. “Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair; thou hast doves' eyes.” She answers, “Behold, thou art fair, my beloved, yea, pleasant.” They imagine resting together in the countryside, where “The beams of our house are cedar.”

**Love Awakens Among the Flowers (Song of Solomon 2:1–17)** — The lovers celebrate affection through spring and vineyard imagery. The woman warns the daughters of Jerusalem not to awaken love before its proper time.

The woman says, “I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys.” He answers, “As the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters.” She compares him to an apple tree among the trees of the forest, and she delights to sit in his shade. He brings her to the banqueting house, “and his banner over me was love.” She is faint with love: “Stay me with flagons, comfort me with apples: for I am sick of love.” “His left hand is under my head, and his right hand doth embrace me.” Then she gives a warning that is repeated three times in the book: “I charge you, O ye daughters of Jerusalem... that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please.” Love should not be rushed.

She hears her beloved coming, “leaping upon the mountains, skipping upon the hills,” like a young deer. He stands outside and calls to her: “Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away. For, lo, the winter is past, the rain is over and gone; The flowers appear on the earth; the time of the singing of birds is come, and the voice of the turtle is heard in our land.” The turtle here is the turtledove. They must guard their love from anything that could harm it: “Take us the foxes, the little foxes, that spoil the vines.” She declares, “My beloved is mine, and I am his.”

**The Woman Searches for Her Beloved (Song of Solomon 3:1–11)** — The woman describes searching for her beloved at night before the poem turns to Solomon's magnificent wedding procession and royal carriage.

At night the woman dreams or imagines that she cannot find her beloved. “By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, but I found him not.” She gets up and searches the streets and squares of the city. She asks the night watchmen if they have seen him. Just after she passes them, she finds him. “I held him, and would not let him go,” until she brings him to her mother's house. Again she warns the daughters of Jerusalem not to awaken love before its time.

Then the scene changes to a royal wedding procession. “Who is this that cometh out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense?” It is King Solomon's carriage, surrounded by sixty of Israel's mightiest soldiers with swords. The carriage is made of cedar from Lebanon, with silver posts, a gold base, and purple cushions. The women of Zion are called to come out and see King Solomon wearing the crown his mother gave him “in the day of his espousals,” his wedding day, “and in the day of the gladness of his heart.”

**The Man Celebrates His Beloved's Beauty (Song of Solomon 4:1–16)** — The man praises the woman through poetic images of nature and precious things, calling her a treasured garden and sealed fountain; she welcomes him to enjoy its fruits.

The man describes his bride's beauty from head to toe using images from the countryside. Her eyes are like doves behind her veil. Her hair is like a flock of goats coming down a mountain. Her teeth are white and even, like freshly washed sheep. Her lips are “like a thread of scarlet.” Her neck is like the tower of David. Her breasts are like twin fawns grazing among the lilies. He says, “Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee.”

He invites her to come away with him from the mountains of Lebanon. “Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse.” Sister was a loving term for a bride in ancient poetry. Her love is sweeter than wine, and her perfume better than spices. He calls her a private garden: “A garden inclosed is my sister, my spouse; a spring shut up, a fountain sealed.” She has kept herself only for him. The garden is full of fruit and fragrant spices. She answers by inviting him in: “Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south; blow upon my garden... Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits.”

**Affection Gives Way to Longing and Separation (Song of Solomon 5:1–16)** — After shared delight, the woman searches for her absent beloved and describes him as outstanding among thousands, both her beloved and her friend.

The man answers, “I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse.” Their marriage is complete. Others encourage them: “eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved.”

Then the woman tells of another night. “I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh.” He calls her to open the door, his hair wet with the night dew. But she hesitates and makes excuses: she has already taken off her robe and washed her feet. When she finally gets up to open the door, “my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone.” She searches but cannot find him. The city watchmen find her instead, beat her, and take away her veil. She begs the daughters of Jerusalem, “if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him, that I am sick of love.”

They ask, “What is thy beloved more than another beloved?” She answers with a beautiful description: “My beloved is white and ruddy, the chiefest among ten thousand.” His head is like fine gold, his hair black as a raven, his eyes like doves, his arms like gold, his legs like marble pillars. “His mouth is most sweet: yea, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend.”

**The Lovers Renew Their Expressions of Devotion (Song of Solomon 6:1–13)** — The woman declares mutual belonging, while the man praises her as uniquely beautiful and admired, using images of cities, gardens, morning, moon, and sun.

The daughters of Jerusalem offer to help her look for him. But she knows where he is: “My beloved is gone down into his garden,” and she says again, “I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine.”

He praises her: “Thou art beautiful, O my love, as Tirzah, comely as Jerusalem, terrible as an army with banners.” Tirzah was a beautiful city. Her eyes overwhelm him: “Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me.” Even among queens and concubines, she is the only one for him: “My dove, my undefiled is but one.” Even the queens praise her: “Who is she that looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners?” The chapter ends with a call, “Return, return, O Shulamite,” using a name for her that may come from her hometown.

**Their Love Is Celebrated with Joy and Desire (Song of Solomon 7:1–13)** — The man praises the woman's beauty, and she responds with confident affection, inviting him into fields and vineyards where blooming plants continue the poem's celebration of love.

As she dances, the man describes her beauty again, this time from her feet upward. “How beautiful are thy feet with shoes, O prince's daughter!” He compares parts of her body to jewels, a wheat heap surrounded by lilies, fawns, an ivory tower, pools of water, and Mount Carmel. “How fair and how pleasant art thou, O love, for delights!” Her figure is like a palm tree, and he says he will climb it and take hold of its branches.

She responds with joy and confidence in his love: “I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me.” She invites him away into the countryside: “Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field; let us lodge in the villages.” They will go out early to see if the vines have budded and the pomegranates have blossomed, “there will I give thee my loves.” She has saved up all kinds of pleasant fruits for him.

**Love Is Strong and Cannot Be Bought (Song of Solomon 8:1–14)** — The final exchanges celebrate love's proper timing, enduring strength, and value beyond wealth. Love is strong as death and cannot be extinguished or purchased; the lovers call to one another at the close.

The woman wishes she could show her love openly, like kissing a brother in public, without anyone looking down on her. She warns the daughters of Jerusalem one last time not to awaken love before its time. Then someone asks, “Who is this that cometh up from the wilderness, leaning upon her beloved?”

Then comes the book's great statement about love: “Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave: the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame. Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it: if a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned.” A seal was a mark of ownership. True love cannot be destroyed, and it cannot be bought.

Her brothers remember when she was a little sister, too young for marriage, and wonder how to protect her. She says she was like a wall, keeping herself pure, “then was I in his eyes as one that found favour.” She mentions Solomon's vineyard, which he rented out to others, and says her own vineyard, herself, is hers to give. The song ends with the lovers calling to each other: “Make haste, my beloved, and be thou like to a roe or to a young hart upon the mountains of spices.”
