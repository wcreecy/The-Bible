# Hosea
When it happened: In the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and of Jeroboam II, king of Israel (Hosea 1:1)—roughly 755–715 BC.
When it was written: Hosea the son of Beeri, a prophet to the northern kingdom of Israel.
Where: The northern kingdom of Israel, often called Ephraim, and its capital Samaria.
The world at the time: Prosperity under Jeroboam II gave way to chaos as several kings were assassinated in quick succession. Israel turned for help to Egypt and Assyria, and in 722 BC Assyria destroyed Samaria.

**Hosea 1-3** — The LORD told Hosea to take "a wife of whoredoms," because the land had departed from the LORD like an unfaithful wife. Hosea married Gomer, and their children were given names that carried God's message: Jezreel, for coming judgment; Lo-ruhamah, because God would no longer have mercy on Israel; and Lo-ammi, "for ye are not my people." Yet God promised a day when he would say to them, "Thou art my people." Then the LORD told Hosea to go again and love an adulterous woman, as the LORD loved Israel even though they turned to other gods, and Hosea bought her for fifteen pieces of silver and a homer and a half of barley.

**Hosea 4-13** — Hosea brought the LORD's charge: "there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land." "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge." Priests and people worshipped idols and the calf of Samaria, set up kings without God, and ran to Egypt and Assyria "like a silly dove without heart." "For they have sown the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind." God desired "mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings." Yet he remembered his love: "When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt... How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?"

**Hosea 14** — The book ends with an invitation: "O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God." God promises, "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely," and Israel will grow like the lily and spread its roots like the cedars of Lebanon.

# Joel
When it happened: Joel gives no date. Suggestions range from the 800s BC to after the exile; the book assumes the temple and its priests are functioning and mentions trade with the Greeks (Joel 3:6).
When it was written: Joel the son of Pethuel (Joel 1:1); nothing more is known about him.
Where: Judah and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: A devastating plague of locusts and a drought had ruined the land's crops, so that even the offerings at the temple had stopped. Joel saw in this disaster a warning of the coming day of the LORD.

**Joel 1** — "The word of the LORD that came to Joel the son of Pethuel" describes a disaster without equal: swarm after swarm of insects—the palmerworm, the locust, the cankerworm, and the caterpiller—had stripped the vines and fig trees bare, and drought had withered the fields, so that the grain and drink offerings were cut off from the house of the LORD. Joel called the priests to put on sackcloth and the people to gather for a solemn fast.

**Joel 2** — He warned of "the day of the LORD," great and very terrible, pictured as an unstoppable army. "Therefore also now, saith the LORD, turn ye even to me with all your heart, and with fasting, and with weeping, and with mourning: And rend your heart, and not your garments." God, who is "gracious and merciful," promised to drive the invaders away: "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten." Afterward, "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy," and "whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered."

**Joel 3** — God will gather the nations into the valley of Jehoshaphat for judgment, because they scattered his people and divided his land—"Multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision." The LORD will be the hope of his people, a fountain will flow from his house, and Judah will dwell for ever.

# Amos
When it happened: In the days of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam II king of Israel, "two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1)—about 760 BC.
When it was written: Amos, a herdman from Tekoa in Judah, who said he was neither a prophet nor a prophet's son (Amos 1:1; 7:14).
Where: Amos came from Tekoa, south of Bethlehem, but preached in the northern kingdom, at the royal shrine of Bethel and in Samaria.
The world at the time: Under Jeroboam II, Israel was prosperous and secure. The wealthy built winter houses and summer houses decorated with ivory (Amos 3:15), while the poor were cheated and oppressed.

**Amos 1-2** — Amos, a herdman from Tekoa, was called to prophesy against the northern kingdom of Israel two years before the earthquake. "The LORD will roar from Zion." He began by announcing judgment on Israel's neighbors—Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and then Judah: "For three transgressions... and for four." Then he turned on Israel itself, because "they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes."

**Amos 3-6** — "You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities." Amos condemned the wealthy women of Samaria, the "kine of Bashan" who oppressed the poor; those who lay on beds of ivory; and the empty worship at Bethel and Gilgal. God had sent famine, drought, and plague, "yet have ye not returned unto me." "Prepare to meet thy God, O Israel." God hated their feasts: "But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream."

**Amos 7-9** — Amos saw visions: locusts and fire, which God held back when Amos pleaded for Israel, then a plumbline, a basket of summer fruit, and the Lord standing beside the altar. Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, told Amos to go home to Judah, and Amos replied, "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the LORD took me." He warned of a coming famine "of hearing the words of the LORD." Yet the book ends with a promise: "In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen," and Israel will be planted in its land, never again to be pulled up.

# Obadiah
When it happened: Most likely soon after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC, when Edom joined in the plunder (Obadiah 1:11–14); some date it earlier.
When it was written: Obadiah, about whom nothing else is known.
Where: The message concerns Edom, the rugged mountain country south and east of the Dead Sea, around Mount Seir.
The world at the time: The Edomites were descended from Esau, Jacob's brother, and had a long history of hostility toward Israel. When Jerusalem fell, Edom rejoiced and took part in the looting (compare Psalm 137:7).

**Obadiah 1:1-9** — "The vision of Obadiah" is the shortest book of the Old Testament—a single message against Edom, the nation descended from Jacob's brother Esau. Edom lived high among the rocks and thought itself safe, saying, "Who shall bring me down to the ground?" The LORD answered, "Though thou exalt thyself as the eagle... thence will I bring thee down."

**Obadiah 1:10-16** — Edom's sin was violence against its brother Jacob. When foreigners carried away Jerusalem's wealth and cast lots for the city, Edom stood by, rejoiced, joined in the looting, and cut off those who tried to escape. "As thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee."

**Obadiah 1:17-21** — "But upon mount Zion shall be deliverance." The house of Jacob will possess its land again, deliverers will come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau, "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's."

# Jonah
When it happened: Jonah the son of Amittai prophesied during the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel (2 Kings 14:25), roughly 790–750 BC.
When it was written: The book tells Jonah's story in the third person; the author is unnamed and the date of writing is debated.
Where: Joppa on the coast of Israel, the Mediterranean Sea, and Nineveh, the great city of Assyria on the Tigris River.
The world at the time: Assyria was Israel's most feared enemy, known for its cruelty in war. Within a few decades it would destroy the northern kingdom.

**Jonah 1** — The word of the LORD came to Jonah the son of Amittai: "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it." Instead Jonah went down to Joppa and took a ship to Tarshish, fleeing "from the presence of the LORD." The LORD sent a great wind, and the sailors cried out each to his own god while Jonah slept below. When the lot fell on Jonah, he told them to throw him into the sea. They rowed hard to reach land but could not, and finally cast him overboard, and the sea grew calm. "Now the LORD had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah," and he was in its belly three days and three nights.

**Jonah 2** — From the belly of the fish Jonah prayed, remembering how the waters closed around him and weeds wrapped about his head, and ended, "Salvation is of the LORD." The LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited Jonah onto dry land.

**Jonah 3** — A second time God told Jonah to go to Nineveh, and this time he went. He walked a day's journey into the city crying, "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown." The people of Nineveh believed God, proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest to the least. The king himself sat in ashes and ordered everyone to turn from violence. When God saw that they turned from their evil way, he did not bring the destruction he had threatened.

**Jonah 4** — Jonah was very angry. He said this was why he had fled, because he knew God was "a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness," and he asked to die. He sat east of the city to see what would happen. God made a gourd grow to shade him, then sent a worm to destroy it and a scorching east wind, and Jonah again wished to die. God told him that he had pity on a plant he had not made grow. "And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?" The book ends with God's question.

# Micah
When it happened: In the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah (Micah 1:1)—roughly 740–700 BC. Jeremiah 26:18 recalls Micah preaching in Hezekiah's reign.
When it was written: Micah the Morasthite, from the town of Moresheth.
Where: Moresheth in the lowlands of Judah; his messages concern both Samaria and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Micah lived at the same time as Isaiah, under the shadow of Assyria, which destroyed Samaria in 722 BC. In Judah, the powerful were seizing the fields and houses of the poor (Micah 2:2).

**Micah 1-3** — "The word of the LORD that came to Micah the Morasthite" concerned both Samaria and Jerusalem. The LORD would come down and make Samaria a heap. Micah condemned those who lay awake planning evil and then seized fields and houses, rulers "Who hate the good, and love the evil," prophets who preached peace for pay, and priests and judges who took bribes. "Therefore shall Zion for your sake be plowed as a field, and Jerusalem shall become heaps."

**Micah 4-5** — Yet "in the last days" the mountain of the house of the LORD would be lifted up, nations would come to learn his ways, "and they shall beat their swords into plowshares." "But thou, Beth-lehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting." He would stand and feed his flock in the strength of the LORD.

**Micah 6-7** — The LORD brought a case against his people, recalling how he had redeemed them from Egypt. He did not want thousands of rams: "what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" Micah mourned that the good man had perished from the earth, yet said, "I will look unto the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation." The book closes, "Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity... he delighteth in mercy... thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea."

# Nahum
When it happened: After the fall of the Egyptian city of Thebes, called No (Nahum 3:8), in 663 BC, and before the fall of Nineveh in 612 BC.
When it was written: Nahum the Elkoshite (Nahum 1:1); his hometown of Elkosh has not been identified with certainty.
Where: The message concerns Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire.
The world at the time: Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and dominated Judah for a century. In 612 BC Nineveh fell to the Babylonians and the Medes.

**Nahum 1** — "The burden of Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite." Nahum declared that the LORD is jealous and takes vengeance on his enemies; he "is slow to anger, and great in power, and will not at all acquit the wicked." The mountains quake before him. Yet "The LORD is good, a strong hold in the day of trouble." Judah's oppressor would be cut off: "Behold upon the mountains the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace!"

**Nahum 2-3** — Nahum described Nineveh's fall in vivid scenes: chariots raging in the streets, the gates of the rivers opened, the palace dissolved, and the city's treasures plundered. "Woe to the bloody city! it is all full of lies and robbery." Just as the great Egyptian city of No had fallen, so would Nineveh. The book ends by saying that everyone who hears of Assyria's end will clap their hands, "for upon whom hath not thy wickedness passed continually?"

# Habakkuk
When it happened: Likely just before Babylon invaded Judah, around 610–600 BC, since God says he is raising up the Chaldeans (Habakkuk 1:6).
When it was written: Habakkuk the prophet (Habakkuk 1:1). Chapter 3 is a prayer with musical directions, as in the Psalms.
Where: Judah.
The world at the time: After the reforms of King Josiah, Judah slid back into violence and injustice. Meanwhile the Chaldeans, the Babylonians, were rising to replace Assyria as the great power of the region.

**Habakkuk 1** — Habakkuk began with a complaint: "O LORD, how long shall I cry, and thou wilt not hear!" Violence and injustice filled Judah, and the law was ignored. God answered that he was raising up the Chaldeans, "that bitter and hasty nation," to bring judgment. That troubled Habakkuk even more: how could the holy God, who is "of purer eyes than to behold evil," use a nation more wicked than Judah?

**Habakkuk 2** — Habakkuk took his stand on the watchtower to see what God would say. God told him to write the vision plainly, for it would surely come: "though it tarry, wait for it." "Behold, his soul which is lifted up is not upright in him: but the just shall live by his faith." God pronounced five woes on the proud oppressor and promised that "the earth shall be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea." "But the LORD is in his holy temple: let all the earth keep silence before him."

**Habakkuk 3** — The book ends with Habakkuk's prayer recalling God's mighty acts, and his song of trust: "Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines... Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation. The LORD God is my strength."

# Zephaniah
When it happened: In the days of Josiah king of Judah (Zephaniah 1:1), who reigned 640–609 BC—likely before Josiah's reforms began in earnest.
When it was written: Zephaniah the son of Cushi, whose family line is traced back four generations to Hizkiah, possibly King Hezekiah (Zephaniah 1:1).
Where: Jerusalem and Judah.
The world at the time: After the long reign of Manasseh, idolatry was widespread in Judah—the worship of Baal, the host of heaven, and Malcham (Zephaniah 1:4–5). Assyria's power was beginning to fade.

**Zephaniah 1** — The word of the LORD came to Zephaniah in the days of King Josiah. He announced that the LORD would sweep away everything from the land and stretch out his hand against Judah and Jerusalem—against those who worshipped Baal and the host of heaven, and those who said in their hearts, "The LORD will not do good, neither will he do evil." "The great day of the LORD is near... That day is a day of wrath."

**Zephaniah 2** — "Seek ye the LORD, all ye meek of the earth... it may be ye shall be hid in the day of the LORD's anger." Judgment would also fall on the Philistines, Moab, Ammon, Ethiopia, and Assyria, whose capital Nineveh would become a desolation.

**Zephaniah 3** — Jerusalem itself, the oppressing city, had not trusted in the LORD; its princes, judges, prophets, and priests were corrupt. But God promised to leave "an afflicted and poor people" who would trust in his name, and to gather his scattered people home. The book ends in joy: "Sing, O daughter of Zion... The LORD thy God in the midst of thee is mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing."

# Haggai
When it happened: In the second year of King Darius of Persia (520 BC), between the sixth and ninth months (Haggai 1:1; 2:10).
When it was written: Haggai the prophet. His four messages are each dated to the day.
Where: Jerusalem.
The world at the time: The first exiles had returned from Babylon eighteen years earlier, but opposition had stopped the rebuilding of the temple (Ezra 4–5). Zerubbabel was governor of Judah, and Joshua was high priest.

**Haggai 1** — In the second year of King Darius, the word of the LORD came by the prophet Haggai to the governor Zerubbabel and the high priest Joshua. The people were saying, "The time is not come... that the LORD's house should be built," while they lived in fine houses of their own. "Consider your ways," the LORD said; they had sown much and brought in little, because of "mine house that is waste." Zerubbabel, Joshua, and all the people obeyed, and the LORD said, "I am with you." Within the month they began work on the temple.

**Haggai 2** — To those who remembered the first temple and thought this one was nothing in comparison, God said, "be strong... and work: for I am with you," and promised, "The glory of this latter house shall be greater than of the former... and in this place will I give peace." He promised to bless them from the day the foundation was laid, and told Zerubbabel that he would make him "as a signet: for I have chosen thee."

# Zechariah
When it happened: Zechariah began prophesying in the eighth month of the second year of Darius (520 BC; Zechariah 1:1), and his dated messages continue into Darius's fourth year (518 BC; Zechariah 7:1). Chapters 9–14 are undated.
When it was written: Zechariah the son of Berechiah, the son of Iddo, from a priestly family (Nehemiah 12:16). Many scholars attribute chapters 9–14 to a later writer.
Where: Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Like Haggai, Zechariah encouraged the returned exiles to finish rebuilding the temple under Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest, in the Persian province of Judah.

**Zechariah 1-6** — Zechariah, who prophesied alongside Haggai, called the returned exiles: "Turn ye unto me, saith the LORD of hosts, and I will turn unto you." In a single night he saw a series of visions: horsemen among myrtle trees reporting that the earth was at rest; four horns scattered by four carpenters; a man measuring Jerusalem, which would overflow its walls; Joshua the high priest in filthy garments, accused by Satan and given clean clothing; a golden candlestick between two olive trees, with the word to Zerubbabel, "Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts"; a flying scroll; a woman in a measuring basket carried away to Shinar; and four chariots. Joshua was crowned as a sign of "the man whose name is The BRANCH," who would build the temple of the LORD.

**Zechariah 7-8** — When people asked whether to keep fasting in memory of the temple's destruction, God told them what he really wanted: "Execute true judgment, and shew mercy and compassions every man to his brother: And oppress not the widow, nor the fatherless, the stranger, nor the poor." He promised that old men and women would again sit in Jerusalem's streets and children would play there, and that many peoples would come to seek the LORD in Jerusalem.

**Zechariah 9-14** — The final chapters look further ahead: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion... behold, thy King cometh unto thee: he is just, and having salvation; lowly, and riding upon an ass." A shepherd is valued at "thirty pieces of silver," which are thrown to the potter. "They shall look upon me whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn for him." A fountain is opened for sin and uncleanness. "Smite the shepherd, and the sheep shall be scattered." The nations gather against Jerusalem, the LORD's feet stand upon the mount of Olives, "And the LORD shall be king over all the earth."

# Malachi
When it happened: Malachi is undated, but the temple had been rebuilt and a governor ruled Judah (Malachi 1:8). The problems he addresses match those Nehemiah faced, so it is usually placed around 450–430 BC.
When it was written: Malachi, whose name means "my messenger"; nothing else is known about him.
Where: Jerusalem and Judah.
The world at the time: Judah was a small province of the Persian Empire. A century after the return, the priests had grown careless, tithes were being withheld, and marriages were breaking down.

**Malachi 1-2** — "The burden of the word of the LORD to Israel by Malachi" begins, "I have loved you, saith the LORD." The people answered, "Wherein hast thou loved us?"—and the book continues as a series of such exchanges. The priests despised God's name by offering blind, lame, and sick animals that they would never dare offer to their governor. The people dealt treacherously with one another and with the wives of their youth, for the LORD "hateth putting away." They wearied the LORD by saying that everyone who does evil is good in his sight.

**Malachi 3** — "Behold, I will send my messenger, and he shall prepare the way before me: and the Lord, whom ye seek, shall suddenly come to his temple," like a refiner's fire. "For I am the LORD, I change not." The people had robbed God in tithes and offerings: "Bring ye all the tithes into the storehouse... and prove me now herewith... if I will not open you the windows of heaven." Those who feared the LORD spoke often to one another, and a book of remembrance was written for them.

**Malachi 4** — The day was coming that would burn like an oven for the wicked, "But unto you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings." The book closes, "Remember ye the law of Moses my servant," with a promise: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD: And he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children."
