# Hosea
When it happened: In the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and of Jeroboam II, king of Israel (Hosea 1:1)—roughly 755–715 BC.
When it was written: Hosea prophesied to the northern kingdom of Israel in its last decades before Assyria conquered it in 722 BC. His messages were likely preserved and collected in Judah after the fall.
Where: The northern kingdom of Israel, often called Ephraim, and its capital Samaria.
The world at the time: Prosperity under Jeroboam II gave way to chaos as several kings were assassinated in quick succession. Israel turned for help to Egypt and Assyria, and in 722 BC Assyria destroyed Samaria.

**A Marriage Reflects Israel's Unfaithfulness (Hosea 1:1–2:23)** — God commands Hosea to marry Gomer as a living picture of Israel's betrayal through false worship. Their children receive names of warning, yet God promises that judgment will not end his love: he will restore his people, renew mercy, and again call them his own.

Hosea is a prophet in the northern kingdom of Israel in its last years before Assyria destroys it. God gives him a shocking command: “Go, take unto thee a wife of whoredoms and children of whoredoms: for the land hath committed great whoredom, departing from the LORD.” Hosea's marriage will become a living picture of how Israel has been unfaithful to God by worshiping other gods. So Hosea marries a woman named Gomer.

Gomer has three children, and God gives each one a name with a message. The first son is named Jezreel, because God will punish the family of King Jehu for the massacre Jehu committed at the city of Jezreel. The daughter is named Loruhamah, meaning not having obtained mercy, “for I will no more have mercy upon the house of Israel.” The second son is named Loammi, meaning not my people, “for ye are not my people, and I will not be your God.” But right away God adds a promise: one day, in the very place where they were told they are not his people, they will be called “the sons of the living God.”

In chapter 2, God speaks like a heartbroken husband. Israel ran after other lovers, the false god Baal, thinking Baal gave her food and wine. “For she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil.” So God will block her path and take away her harvests until she says, “I will go and return to my first husband.” Then God will win her back: “I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her.” he promises, “I will betroth thee unto me for ever.” and he reverses the children's names: “I will have mercy upon her that had not obtained mercy; and I will say to them which were not my people, Thou art my people; and they shall say, Thou art my God.”

**Hosea Demonstrates God's Continuing Love (Hosea 3:1–5)** — Hosea is commanded to love an unfaithful woman and purchases her back, illustrating God's continuing love for Israel. The nation will live for a time without king or its former religious life, then return to seek the Lord and David their king.

Gomer leaves Hosea for other men. Then God tells Hosea, “Go yet, love a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress, according to the love of the LORD toward the children of Israel.” So Hosea goes and pays a price to get her back: “So I bought her to me for fifteen pieces of silver, and for an homer of barley, and an half homer of barley.” He tells her that she must stay with him and be faithful, and he will be faithful to her.

This shows what will happen to Israel. “For the children of Israel shall abide many days without a king, and without a prince, and without a sacrifice.” They will live in exile for a long time. But afterward, “shall the children of Israel return, and seek the LORD their God, and David their king.” Just as Hosea bought back his unfaithful wife, God will buy back his unfaithful people.

**Israel Has Forgotten the Lord (Hosea 4:1–5:15)** — God brings charges of violence, dishonesty, adultery, corrupt priesthood, and idol worship against Israel. Ephraim and Judah cannot hide rebellion behind sacrifices or foreign alliances, and God warns that he will withdraw until they acknowledge their guilt and seek him.

God brings charges against Israel as if in a court of law. “There is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land.” Instead, there is cursing, lying, murder, stealing, and adultery. God especially blames the priests, who were supposed to teach his law: “My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge: because thou hast rejected knowledge, I will also reject thee.” The priests are as bad as the people, so “there shall be, like people, like priest.”

The people ask wooden idols for advice. They sacrifice on the hilltops and join in the sexual immorality of pagan worship. God says, “Ephraim is joined to idols: let him alone.” Ephraim is another name for the northern kingdom. When Israel and Judah see how sick they are, they run to Assyria for help instead of to God, but the king of Assyria cannot heal them. So God will be like a lion tearing its prey. Then he will go back to his place “till they acknowledge their offence, and seek my face.”

**God Desires Mercy Rather Than Empty Worship (Hosea 6:1–7:16)** — Israel speaks of returning, but its devotion fades quickly like morning dew. God desires mercy and knowledge of him rather than empty sacrifice, while the nation continues deceit, corrupt leadership, and trust in Egypt and Assyria instead of sincere repentance.

The people seem to repent. They say, “Come, and let us return unto the LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal us.” “After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up.” But their promises do not last. God says sadly, “O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee?... for your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away.” Then he tells them what he really wants: “For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings.” Jesus quoted this verse twice.

Chapter 7 shows a nation falling apart. The palace is full of plots, and leaders burn with anger like an oven. King after king is murdered, “there is none among them that calleth unto me.” Israel has mixed with other nations until “Ephraim is a cake not turned,” like a pancake burned on one side and raw on the other. Foreigners are draining its strength, “yea, gray hairs are here and there upon him, yet he knoweth not.” Israel is “like a silly dove without heart,” flying back and forth between Egypt and Assyria for help, instead of turning to God.

**Israel Reaps the Consequences of Rebellion (Hosea 8:1–10:15)** — Israel has broken God's covenant, made idols, and trusted in leaders, wealth, and military strength. Having sown the wind, it will reap the whirlwind through invasion, exile, failed prosperity, and the destruction of its false security.

An alarm sounds because Israel has broken God's covenant. “They have set up kings, but not by me.” They worship a golden calf at Samaria, but a craftsman made it, “therefore it is not God.” Then comes a famous warning: “For they have sown the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind.” What they planted will come back much bigger and more destructive.

Israel has forgotten its Maker and trusted in alliances and strong cities. So the people will be taken captive to Assyria. God remembers when he first found Israel: “I found Israel like grapes in the wilderness.” But they turned to Baal, and now “they shall be wanderers among the nations.” Israel is like “an empty vine.” The richer it became, the more altars it built for idols. Their idols will be carried off, and the people will be so terrified that they will say “to the mountains, Cover us; and to the hills, Fall on us.” Jesus repeated these words on the way to the cross.

Yet God still calls them to change: “Sow to yourselves in righteousness, reap in mercy; break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the LORD.” But they have done the opposite: “Ye have plowed wickedness, ye have reaped iniquity.”

**A Father's Love for a Rebellious Nation (Hosea 11:1–12:14)** — God remembers loving Israel as a child and bringing it from Egypt, even though the people turned to Baal. Judgment through Assyria is coming, but his compassion remains; Israel is called to return, practice mercy and justice, and wait on God.

This is one of the most tender chapters in the Bible. God speaks as a loving father: “When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt.” Matthew applies this verse to Jesus coming back from Egypt as a child. God taught Israel to walk, holding them by the arms. “I drew them with cords of a man, with bands of love.” But the more God called them, the more they ran to other gods.

So Assyria will rule over them. But then God's heart breaks: “How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel?... mine heart is turned within me.” he will not destroy them completely, “for I am God, and not man; the Holy One in the midst of thee.” One day his children will come home trembling, like birds flying back from Egypt and Assyria.

In chapter 12, Hosea reminds Israel of its ancestor Jacob. Jacob grabbed his brother's heel in the womb and later wrestled with God and wept, asking for his blessing. Israel is like Jacob in his deceit, but not in his seeking God. So God calls, “turn thou to thy God: keep mercy and judgment, and wait on thy God continually.”

**The Downfall of a Once-Prosperous Kingdom (Hosea 13:1–16)** — Ephraim's pride, prosperity, and idol worship have led it to forget the God who cared for Israel from Egypt onward. Earthly rulers cannot save the nation, and Samaria faces the severe consequences of rebellion, though God alone remains its true help.

Ephraim was once honored among the tribes, but it turned to Baal. Now “they sin more and more,” making idols of silver and saying, “Let the men that sacrifice kiss the calves.” So they will vanish like morning mist, like chaff blown away, and like smoke from a chimney.

God reminds them, “Yet I am the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt, and thou shalt know no god but me: for there is no saviour beside me.” he cared for them in the desert, but when they became full and comfortable, “their heart was exalted; therefore have they forgotten me.” Now he will meet them “as a bear that is bereaved of her whelps,” a mother bear whose cubs have been stolen. “O Israel, thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help.”

Yet even here there is a flash of hope: “I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction.” Paul echoes these words in his chapter on the resurrection. But the chapter ends with the terrible reality of the coming Assyrian conquest: “Samaria shall become desolate; for she hath rebelled against her God.”

**God Promises Healing and Restoration (Hosea 14:1–9)** — Hosea closes by calling Israel to return, reject trust in Assyria, military power, and idols, and seek forgiveness. God promises healing, free love, and renewed fruitfulness, while the final words distinguish those who walk in God's ways from those who stumble in rebellion.

Hosea's book ends with an invitation. “O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God; for thou hast fallen by thine iniquity. Take with you words, and turn to the LORD: say unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously.” He even gives them the words to pray, confessing that Assyria cannot save them and promising never again to call idols their gods.

God answers with grace: “I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him. I will be as the dew unto Israel: he shall grow as the lily.” Israel will become like a beautiful, fragrant olive tree. Ephraim will say, “What have I to do any more with idols?” The book ends with a word for every reader: “Who is wise, and he shall understand these things?... for the ways of the LORD are right, and the just shall walk in them: but the transgressors shall fall therein.”

# Joel
When it happened: Joel gives no date. Suggestions range from the 800s BC to after the exile; the book assumes the temple and its priests are functioning and mentions trade with the Greeks (Joel 3:6).
When it was written: Nothing more is known about Joel than his father's name (Joel 1:1). The book's descriptions of locusts and drought (Joel 1:4, 10–12) suggest it was written in response to a real disaster.
Where: Judah and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: A devastating plague of locusts and a drought had ruined the land's crops, so that even the offerings at the temple had stopped. Joel saw in this disaster a warning of the coming day of the LORD.

**A Devastating Plague Strikes the Land (Joel 1:1–20)** — A severe locust plague, drought, and fire destroy Judah's crops, leaving fields, animals, homes, and temple offerings in distress. Joel calls priests, elders, and all the people to mourn, fast, gather at the temple, and cry out to the Lord.

A terrible disaster has struck the land of Judah. Joel asks the old men if they have ever seen anything like it. “Tell ye your children of it, and let your children tell their children.” Wave after wave of locusts has swept through, eating everything: “That which the palmerworm hath left hath the locust eaten; and that which the locust hath left hath the cankerworm eaten; and that which the cankerworm hath left hath the caterpiller eaten.” These may be different kinds of locusts, or the same insects at different stages of growth.

The locusts are like an invading army with lion's teeth. They strip the vines and the fig trees bare. “The field is wasted, the land mourneth.” With no grain or wine left, even the daily offerings at the temple have stopped. A drought makes it worse, and fire burns the pastures. Even the animals cry out because there is no water.

Joel calls the priests to put on sackcloth and mourn. He tells them, “Sanctify ye a fast, call a solemn assembly... and cry unto the LORD.” This disaster is a warning of something worse: “Alas for the day! for the day of the LORD is at hand.”

**The Approaching Day of the Lord (Joel 2:1–11)** — Joel sounds an alarm in Zion and describes the approaching day of the Lord as an overwhelming force that leaves a fruitful land desolate. The passage uses locust-like and army-like imagery and presents the judgment as coming under God's authority.

“Blow ye the trumpet in Zion, and sound an alarm in my holy mountain.” The day of the LORD is coming, “A day of darkness and of gloominess.” Joel describes a great army sweeping across the land. In front of it the land is like the garden of Eden, but behind it is a desert. The soldiers look like horses and run like war horses. They climb walls, never break ranks, and “enter in at the windows like a thief.”

Joel may be describing the locusts as if they were an army, or a human army coming like a swarm of locusts. Either way, God is the one leading it: “the LORD shall utter his voice before his army.” “For the day of the LORD is great and very terrible; and who can abide it?”

**God Calls His People to Return (Joel 2:12–17)** — Even as judgment approaches, God calls the people to return with all their hearts through fasting, mourning, and prayer. he seeks hearts changed within, not only outward signs of grief, and summons every part of the nation to seek his mercy.

But it is not too late. God says, “turn ye even to me with all your heart, and with fasting, and with weeping, and with mourning: And rend your heart, and not your garments.” People tore their clothes to show grief, but God wants their hearts to change, not just their clothes. “Turn unto the LORD your God: for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness.” “Who knoweth if he will return and repent, and leave a blessing behind him?”

Joel calls everyone to gather at the temple, the elders, the children, and even nursing babies. Even a bride and groom must leave their wedding. The priests should weep between the porch and the altar and pray, “Spare thy people, O LORD, and give not thine heritage to reproach,” so the nations will not ask, “Where is their God?”

**God Promises to Restore the Land (Joel 2:18–27)** — God promises pity for his people, renewed grain, wine, oil, rain, and fruitful fields. he will remove the destructive force and restore the years consumed by locusts, so Judah will know that the Lord is present among them.

God answers their prayer. “Then will the LORD be jealous for his land, and pity his people.” he will send grain, wine, and oil again and drive the invading army away into the desert. Joel speaks comfort to the land and the animals: “Fear not, O land; be glad and rejoice: for the LORD will do great things.” The rains will come, the threshing floors will be full of wheat, and the barrels will overflow with wine and oil.

Then God makes a promise that has comforted many people after hard times: “And I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten.” The people will eat until they are full and praise God, who has done wonders for them. “And ye shall know that I am in the midst of Israel... and my people shall never be ashamed.”

**God Promises to Pour Out His Spirit (Joel 2:28–32)** — God promises to pour out his Spirit on people of every generation and social position, with sons and daughters prophesying and servants included. Signs will precede the day of the Lord, yet everyone who calls on the Lord's name will find deliverance.

Then God promises something even greater than a good harvest: “And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, your young men shall see visions.” his Spirit will come even on servants, men and women alike.

There will be signs in the sky: “The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come. And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered.” On the day of Pentecost, when the Holy Spirit came on Jesus' followers, Peter stood up and quoted this passage, saying, “this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel” (Acts 2:16).

**The Nations Face God's Judgment (Joel 3:1–16)** — God gathers nations for judgment because they scattered his people, divided their land, and traded their lives. The valley of Jehoshaphat becomes a place of divine judgment, while the Lord is a refuge for Israel amid the shaking of heaven and earth.

When God restores his people, he will also judge the nations that hurt them. he will gather them in “the valley of Jehoshaphat,” a name that means the LORD judges. These nations scattered God's people and divided up their land. They even traded children for prostitutes and wine. Tyre, Sidon, and the Philistines stole the treasures of God's temple and sold the people of Judah as slaves to the Greeks. God will pay them back.

Then God calls the nations to prepare for war. In a reversal of Isaiah's famous promise of peace, he says, “Beat your plowshares into swords, and your pruninghooks into spears.” Huge crowds gather for judgment: “Multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision: for the day of the LORD is near in the valley of decision.” The sun and moon will go dark, and “The LORD also shall roar out of Zion.” But for his own people, “the LORD will be the hope of his people, and the strength of the children of Israel.”

**The Lord Dwells With His People (Joel 3:17–21)** — Joel ends with Jerusalem made holy, Judah restored to abundant fruitfulness, and the Lord dwelling in Zion. Egypt and Edom face judgment for violence against Judah, while God promises an enduring future for his people.

Joel ends with a beautiful picture of the future. “So shall ye know that I am the LORD your God dwelling in Zion, my holy mountain.” The land that the locusts ruined will overflow with good things: “the mountains shall drop down new wine, and the hills shall flow with milk, and all the rivers of Judah shall flow with waters.” A spring will flow out of the temple. Egypt and Edom will be desolate because of their violence against Judah. “But Judah shall dwell for ever, and Jerusalem from generation to generation.” The book ends, “for the LORD dwelleth in Zion.”

# Amos
When it happened: In the days of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam II king of Israel, "two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1)—about 760 BC.
When it was written: Amos was not a professional prophet but "an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit" (Amos 7:14), sent from Judah to preach in the northern kingdom. Dating his words "two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1) suggests they were written down after that earthquake.
Where: Amos came from Tekoa, south of Bethlehem, but preached in the northern kingdom, at the royal shrine of Bethel and in Samaria.
The world at the time: Under Jeroboam II, Israel was prosperous and secure. The wealthy built winter houses and summer houses decorated with ivory (Amos 3:15), while the poor were cheated and oppressed.

**God Announces Judgment Against the Nations (Amos 1:1–2:16)** — Amos, a herdsman from Tekoa, announces judgment against surrounding nations for cruelty and violence, then against Judah and Israel. Israel's prosperity hides oppression of the poor, corrupt worship, and rejection of God's commands, so military strength and wealth will not save it.

Amos is not a professional prophet. He is a shepherd and fig farmer from Tekoa, a small town in Judah. God sends him north to preach to Israel during a time when the nation is rich and comfortable. His book begins, “The LORD will roar from Zion.”

Amos starts by announcing God's judgment on Israel's neighbors, one after another. Each message begins the same way: “For three transgressions of Damascus, and for four, I will not turn away the punishment thereof.” This means their sins have piled up beyond counting. Syria was cruel to Gilead. Gaza and Tyre sold whole villages as slaves. Edom chased his brother with the sword. Ammon ripped open pregnant women to grab more land. Moab burned the bones of Edom's king. The people of Israel listening would have cheered each time. Amos even condemns Judah, his own homeland.

Then Amos turns to Israel itself. “For three transgressions of Israel, and for four.” Israel's sins are injustice against the poor: “they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes.” They crush the poor into the dust. A father and son both sleep with the same girl. God reminds them how he brought them out of Egypt and gave them prophets, but they told the prophets, “Prophesy not.” So judgment will come, and even the bravest warrior “shall flee away naked in that day.”

**Israel Must Answer for Its Unfaithfulness (Amos 3:1–4:13)** — God reminds Israel that being chosen brings greater responsibility, not protection from judgment. Amos exposes Samaria's violence, luxury, and empty worship, recalling famine, drought, disease, and losses that failed to lead the people back to God.

God tells Israel, “You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities.” Being God's chosen people does not mean they can get away with sin. It means they are more responsible. Amos asks a series of questions to show that every effect has a cause. “Can two walk together, except they be agreed?” Does a lion roar if it has caught nothing? In the same way, when God speaks, a prophet must prophesy: “The lion hath roared, who will not fear? the Lord GOD hath spoken, who can but prophesy?”

Amos speaks to the wealthy people of Samaria, who have “the winter house with the summer house” and “houses of ivory.” He calls the rich women “ye kine of Bashan,” fat cows, “which oppress the poor, which crush the needy, which say to their masters, Bring, and let us drink.”

God lists all the ways he tried to get their attention. he sent famine: “I also have given you cleanness of teeth in all your cities.” he sent drought, crop disease, locusts, plague, and military defeat. After each one comes the same sad refrain: “yet have ye not returned unto me, saith the LORD.” So God gives a final warning: “prepare to meet thy God, O Israel.”

**Seek the Lord and Live (Amos 5:1–27)** — Amos mourns Israel's coming fall while inviting the people to seek the Lord, reject evil, and establish justice in their courts. God rejects festivals and sacrifices detached from righteousness and warns that the day of the Lord will be darkness for an unfaithful nation.

Amos sings a funeral song for Israel, as if the nation were already dead. But then he gives God's invitation: “Seek ye me, and ye shall live.” They must not seek God at their idol shrines in Bethel and Gilgal, but seek the God who made the stars.

Israel's courts are corrupt. The rich take bribes and turn away the poor. “Seek good, and not evil, that ye may live.” “Hate the evil, and love the good, and establish judgment in the gate.” The city gate was where trials were held.

Many people in Israel were looking forward to “the day of the LORD,” expecting it to be a day of victory for them. Amos warns, “Woe unto you that desire the day of the LORD!... the day of the LORD is darkness, and not light.” It will be like a man who runs from a lion, only to meet a bear. He escapes into his house and leans his hand on the wall, and a snake bites him.

God says he hates their religious festivals and sacrifices: “I hate, I despise your feast days... Take thou away from me the noise of thy songs.” What he wants is this: “But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.” Martin Luther King Jr. famously quoted this verse in his speeches.

**The Comfortable Are Warned of Exile (Amos 6:1–14)** — Leaders and wealthy citizens who feel secure in Zion and Samaria are condemned for luxury and indifference to Israel's ruin. Their feasting, pride, and confidence in military power will end in captivity and national destruction.

“Woe to them that are at ease in Zion, and trust in the mountain of Samaria.” Amos describes Israel's rich and powerful people. They “lie upon beds of ivory,” eat the best lambs and calves, play music, and “drink wine in bowls.” They rub themselves with the finest lotions, “but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph.” They do not care that their nation is falling apart. So they will be the first to go into exile. God says he hates their pride and their palaces. Their injustice is as unnatural as horses running on rocks, for “ye have turned judgment into gall, and the fruit of righteousness into hemlock,” both bitter and poisonous. God will raise up a nation to crush them.

**Amos Sees Visions of Coming Judgment (Amos 7:1–8:3)** — God shows Amos locusts and fire, then relents when Amos pleads. A plumbline and basket of summer fruit announce that Israel's judgment is now near. When Amaziah tries to silence Amos at Bethel, Amos reaffirms his calling and declares judgment.

God shows Amos a series of visions. First, a swarm of locusts eating the crops. Amos begs, “O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small.” And “The LORD repented for this: It shall not be.” Next, a great fire that dries up the sea. Again Amos pleads, and again God relents.

Then Amos sees the Lord standing beside a wall, holding a plumbline, a weight on a string that builders use to check whether a wall is straight. God says he is setting a plumbline among his people. Israel is crooked and will be torn down. “I will not again pass by them any more.”

At this point, Amaziah, the priest at the royal shrine at Bethel, sends a message to King Jeroboam, accusing Amos of plotting against him: “the land is not able to bear all his words.” He tells Amos to go back to Judah and earn his living there. “But prophesy not again any more at Bethel: for it is the king's chapel.” Amos answers, “I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the LORD took me as I followed the flock.” Because Amaziah tried to silence God's word, Amos announces disaster on his family.

In the fourth vision, God shows Amos “a basket of summer fruit,” fruit that is ripe and about to spoil. In Hebrew, the word for summer fruit sounds like the word for end. “The end is come upon my people of Israel.”

**Greed and Dishonesty Bring Devastation (Amos 8:4–14)** — God condemns merchants who exploit the poor through false measures and dishonest scales. Their greed will bring mourning and darkness, followed by a famine of hearing God's word as people search for the message they had rejected.

Amos speaks to the greedy merchants who cheat the poor. They can hardly wait for the holy days to be over so they can get back to business. They use false measures, “making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit,” cheating customers on both the amount and the price. They even sell the leftover chaff mixed with the wheat. They want to “buy the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes.” God swears, “Surely I will never forget any of their works.”

On that day, God will make the sun go down at noon and turn their feasts into funerals. But the worst punishment of all will be a different kind of famine: “I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD.” People will wander from sea to sea looking for a word from God, “and shall not find it.”

**No One Can Escape God's Judgment (Amos 9:1–10)** — Amos sees the Lord declaring that no hiding place, sacred building, or captivity can shield persistent sinners. Israel will be sifted among the nations, and those who confidently deny coming disaster will fall under judgment.

In his last vision, Amos sees the Lord standing beside the altar, ordering the temple to be struck so that it collapses on the worshipers. No one will escape God's judgment. “Though they dig into hell, thence shall mine hand take them; though they climb up to heaven, thence will I bring them down.” Even if they hide at the bottom of the sea or go into captivity, God will find them.

Israel thinks it is special because God brought it out of Egypt. But God says, “Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel?” he has also guided other nations' movements, like the Philistines and the Syrians. Still, God will not completely destroy his people. he will shake them among the nations like grain in a sieve, “yet shall not the least grain fall upon the earth.” The faithful will be kept safe, but the sinners who say disaster will never come will die by the sword.

**God Promises to Restore His People (Amos 9:11–15)** — After judgment, God promises to raise David's fallen house, restore his people, and renew the land with extraordinary abundance. The returned people will rebuild cities, plant vineyards and gardens, and be established securely in their inheritance.

After all the warnings, the book ends with hope. “In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen, and close up the breaches thereof; and I will raise up his ruins, and I will build it as in the days of old.” David's royal family will be restored. At the Jerusalem council in Acts 15, James quoted this verse to show that God planned to include Gentiles among his people.

The land will be so fruitful that “the plowman shall overtake the reaper.” The harvest will be so big that the farmers will still be gathering it when it is time to plant again. “The mountains shall drop sweet wine.” God will bring his people back from exile, and they will rebuild their cities, plant vineyards, and enjoy their fruit. And they will never be uprooted again: “I will plant them upon their land, and they shall no more be pulled up out of their land which I have given them, saith the LORD thy God.”

# Obadiah
When it happened: Most likely soon after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC, when Edom joined in the plunder (Obadiah 1:11–14); some date it earlier.
When it was written: At 21 verses, it is the shortest book in the Old Testament. Obadiah 1:1–6 closely parallels Jeremiah 49:9–16, suggesting the two prophets drew on a shared message about Edom.
Where: The message concerns Edom, the rugged mountain country south and east of the Dead Sea, around Mount Seir.
The world at the time: The Edomites were descended from Esau, Jacob's brother, and had a long history of hostility toward Israel. When Jerusalem fell, Edom rejoiced and took part in the looting (compare Psalm 137:7).

**Edom's Pride Leads to Its Downfall (Obadiah 1:1–9)** — God announces judgment against Edom, Esau's descendants, for trusting in mountain strongholds, wealth, allies, and wisdom. Their elevated settlements and warriors cannot protect them; hidden treasures will be taken, allies will deceive them, and the Lord will bring them down.

Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, just twenty-one verses. It is a message against Edom, the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother. The Edomites lived in rugged mountains south and east of the Dead Sea, with cities built high among the cliffs. They felt completely safe. God says, “The pride of thine heart hath deceived thee, thou that dwellest in the clefts of the rock, whose habitation is high; that saith in his heart, Who shall bring me down to the ground?”

God answers that question: “Though thou exalt thyself as the eagle, and though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down.” If thieves came, they would take only what they wanted. If grape pickers came, they would leave a few grapes. But Edom will be completely stripped bare. Even Edom's allies will turn against it, and its famous wise men and mighty warriors will be destroyed.

**Edom Betrays Its Brother Judah (Obadiah 1:10–14)** — Edom is condemned for violence against Judah, its related nation, during Jerusalem's disaster. Rather than helping, Edom rejoiced, entered the city for gain, seized possessions, cut off fugitives, and delivered survivors to enemies.

Why is God judging Edom? Because of how it treated its brother nation, Judah. “For thy violence against thy brother Jacob shame shall cover thee, and thou shalt be cut off for ever.” When Babylon attacked Jerusalem, Edom stood by and watched. “Even thou wast as one of them.”

Obadiah lists what Edom did wrong. They gloated over Judah's misfortune and laughed at their brothers on the day of their destruction. They marched into Jerusalem's gates and grabbed its treasures. Worst of all, they stood at the crossroads to catch Jews who were trying to escape and handed them over to the enemy. Psalm 137 also remembers how the Edomites cheered when Jerusalem fell.

**The Day of the Lord Approaches (Obadiah 1:15–16)** — Obadiah widens the warning to all nations: the day of the Lord is near, and what violent nations have done will return upon them. No strength, wealth, wisdom, or alliance can place a people beyond God's judgment.

Obadiah's message widens to all the nations. “For the day of the LORD is near upon all the heathen: as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee: thy reward shall return upon thine own head.” Edom will be treated the way it treated others. The nations celebrated and drank on God's holy mountain when Jerusalem fell. Now they will drink the cup of God's judgment, “and they shall be as though they had not been.”

**God Promises Deliverance for His People (Obadiah 1:17–21)** — God promises holiness and deliverance on Mount Zion, the restoration of Israel's inheritance, and judgment on Edom. The prophecy closes with displaced people returning, Mount Esau judged, and the declaration that the kingdom belongs to the Lord.

But for God's people, there is hope. “But upon mount Zion shall be deliverance, and there shall be holiness; and the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.” The roles will be reversed. Israel will be like a fire, and Edom like dry straw that burns up. God's scattered people will come home and take back their land in every direction, including the mountains of Edom. The book ends with a short but powerful statement: “and the kingdom shall be the LORD'S.” Proud nations may rise and fall, but in the end God rules over all.

# Jonah
When it happened: Jonah the son of Amittai prophesied during the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel (2 Kings 14:25), roughly 790–750 BC.
When it was written: The book tells Jonah's story in the third person, and the date of writing is debated; proposals range from Jonah's own time to after the exile.
Where: Joppa on the coast of Israel, the Mediterranean Sea, and Nineveh, the great city of Assyria on the Tigris River.
The world at the time: Assyria was Israel's most feared enemy, known for its cruelty in war. Within a few decades it would destroy the northern kingdom.

**Jonah Runs Away from God's Command (Jonah 1:1–17)** — God sends Jonah to warn Nineveh, but he boards a ship for Tarshish instead. A violent storm exposes his flight, and Jonah tells the sailors to throw him overboard. The sea becomes calm, the sailors fear the Lord, and God prepares a great fish to swallow Jonah.

God tells Jonah, “Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it; for their wickedness is come up before me.” Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, Israel's cruelest enemy. Instead, “Jonah rose up to flee unto Tarshish from the presence of the LORD.” He goes down to the port of Joppa, pays his fare, and boards a ship sailing the opposite direction. Then “the LORD sent out a great wind into the sea,” and the storm is so strong that the ship is about to break apart. The sailors are terrified. Each one prays to his own god, and they throw cargo overboard. Meanwhile, Jonah is below deck, “fast asleep.” The captain wakes him: “What meanest thou, O sleeper? arise, call upon thy God.” The sailors cast lots to find out who is to blame, and the lot falls on Jonah. He admits, “I am an Hebrew; and I fear the LORD, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land,” and that he is running from him. He tells them, “Take me up, and cast me forth into the sea; so shall the sea be calm unto you.” The sailors row hard to reach land, but they cannot. Finally they pray to the LORD not to hold them guilty, and they throw Jonah over. At once “the sea ceased from her raging.” The pagan sailors now fear the LORD and offer him sacrifices. “Now the LORD had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.” Jesus later pointed to this as a sign of his own three days in the grave (Matthew 12:40).

**Jonah Prays from Inside the Great Fish (Jonah 2:1–10)** — Inside the great fish, Jonah prays to God from the edge of death, remembers that the Lord heard him, and declares that salvation belongs to the Lord. God then commands the fish to put Jonah onto dry land.

“Then Jonah prayed unto the LORD his God out of the fish's belly.” His prayer reads like a psalm. He remembers sinking into the deep: “all thy billows and thy waves passed over me,” and “the weeds were wrapped about my head.” He felt cut off from God: “I am cast out of thy sight; yet I will look again toward thy holy temple.” But at the edge of death, “When my soul fainted within me I remembered the LORD,” and God heard him. Jonah promises to give thanks and keep his vows, and he ends with the heart of the book: “Salvation is of the LORD.” Then “the LORD spake unto the fish, and it vomited out Jonah upon the dry land.”

**Nineveh Hears God's Warning and Repents (Jonah 3:1–10)** — God sends Jonah to Nineveh again, and he announces that the city will be overthrown in forty days. The people and king respond with fasting, sackcloth, and a turn from evil and violence, so God relents from the announced destruction.

“And the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the second time.” This time Jonah obeys. Nineveh is so large that it takes three days to walk across it. Jonah walks one day into the city and gives a short message: “Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown.” The response is amazing. “So the people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them even to the least of them.” When the king hears, he gets up from his throne, takes off his royal robe, puts on sackcloth, and sits in ashes. He orders everyone, even the animals, to fast and wear sackcloth: “let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands. Who can tell if God will turn and repent?” And God does. “God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not.”

**Jonah Struggles with God's Mercy (Jonah 4:1–11)** — Jonah becomes angry when God spares Nineveh. Through a plant that gives shade and quickly dies, God exposes Jonah's concern for what he did not create and contrasts it with his own compassion for Nineveh's people and animals.

Jonah should be happy, but “it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry.” Now he explains why he ran away in the first place: “for I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness.” He did not want his enemies to be forgiven. He even asks God to take his life. God answers with a question: “Doest thou well to be angry?” Jonah leaves the city and sits on a hill to the east, waiting to see if Nineveh will still be destroyed. God makes a leafy plant grow up over him for shade, “So Jonah was exceeding glad of the gourd.” But the next morning God sends a worm to kill the plant, and then a hot east wind. As the sun beats on his head, Jonah again wants to die. God asks if he is right to be angry about the plant, and Jonah says, “I do well to be angry, even unto death.” God's final words make the point. Jonah cared about a plant he did not plant or grow, “which came up in a night, and perished in a night.” “And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?” Sixscore thousand is 120,000. The book ends there, with God's question left for Jonah, and the reader, to answer.

# Micah
When it happened: In the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah (Micah 1:1)—roughly 740–700 BC. Jeremiah 26:18 recalls Micah preaching in Hezekiah's reign.
When it was written: Micah came from Moresheth, a country town in the Judean foothills, and preached against Jerusalem. Over a century later, elders in Jeremiah's day quoted his warning (Jeremiah 26:18; Micah 3:12), showing his words had been preserved.
Where: Moresheth in the lowlands of Judah; his messages concern both Samaria and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Micah lived at the same time as Isaiah, under the shadow of Assyria, which destroyed Samaria in 722 BC. In Judah, the powerful were seizing the fields and houses of the poor (Micah 2:2).

**God Announces Judgment Against Israel and Judah (Micah 1:1–16)** — Micah warns both northern Israel and Judah that idolatry and disobedience will bring destruction. Samaria will be reduced to rubble, and the wound of Israel's rebellion will reach Judah and Jerusalem, bringing suffering and captivity to towns and families.

Micah calls the whole earth to listen as God comes to court: “Hear, all ye people; hearken, O earth.” The LORD comes down from heaven, and “the mountains shall be molten under him... as wax before the fire.” The cause is the sin of his own people. Their capital cities are the center of it: “What is the transgression of Jacob? is it not Samaria? and what are the high places of Judah? are they not Jerusalem?” God will turn Samaria into “an heap of the field” and smash its idols. Micah is so grieved that he says, “I will wail and howl, I will go stripped and naked,” because “her wound is incurable; for it is come unto Judah.” He then lists about a dozen towns near his own home in Judah. In Hebrew, each town's name makes a sad pun about what will happen to it. The chapter ends by telling the people to shave their heads in mourning, “for they are gone into captivity from thee.”

**The Powerful Take What Belongs to Others (Micah 2:1–13)** — God condemns powerful people who seize fields, houses, and families' inheritance through injustice. Though people prefer messages of comfort, God promises judgment and also a future gathering of the scattered remnant, with the Lord himself leading them.

“Woe to them that devise iniquity, and work evil upon their beds!” These are rich and powerful people who lie awake planning how to grab more. When morning comes, they do it, simply because they can. “They covet fields, and take them by violence; and houses, and take them away.” Families lose the land God gave them. So God will take the land from these land-grabbers. The people do not want to hear it. They tell Micah, “Prophesy ye not.” Micah says what they really want is a preacher who promises good times: “I will prophesy unto thee of wine and of strong drink; he shall even be the prophet of this people.” Yet the chapter ends with hope. God says, “I will surely gather the remnant of Israel,” like sheep in a fold. “The breaker,” one who breaks open the way, will go before them through the gate, “and their king shall pass before them, and the LORD on the head of them.”

**Corrupt Leaders Bring Jerusalem Toward Ruin (Micah 3:1–12)** — Micah exposes rulers, priests, and prophets who exploit the people, accept bribes, and sell religious messages for gain. Their claim that God will protect Jerusalem is false; because of their corruption, Zion and Jerusalem will become ruins.

Micah speaks to Israel's leaders: “Is it not for you to know judgment?” Instead, they “hate the good, and love the evil.” In a shocking picture, he says they treat the poor like meat for a stew. They skin them, break their bones, and “chop them in pieces, as for the pot.” The false prophets are no better. They cry “Peace” to anyone who feeds them, and they declare war on anyone who does not. But Micah says, “truly I am full of power by the spirit of the LORD... to declare unto Jacob his transgression, and to Israel his sin.” Everyone in power is for sale: “The heads thereof judge for reward, and the priests thereof teach for hire, and the prophets thereof divine for money.” Still, they feel safe and say, “Is not the LORD among us? none evil can come upon us.” Micah gives them God's answer: “Therefore shall Zion for your sake be plowed as a field, and Jerusalem shall become heaps.” A hundred years later, people still remembered this warning. It saved the prophet Jeremiah's life when his enemies wanted to kill him for saying the same thing (Jeremiah 26:18).

**God Promises Peace and a Restored Kingdom (Micah 4:1–13)** — After announcing destruction, Micah foresees nations seeking God's instruction and turning weapons into tools for cultivation. God will gather the weak and scattered, but Jerusalem will first experience distress and exile in Babylon before promised deliverance.

Right after saying the temple hill will become a ruin, Micah sees it raised up high “in the last days.” Nations stream to it, saying, “Come, and let us go up to the mountain of the LORD... and he will teach us of his ways, and we will walk in his paths.” Then comes one of the Bible's most famous pictures of peace: “they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift up a sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” Plowshares and pruning hooks are farm tools, so weapons will be turned into tools for growing food. “But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid.” Isaiah records nearly the same words (Isaiah 2:2–4). God promises to gather the lame and the outcast and make them strong. But first, Jerusalem must suffer like a woman in labor: “thou shalt go even to Babylon; there shalt thou be delivered; there the LORD shall redeem thee.” Micah names Babylon more than a century before it took Judah captive.

**A Ruler Will Come from Bethlehem (Micah 5:1–15)** — God promises a future ruler from Bethlehem who will shepherd Israel in the Lord's strength and bring security. The prophecy also calls people away from trust in military power, fortified cities, sorcery, idols, and false worship.

Jerusalem is under siege, and its ruler is humiliated, struck “with a rod upon the cheek.” But God points to a small town: “But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting.” Bethlehem was King David's hometown. Seven hundred years later, when wise men came looking for the newborn King of the Jews, the priests quoted this verse to show he would be born in Bethlehem (Matthew 2:5–6). This ruler “shall stand and feed in the strength of the LORD,” like a shepherd. He will be great “unto the ends of the earth,” and “this man shall be the peace.” God's scattered people will live among the nations like dew from heaven and like a lion among sheep. Then God will remove everything Israel trusted in place of him: its horses and chariots, its fortified cities, its witchcraft, and its idols, “and thou shalt no more worship the work of thine hands.”

**What God Truly Requires of His People (Micah 6:1–16)** — God recalls his past faithfulness to Israel and rejects the idea that costly offerings can replace obedience. he requires justice, mercy, and humility, while warning that dishonest trade, violence, and idolatrous practices will bring loss and disgrace.

God takes his people to court, with the mountains as witnesses. But he speaks like a hurt friend: “O my people, what have I done unto thee? and wherein have I wearied thee? testify against me.” He reminds them how he brought them out of Egypt, sent them Moses, Aaron, and Miriam, and turned Balaam's curse into a blessing. The people respond by asking what they should bring to make God happy: “shall I come before him with burnt offerings...? Will the LORD be pleased with thousands of rams, or with ten thousands of rivers of oil? shall I give my firstborn for my transgression?” Micah answers with one of the best-known verses in the Old Testament: “He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?” God then exposes cheating in the marketplace, with “wicked balances” and “the bag of deceitful weights,” along with violence and lies. Because of this, they will work and not enjoy the results: “Thou shalt sow, but thou shalt not reap.”

**God Will Forgive and Restore His People (Micah 7:1–20)** — Micah mourns widespread corruption and broken trust, yet waits for the God of salvation. He ends by praising God's mercy, forgiveness, and faithfulness to his covenant with Jacob and Abraham, promising that sin will not have the final word.

Micah feels like someone who goes to the garden after the harvest and finds nothing left to eat. “The good man is perished out of the earth: and there is none upright among men.” Officials and judges take bribes. No one can be trusted, not even a friend or a spouse: “a man's enemies are the men of his own house,” a line Jesus later quoted (Matthew 10:36). But Micah turns to God: “Therefore I will look unto the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation: my God will hear me.” He tells his enemy, “Rejoice not against me, O mine enemy: when I fall, I shall arise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD shall be a light unto me.” God promises that the walls will be rebuilt and that people will come from far away. Micah's name means Who is like the LORD, and he closes the book by asking the same question: “Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity... he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy.” God “will subdue our iniquities; and thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea.” he will keep the promises he made long ago to Abraham and Jacob.

# Nahum
When it happened: After the fall of the Egyptian city of Thebes, called No (Nahum 3:8), in 663 BC, and before the fall of Nineveh in 612 BC.
When it was written: The book calls itself "The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite" (Nahum 1:1); his hometown of Elkosh has not been identified with certainty.
Where: The message concerns Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire.
The world at the time: Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and dominated Judah for a century. In 612 BC Nineveh fell to the Babylonians and the Medes.

**The Lord Announces Judgment Against Nineveh (Nahum 1:1–15)** — Nahum declares that the Lord is slow to anger yet will not leave wickedness unpunished. Nineveh's power cannot protect it, while God is a refuge for those who trust him and promises Judah relief from Assyrian oppression.

About a hundred years after Nineveh repented at Jonah's preaching, the city had returned to its cruelty. Nahum's message is that God will now judge it. “God is jealous, and the LORD revengeth.” In other words, he will not let evil win. “The LORD is slow to anger, and great in power, and will not at all acquit the wicked.” Storms, mountains, and seas shake before him: “Who can stand before his indignation?” Yet the same God is safe shelter for his people: “The LORD is good, a strong hold in the day of trouble; and he knoweth them that trust in him.” he tells Judah, which had suffered under Assyria for a century, “now will I break his yoke from off thee, and will burst thy bonds in sunder.” Nahum pictures a messenger running over the hills with the news: “Behold upon the mountains the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace!” Isaiah used similar words (Isaiah 52:7).

**An Overwhelming Army Comes Against Nineveh (Nahum 2:1–13)** — Nahum pictures an attacking army overwhelming Nineveh's defenses, scattering its people, and taking its silver and gold. Assyria, once like a lion's den filled with prey from conquered nations, will become empty because the Lord stands against it.

Nahum describes the attack on Nineveh as if he is watching it happen. The enemy soldiers carry red shields and wear scarlet. “The chariots shall rage in the streets... they shall seem like torches, they shall run like the lightnings.” Nineveh's defenders rush to the wall, but “The gates of the rivers shall be opened, and the palace shall be dissolved.” Ancient writers later reported that floodwaters helped break Nineveh's walls. The people flee, and officers shout “Stand, stand,” but no one turns back. The invaders cry, “Take ye the spoil of silver, take the spoil of gold.” Then the city is “empty, and void, and waste.” Nahum mocks Assyria, whose kings liked to compare themselves to lions: “Where is the dwelling of the lions?” The lion used to fill its den with prey torn from other nations. Now God says, “Behold, I am against thee... and the sword shall devour thy young lions.”

**Nineveh's Violence Leads to Its Destruction (Nahum 3:1–7)** — God condemns Nineveh as a city of bloodshed, lies, plunder, and corrupting influence. The city's violence is met with battle and devastation, and the proud capital will be publicly exposed, shamed, and left without comfort.

“Woe to the bloody city! it is all full of lies and robbery.” Nahum fills the scene with sound and motion: “The noise of a whip, and the noise of the rattling of the wheels, and of the pransing horses, and of the jumping chariots.” Swords flash, and there are so many dead that soldiers stumble over the bodies. Nineveh is pictured as a prostitute and witch who lured and enslaved whole nations. Now God will expose her to shame before the world. When people see her ruins, they will say, “Nineveh is laid waste: who will bemoan her?” No one will be sorry to see her go.

**No Stronghold Can Save the City (Nahum 3:8–13)** — Nahum points to the fall of No, a well-defended city with strong allies, to challenge Nineveh's confidence. Its fortifications, soldiers, gates, and allies will prove no stronger when God's judgment arrives.

Nahum asks Nineveh, “Art thou better than populous No?” No was the great Egyptian city of Thebes. It sat among the waters of the Nile and had powerful allies in Ethiopia, Egypt, and Libya. Yet Assyria itself had conquered Thebes in 663 BC. “Yet was she carried away, she went into captivity.” Her children were killed and her leaders were put in chains. Nineveh will fare no better. Its fortresses are like fig trees with ripe figs: shake them, and the fruit falls right into the eater's mouth. Its soldiers will lose their courage, and “the gates of thy land shall be set wide open unto thine enemies.”

**Nineveh Cannot Escape Its Final Defeat (Nahum 3:14–19)** — Nineveh's preparations for siege will fail as fire and sword consume it and its leaders disappear. The city's wound is beyond healing, and nations harmed by Assyria's continual cruelty will rejoice at the end of its oppression.

With bitter humor, Nahum tells Nineveh to get ready for the siege. They should draw water, strengthen the walls, and make bricks. It will not help: “There shall the fire devour thee; the sword shall cut thee off.” Assyria's merchants and officials are like a swarm of locusts. When the sun comes up, they fly away and no one can find them. The book ends with words to the king himself: “Thy shepherds slumber, O king of Assyria... thy people is scattered upon the mountains, and no man gathereth them. There is no healing of thy bruise; thy wound is grievous.” Everyone who hears the news will clap for joy, “for upon whom hath not thy wickedness passed continually?” Nineveh fell in 612 BC and was never rebuilt.

# Habakkuk
When it happened: Likely just before Babylon invaded Judah, around 610–600 BC, since God says he is raising up the Chaldeans (Habakkuk 1:6).
When it was written: The book records a dialogue between the prophet and God (chapters 1–2), followed by a prayer with musical directions, as in the Psalms (chapter 3).
Where: Judah.
The world at the time: After the reforms of King Josiah, Judah slid back into violence and injustice. Meanwhile the Chaldeans, the Babylonians, were rising to replace Assyria as the great power of the region.

**Habakkuk Questions Why God Allows Injustice (Habakkuk 1:1–11)** — Habakkuk cries out over violence, twisted justice, and corruption in Judah. God answers that He is raising the fierce Babylonians to bring judgment, though their swift conquests and pride reveal that they too are violent and corrupt.

Habakkuk does something unusual for a prophet. Instead of speaking to the people for God, he speaks to God for himself, and he complains. “O LORD, how long shall I cry, and thou wilt not hear! even cry out unto thee of violence, and thou wilt not save!” He sees fighting and injustice everywhere in Judah. “The law is slacked, and judgment doth never go forth,” because the wicked surround the righteous. God answers, but not in the way Habakkuk expects: “I will work a work in your days, which ye will not believe, though it be told you. For, lo, I raise up the Chaldeans, that bitter and hasty nation.” The Chaldeans are the Babylonians. God describes their army as faster than leopards and fiercer than wolves. They swoop down like eagles, laugh at kings, and capture cities. But they make their own strength their god.

**The Prophet Struggles with God's Answer (Habakkuk 1:12–2:1)** — Habakkuk knows God is holy and just, yet asks how He can use a nation more wicked than Judah to punish His people. He compares Babylon to fishermen gathering nations in nets, then waits attentively for God's response.

Habakkuk is even more confused now. He knows God is holy: “Thou art of purer eyes than to behold evil.” So how can God use people more wicked than Judah to punish Judah? “wherefore lookest thou upon them that deal treacherously, and holdest thy tongue when the wicked devoureth the man that is more righteous than he?” He compares Babylon to a fisherman who drags whole nations out of the sea with hooks and nets. Then the fisherman worships his net: “they sacrifice unto their net,” because it makes them rich. Habakkuk does not walk away from God with his questions. He waits for an answer: “I will stand upon my watch, and set me upon the tower, and will watch to see what he will say unto me.”

**The Righteous Will Live by Faith (Habakkuk 2:2–5)** — God commands Habakkuk to write the vision clearly and wait for its appointed fulfillment. The proud conqueror is not upright and will fall, but the righteous will live through faithful trust in God rather than confidence in power and gain.

God answers: “Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables, that he may run that readeth it.” In other words, write it so clearly that someone can read it at a glance and run to tell others. The answer may take time, but it will come: “though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come, it will not tarry.” Then God draws a line between two kinds of people. The proud Babylonian is puffed up and not right inside, “but the just shall live by his faith.” “The just” means the righteous person, who will live by trusting God. Paul quoted this line in Romans and Galatians to explain that people are made right with God through faith (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11). Hebrews quotes it too (Hebrews 10:38). Meanwhile the proud conqueror is like a drunk who is never satisfied, gathering nation after nation.

**Babylon's Cruelty Will Not Go Unpunished (Habakkuk 2:6–20)** — God pronounces woes against those who gain wealth through violence, build cities by bloodshed, exploit neighbors, destroy creation, and trust idols. Babylon's victories do not excuse its wickedness, and the earth will know God's glory.

The nations Babylon crushed will sing a song mocking it. The song has five “woes.” The first is against the one who steals: “Woe to him that increaseth that which is not his! how long?” The robbed nations will one day rise up and rob him back. The next is against the one who builds his house with dishonest gain. Even the building will testify against him: “the stone shall cry out of the wall, and the beam out of the timber shall answer it.” Then comes “Woe to him that buildeth a town with blood.” In the middle of these woes, God gives a promise: “For the earth shall be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea.” Another woe condemns the one who gets his neighbor drunk to shame him. The last woe mocks people who pray to idols, saying “to the wood, Awake; to the dumb stone, Arise.” They are covered in gold and silver, “and there is no breath at all in the midst of it.” The chapter ends with a contrast: “But the LORD is in his holy temple: let all the earth keep silence before him.”

**Habakkuk Remembers God's Mighty Works (Habakkuk 3:1–15)** — In a poetic prayer, Habakkuk asks God to remember mercy amid judgment and recalls His power over creation and His past deliverance of His people. Mountains, seas, and nations tremble before the God whose authority surpasses every earthly army.

Chapter 3 is a prayer set to music, like a psalm. Habakkuk has heard what God plans to do, and it scares him. He asks, “O LORD, revive thy work in the midst of the years... in wrath remember mercy.” Then he remembers how God came to rescue His people in the past, from the wilderness of Sinai: “God came from Teman, and the Holy One from mount Paran... His glory covered the heavens, and the earth was full of his praise.” When God appears, mountains crumble and nations tremble: “the everlasting mountains were scattered, the perpetual hills did bow: his ways are everlasting.” Habakkuk recalls the sea and the rivers splitting at God's command, as they did at the Red Sea and the Jordan, and the sun and moon standing still, as in Joshua's day. God marched out to save His people and crush the wicked.

**Habakkuk Chooses to Rejoice in the Lord (Habakkuk 3:16–19)** — Though he trembles at the approaching disaster and imagines failed crops and empty herds, Habakkuk chooses to rejoice in the God of his salvation. His strength rests in the Lord, who enables him to stand through coming hardship.

Just thinking about the coming invasion makes Habakkuk shake: “my lips quivered at the voice: rottenness entered into my bones.” Still, he decides to wait quietly for God. Then he writes one of the most faith-filled passages in the Bible. Even if everything fails, he will trust God: “Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls: Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation.” The book that began with “how long?” ends with confidence: “The LORD God is my strength, and he will make my feet like hinds' feet, and he will make me to walk upon mine high places.” A hind is a female deer that can climb steep, rocky hills without falling.

# Zephaniah
When it happened: In the days of Josiah king of Judah (Zephaniah 1:1), who reigned 640–609 BC—likely before Josiah's reforms began in earnest.
When it was written: Zephaniah's family line is traced back four generations to Hizkiah, possibly King Hezekiah (Zephaniah 1:1).
Where: Jerusalem and Judah.
The world at the time: After the long reign of Manasseh, idolatry was widespread in Judah—the worship of Baal, the host of heaven, and Malcham (Zephaniah 1:4–5). Assyria's power was beginning to fade.

**God Warns Judah of Coming Judgment (Zephaniah 1:1–13)** — During Josiah's reign, Zephaniah warns Judah and Jerusalem that idol worship, corrupt leadership, violence, deceit, and refusal to seek God will bring judgment. Wealth and comfort cannot protect people who assume that the Lord will never act.

Zephaniah begins with a warning as big as the Flood: “I will utterly consume all things from off the land, saith the LORD.” People, animals, birds, and fish will all be swept away. Then God narrows His aim to Judah and Jerusalem. He will remove what is left of Baal worship, the idol priests, and “them that worship the host of heaven upon the housetops.” He will also judge those who try to worship both the LORD and the god Malcham, and those who simply never seek God at all. Royal officials who copy foreign fashions and servants who fill their masters' houses “with violence and deceit” will be punished. God says, “I will search Jerusalem with candles,” looking into every dark corner. He is after the comfortable people who are “settled on their lees.” Lees are the dregs at the bottom of a wine jar, so these people are like wine left to sit until it turns thick and stale. They say in their heart, “The LORD will not do good, neither will he do evil.” They think God does nothing. Their houses and vineyards will go to others.

**The Great Day of the Lord Approaches (Zephaniah 1:14–2:3)** — Zephaniah describes the day of the Lord as a near time of wrath, darkness, distress, and destruction that neither wealth nor defenses can prevent. Before it arrives, the humble are called to seek the Lord, righteousness, and humility.

“The great day of the LORD is near, it is near, and hasteth greatly.” Zephaniah piles up words to describe it: “That day is a day of wrath, a day of trouble and distress, a day of wasteness and desolation, a day of darkness and gloominess, a day of clouds and thick darkness.” These lines later inspired a famous medieval hymn called Dies Irae, which is Latin for Day of Wrath. Trumpets sound against walled cities and high towers. People stumble around like blind men. Money will not help: “Neither their silver nor their gold shall be able to deliver them in the day of the LORD'S wrath.” But there is still time. Zephaniah urges the nation to gather before the day comes. “Seek ye the LORD, all ye meek of the earth... seek righteousness, seek meekness: it may be ye shall be hid in the day of the LORD'S anger.”

**Judgment Reaches the Surrounding Nations (Zephaniah 2:4–15)** — Philistia, Moab, Ammon, Ethiopia, and Assyria face judgment for pride and hostility. Nineveh's confidence and greatness will become desolation, while God promises that the remnant of Judah will inherit and find pasture in lands once held by enemies.

The judgment reaches Judah's neighbors in every direction. To the west, the Philistine cities of Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, and Ekron will be emptied, and their coast will become pasture for Judah's shepherds. To the east, Moab and Ammon mocked God's people, so they will become like Sodom and Gomorrah, a land of weeds and salt pits. To the far south, the Ethiopians will fall by the sword. To the north, mighty Assyria will be destroyed: God “will make Nineveh a desolation, and dry like a wilderness.” Wild animals and birds will nest in the ruins of its palaces. This is the proud city “that said in her heart, I am, and there is none beside me: how is she become a desolation, a place for beasts to lie down in!” Nineveh fell in 612 BC, just as Zephaniah said.

**Jerusalem Refuses to Listen to God (Zephaniah 3:1–8)** — Jerusalem's leaders, judges, prophets, and priests are corrupt, and the city refuses correction despite God's constant righteousness and warnings through other nations' falls. God therefore announces judgment against the rebellious city and gathered kingdoms.

Then Zephaniah turns back to Jerusalem: “Woe to her that is filthy and polluted, to the oppressing city!” “She obeyed not the voice; she received not correction; she trusted not in the LORD; she drew not near to her God.” Her leaders are like wild animals. “Her princes within her are roaring lions; her judges are evening wolves,” so greedy they leave nothing until morning. Her prophets are reckless liars, and her priests have made the temple unclean. Yet the whole time, God has been right there in the city: “every morning doth he bring his judgment to light, he faileth not; but the unjust knoweth no shame.” God had wiped out other nations as a warning, hoping Jerusalem would learn, but it only became more eager to do wrong. So He tells the faithful, “wait ye upon me,” for He will gather the nations and pour out His anger on the whole earth.

**God Promises to Gather a Humble People (Zephaniah 3:9–13)** — After judgment, God promises purified worship among peoples from distant lands and a humble remnant in Jerusalem. These survivors will trust in His name, reject lies and wrongdoing, and live securely like sheep resting without fear.

After the fire of judgment, God promises a new beginning: “For then will I turn to the people a pure language, that they may all call upon the name of the LORD, to serve him with one consent.” This is a reversal of the tower of Babel, where God confused the languages. Worshipers will come with offerings from as far away as Ethiopia. The proud will be removed from Jerusalem, and in their place, “I will also leave in the midst of thee an afflicted and poor people, and they shall trust in the name of the LORD.” This humble remnant “shall not do iniquity, nor speak lies.” Like sheep, “they shall feed and lie down, and none shall make them afraid.”

**The Lord Rejoices Over His Restored People (Zephaniah 3:14–20)** — God promises to remove shame, gather the scattered, defeat oppressors, and dwell among His people. Zion is called to rejoice because the Lord will save, restore, and honor those once weak, grieving, and disgraced.

The book that began with “a day of wrath” ends with a song of joy: “Sing, O daughter of Zion; shout, O Israel; be glad and rejoice with all the heart.” God has taken away their punishment and driven out their enemy, and “the king of Israel, even the LORD, is in the midst of thee.” Jerusalem is told, “Fear thou not.” Then comes one of the most tender verses in the prophets: “The LORD thy God in the midst of thee is mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing.” In other words, God Himself sings over His people. He will rescue the lame, gather the outcasts, and turn their shame into praise. “For I will make you a name and a praise among all people of the earth, when I turn back your captivity before your eyes, saith the LORD.”

# Haggai
When it happened: In the second year of King Darius of Persia (520 BC), between the sixth and ninth months (Haggai 1:1; 2:10).
When it was written: His four messages are each dated to the day (Haggai 1:1; 2:1; 2:10; 2:20), all within a few months of 520 BC.
Where: Jerusalem.
The world at the time: The first exiles had returned from Babylon eighteen years earlier, but opposition had stopped the rebuilding of the temple (Ezra 4–5). Zerubbabel was governor of Judah, and Joshua was high priest.

**God Calls His People to Rebuild the Temple (Haggai 1:1–15)** — Returned exiles in Judah have neglected the ruined temple while completing their own homes. Through Haggai, God connects their poor harvests to misplaced priorities, calls them to rebuild, and assures Zerubbabel, Joshua, and the people that He is with them when they obey.

In 520 BC, Haggai brings God's word to Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest. The people are saying, “The time is not come, the time that the LORD'S house should be built.” God answers with a sharp question: “Is it time for you, O ye, to dwell in your cieled houses, and this house lie waste?” Cieled houses had fine wooden paneling. The people had nice homes, while God's temple was still a pile of ruins. Twice God says, “Consider your ways.” They have worked hard but have little to show for it: “Ye have sown much, and bring in little; ye eat, but ye have not enough... and he that earneth wages earneth wages to put it into a bag with holes.” The reason is simple: “Because of mine house that is waste, and ye run every man unto his own house.” So God sent drought on their crops. His command is clear: “Go up to the mountain, and bring wood, and build the house.” This time the people listen. Haggai brings a short but powerful message, “I am with you, saith the LORD,” and God stirs up the spirit of the leaders and the people. Just twenty-three days later, they start working on the temple.

**God Encourages the Builders with a Greater Promise (Haggai 2:1–9)** — Some builders remember Solomon's temple and see the new work as small by comparison, but God commands them to be strong because His Spirit remains with them. He promises to fill His house with glory and give peace beyond what its outward appearance suggests.

About a month into the work, the builders grow discouraged. A few of the oldest people had seen Solomon's magnificent temple before Babylon destroyed it, about seventy years earlier. God asks, “Who is left among you that saw this house in her first glory? and how do ye see it now? is it not in your eyes in comparison of it as nothing?” But He tells them, “Yet now be strong, O Zerubbabel... and be strong, O Joshua... and be strong, all ye people of the land, saith the LORD, and work: for I am with you.” “My spirit remaineth among you: fear ye not.” Then God makes a great promise. He will soon “shake the heavens, and the earth,” and “I will shake all nations, and the desire of all nations shall come: and I will fill this house with glory.” They should not worry that they lack gold. “The silver is mine, and the gold is mine, saith the LORD of hosts.” “The glory of this latter house shall be greater than of the former... and in this place will I give peace.” Hebrews 12:26 quotes the promise of God shaking heaven and earth.

**God Calls for Holiness and Promises Blessing (Haggai 2:10–19)** — Questions about ceremonial holiness and uncleanness reveal that religious activity cannot replace obedient lives. God recalls failed harvests before the rebuilding resumed, then promises blessing from that day forward although the fields have not yet shown its results.

Two months later, Haggai asks the priests two questions about the law. If holy meat touches other food, does the food become holy? The priests say no. But if a person made unclean by touching a dead body touches food, does it become unclean? They say yes. Holiness does not spread by touch, but uncleanness does. Haggai explains, “So is this people, and so is this nation before me... and that which they offer there is unclean.” Their sacrifices could not make them holy while they ignored God's house. God reminds them of the years before they started rebuilding. A pile of grain that should have been twenty measures had only ten. He struck their crops “with blasting and with mildew and with hail,” yet they did not turn to Him. Now, from the day the foundation was laid, things will change. The seed is not yet planted, and the trees have not yet produced fruit, but God promises, “from this day will I bless you.”

**God Promises to Establish Zerubbabel (Haggai 2:20–23)** — God promises Zerubbabel that He will shake creation and overthrow earthly kingdoms. Though Zerubbabel serves as governor under Persia, God chooses this descendant of David's royal line and makes him like a signet ring, a sign of His continuing purpose.

On the same day, Haggai brings a final message just for Zerubbabel, the governor and a grandson of King Jehoiachin. God says, “I will shake the heavens and the earth; And I will overthrow the throne of kingdoms,” throwing down chariots and riders. Then He makes a personal promise: “In that day, saith the LORD of hosts, will I take thee, O Zerubbabel, my servant... and will make thee as a signet: for I have chosen thee.” A signet ring was a king's seal, used to sign official orders. Years before, God had told Zerubbabel's grandfather Jehoiachin, also called Coniah, that even if he were a signet ring on God's hand, God would pull it off (Jeremiah 22:24). Now God puts the ring back on. David's royal line still has a future, and both Matthew and Luke list Zerubbabel among the ancestors of Jesus (Matthew 1:12; Luke 3:27).

# Zechariah
When it happened: Zechariah began prophesying in the eighth month of the second year of Darius (520 BC; Zechariah 1:1), and his dated messages continue into Darius's fourth year (518 BC; Zechariah 7:1). Chapters 9–14 are undated.
When it was written: Zechariah came from a priestly family; his grandfather Iddo is listed among the priests who returned from exile (Nehemiah 12:4, 16). Chapters 9–14 are undated and different in style from chapters 1–8.
Where: Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Like Haggai, Zechariah encouraged the returned exiles to finish rebuilding the temple under Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest, in the Persian province of Judah.

**God Calls His People to Return (Zechariah 1:1–6)** — To returned exiles rebuilding in Jerusalem, God calls for repentance and warns them to learn from ancestors who ignored earlier prophets. If they return to Him, He promises to return to them; rebuilding the city must include renewed faithfulness.

Zechariah begins preaching in 520 BC, only two months after Haggai. His first message is a call to come back to God: “Turn ye unto me, saith the LORD of hosts, and I will turn unto you.” He warns the people not to repeat the mistakes of their ancestors: “Be ye not as your fathers, unto whom the former prophets have cried... but they did not hear.” Then he asks, “Your fathers, where are they? and the prophets, do they live for ever?” Both the ancestors and the prophets were gone, but God's words had come true. Even their ancestors finally admitted that God had treated them just as He had warned.

**Visions of Jerusalem's Coming Restoration (Zechariah 1:7–2:13)** — Night visions of horses, horns, craftsmen, and a measured city promise mercy for Jerusalem, judgment on powers that scattered God's people, and a future city too full for ordinary walls. God Himself will be a wall of fire and dwell within it.

In one night, Zechariah sees eight visions, and an angel explains each one. In the first, a man on a red horse stands among myrtle trees in a valley, with other horses behind him. They have patrolled the earth and report that the whole world is at rest. But Jerusalem is still in ruins, so the angel asks, “O LORD of hosts, how long wilt thou not have mercy on Jerusalem?” God answers with comfort: “I am jealous for Jerusalem and for Zion with a great jealousy... I am returned to Jerusalem with mercies: my house shall be built in it.” In the second vision, four horns stand for the nations that scattered God's people, and four craftsmen come to break them. In the third, a young man goes out with a measuring line to mark off Jerusalem's walls. An angel stops him, because the city will have so many people that it will be like “towns without walls.” God says, “For I... will be unto her a wall of fire round about, and will be the glory in the midst of her.” God calls the exiles still in Babylon to come home, for “he that toucheth you toucheth the apple of his eye.” The apple of the eye is the pupil, the part a person protects most. “Sing and rejoice, O daughter of Zion: for, lo, I come, and I will dwell in the midst of thee.” Many nations will join themselves to the LORD.

**God Cleanses the High Priest and Encourages Zerubbabel (Zechariah 3:1–4:14)** — Joshua the high priest is cleansed despite Satan's accusation, and God promises His coming Branch. A lampstand and olive trees bring encouragement to Zerubbabel: the temple will be completed not by human might but by God's Spirit, and small beginnings are not to be despised.

In the fourth vision, Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the LORD in filthy clothes. Satan stands beside him to accuse him. But “the LORD said unto Satan, The LORD rebuke thee, O Satan... is not this a brand plucked out of the fire?” A brand is a burning stick pulled out of the fire just in time. The angel commands, “Take away the filthy garments from him,” and tells Joshua, “Behold, I have caused thine iniquity to pass from thee.” They dress him in clean robes and a clean turban. God promises, “I will bring forth my servant the BRANCH,” a coming king from David's family tree, and says, “I will remove the iniquity of that land in one day.” In the fifth vision, Zechariah sees a golden lampstand fed with oil by two olive trees. The message is for Zerubbabel, who faced a mountain of problems in rebuilding the temple: “Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts.” The mountain will become flat ground, and Zerubbabel will finish the temple as the people shout, “Grace, grace unto it.” His hands laid the foundation, and “his hands shall also finish it.” Some people looked down on the small new temple, but God asks, “For who hath despised the day of small things?” The two olive trees are “the two anointed ones,” most likely Zerubbabel and Joshua.

**God Removes Wickedness from the Land (Zechariah 5:1–6:15)** — A flying scroll brings judgment on thieves and false swearers, while wickedness is carried to Shinar. Four chariots go out under God's authority, and crowns placed on Joshua point toward the Branch who will build the Lord's temple and unite royal honor with priestly peace.

In the sixth vision, Zechariah sees a huge flying scroll, thirty feet long and fifteen feet wide. It is God's curse, flying into the houses of thieves and of those who lie under oath, and it destroys them. In the seventh vision, a woman named “Wickedness” sits inside a large measuring basket. An angel shoves her down and slams a heavy lead lid on top. Then two women with wings like a stork carry the basket away to Shinar, the land of Babylon. Sin is being removed from God's land and sent back where it belongs. In the eighth vision, four chariots pulled by red, black, white, and spotted horses come out from between two bronze mountains. They are “the four spirits of the heavens,” going out over the whole earth for God. Afterward, God tells Zechariah to make a crown of silver and gold and place it on Joshua the high priest. He announces, “Behold the man whose name is The BRANCH... and he shall build the temple of the LORD.” This coming one “shall sit and rule upon his throne; and he shall be a priest upon his throne.” He will be both king and priest, two roles that were always kept separate in Israel. Christians see this fulfilled in Jesus.

**True Worship Requires Justice and Mercy (Zechariah 7:1–8:23)** — God questions whether the people's fasting was truly for Him and recalls that earlier generations rejected justice and compassion. He promises Jerusalem's restoration, truth, security, and blessing, but still commands honest judgment, truth, and care for the vulnerable.

Two years later, men come from Bethel to ask a question. For seventy years they have fasted every summer to mourn the temple's destruction. Now that it is being rebuilt, should they keep fasting? God answers with questions of His own: “When ye fasted and mourned... did ye at all fast unto me, even to me?” What He wants is the same thing the earlier prophets preached: “Execute true judgment, and shew mercy and compassions every man to his brother: And oppress not the widow, nor the fatherless, the stranger, nor the poor.” Their ancestors refused to listen and “made their hearts as an adamant stone,” as hard as diamond, so God scattered them “with a whirlwind among all the nations.” But chapter 8 is full of hope. God says, “I am returned unto Zion,” and Jerusalem “shall be called a city of truth.” He paints a peaceful picture: “There shall yet old men and old women dwell in the streets of Jerusalem... And the streets of the city shall be full of boys and girls playing.” God will bring His people home “from the east country, and from the west country.” He tells them to “Speak ye every man the truth to his neighbour,” and He promises that their sad fasts will become “joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts.” Someday people from many nations will come to Jerusalem to seek God. “Ten men... shall take hold of the skirt of him that is a Jew, saying, We will go with you: for we have heard that God is with you.”

**A Coming King Brings Peace (Zechariah 9:1–10:12)** — God announces judgment on surrounding nations and promises a righteous, humble king arriving in Jerusalem on a donkey. He will protect and gather His scattered people, strengthen Judah, and call them away from false gods and failed shepherds.

Chapter 9 begins a new section of the book, with no dates. God announces judgment on the cities around Israel, including Damascus, Tyre, Sidon, and the Philistines. Then comes one of the most famous prophecies in the Old Testament: “Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion; shout, O daughter of Jerusalem: behold, thy King cometh unto thee: he is just, and having salvation; lowly, and riding upon an ass, and upon a colt the foal of an ass.” Kings usually rode warhorses, but this King comes humbly on a donkey. Jesus fulfilled this when He rode into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday (Matthew 21:4–5; John 12:14–15). He will remove chariots and war horses, “and he shall speak peace unto the heathen: and his dominion shall be from sea even to sea.” God will save His people, and they will shine “as the stones of a crown.” In chapter 10, God tells the people to ask Him, not idols or fortune-tellers, for rain. The people had wandered like sheep “because there was no shepherd,” so God is angry with their bad leaders. He will strengthen Judah and save the house of Joseph, the northern tribes. “I will hiss for them, and gather them,” calling them home like a shepherd whistling for his flock.

**The Rejected Shepherd and the Scattered Flock (Zechariah 11:1–17)** — Through the image of a shepherd and a flock marked for slaughter, God exposes corrupt leadership and rejection of His care. Zechariah receives thirty pieces of silver, breaks staffs called Beauty and Bands, and warns of a foolish shepherd who neglects the vulnerable.

This is one of the hardest chapters in the book to understand. God tells Zechariah to act out the part of a shepherd over “the flock of the slaughter,” sheep whose owners are killing and selling them for profit. Zechariah takes two staffs, a shepherd's walking sticks. He names one Beauty, which stands for God's favor, and the other Bands, which stands for the unity of Israel and Judah. He gets rid of three bad shepherds, but the flock rejects him. So he breaks the staff called Beauty. Then he asks for his wages: “So they weighed for my price thirty pieces of silver.” Thirty pieces of silver was the price paid for a slave (Exodus 21:32), an insult. God says sarcastically, “Cast it unto the potter: a goodly price that I was prised at of them.” Zechariah throws the silver to the potter in the temple. Five hundred years later, Judas was paid thirty pieces of silver to betray Jesus. He threw the money down in the temple, and the priests used it to buy a potter's field (Matthew 27:3–10). Zechariah then breaks the second staff, Bands. Finally God warns that He will raise up a worthless shepherd who does not care for the flock: “Woe to the idol shepherd that leaveth the flock!”

**Jerusalem Faces Conflict and Receives God's Mercy (Zechariah 12:1–13:9)** — God promises to defend Jerusalem, pour out grace and supplication, and open a fountain for cleansing from sin. Idols and false prophecy will be removed; after a shepherd is struck and the flock scattered, a refined remnant will call God their own.

In a future battle, all nations will come against Jerusalem, but God will make the city “a burdensome stone for all people.” Anyone who tries to lift it will be hurt. God will defend Jerusalem so well that the weakest person there will be as strong as David. Then something surprising happens. God says, “I will pour upon the house of David, and upon the inhabitants of Jerusalem, the spirit of grace and of supplications: and they shall look upon me whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn for him, as one mourneth for his only son.” John's Gospel connects this verse to the soldier who pierced Jesus' side at the cross (John 19:37). The whole land will mourn, family by family. Then God promises, “In that day there shall be a fountain opened to the house of David and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem for sin and for uncleanness.” He will wipe out idols and false prophets from the land. Then God says, “Awake, O sword, against my shepherd... smite the shepherd, and the sheep shall be scattered.” On the night He was arrested, Jesus quoted this verse to warn His disciples that they would run away (Matthew 26:31). Only a third of the people will survive, but God will refine them like silver and gold. “They shall call on my name, and I will hear them: I will say, It is my people: and they shall say, The LORD is my God.”

**The Lord Reigns Over All the Earth (Zechariah 14:1–21)** — Zechariah closes with nations attacking Jerusalem and the Lord intervening in decisive judgment. Living waters flow from the city, the Lord is King over all the earth, nations come to worship Him, and holiness extends into ordinary life throughout Jerusalem and Judah.

The book ends with a dramatic picture of the day of the LORD. All nations attack Jerusalem, and the city is captured. Then “shall the LORD go forth, and fight against those nations.” “And his feet shall stand in that day upon the mount of Olives,” which will split in two, opening a valley for His people to escape. That day will be unlike any other: “at evening time it shall be light.” “Living waters shall go out from Jerusalem,” flowing east to the Dead Sea and west to the Mediterranean all year long. Then the great announcement: “And the LORD shall be king over all the earth: in that day shall there be one LORD, and his name one.” Jerusalem will be raised up and live in safety forever, while a terrible plague strikes its attackers. Survivors from the nations will come year after year “to worship the King, the LORD of hosts, and to keep the feast of tabernacles.” Even ordinary things will be holy. The bells on the horses will say, “HOLINESS UNTO THE LORD,” the same words once engraved on the high priest's gold headband, and every cooking pot in Jerusalem will be as holy as the temple bowls.

# Malachi
When it happened: Malachi is undated, but the temple had been rebuilt and a governor ruled Judah (Malachi 1:8). The problems he addresses match those Nehemiah faced, so it is usually placed around 450–430 BC.
When it was written: Nothing else is known about the prophet. The book is written as a series of disputes, in which God makes a statement, the people question it, and God answers (for example Malachi 1:2).
Where: Jerusalem and Judah.
The world at the time: Judah was a small province of the Persian Empire. A century after the return, the priests had grown careless, tithes were being withheld, and marriages were breaking down.

**God Reminds Israel of His Love (Malachi 1:1–5)** — To the returned community in Judah, God declares His enduring love and points to His treatment of Jacob and Esau. Israel's difficult circumstances have not erased His covenant faithfulness, while Edom's efforts to rebuild cannot overturn His judgment.

Malachi's name means “my messenger,” and his book is written like a series of arguments. God makes a statement, the people talk back, and God answers. It starts with love: “I have loved you, saith the LORD. Yet ye say, Wherein hast thou loved us?” The people had come home from exile, but life was still hard, and they doubted that God cared. God points to the twin brothers Jacob and Esau: “yet I loved Jacob, And I hated Esau.” This is a strong way of saying that God chose Jacob, not Esau, to carry His promises. Paul quotes this line in Romans 9:13. Esau's descendants, the Edomites, say they will rebuild their ruined land, but God says, “They shall build, but I will throw down.” When Israel sees it, they will say, “The LORD will be magnified from the border of Israel.”

**The Priests Dishonor God's Name (Malachi 1:6–2:9)** — God rebukes priests who accept blind, lame, and sick sacrifices and treat worship as a burden. They have abandoned the covenant responsibilities associated with Levi, failed to teach truth, caused people to stumble, and shown favoritism in applying the law.

God confronts the priests: “A son honoureth his father, and a servant his master: if then I be a father, where is mine honour?” The priests ask, “Wherein have we despised thy name?” God answers that they offer blind, lame, and sick animals as sacrifices. He dares them, “offer it now unto thy governor; will he be pleased with thee?” They would never give a sick animal as a gift to a human ruler, yet they give it to God. They even complain, “Behold, what a weariness is it!” God would rather someone shut the temple doors than keep up such worthless worship. Yet one day, “from the rising of the sun even unto the going down of the same my name shall be great among the Gentiles.” He reminds the priests of Levi, the father of the priestly tribe, who once served faithfully: “The law of truth was in his mouth... and did turn many away from iniquity.” “For the priest's lips should keep knowledge,” God says, “for he is the messenger of the LORD of hosts.” But these priests have “caused many to stumble at the law,” and so God has made them “contemptible and base before all the people.”

**Judah Breaks Faith with God and Family (Malachi 2:10–17)** — Judah profanes its covenant through marriages joined to foreign worship and through treachery against wives. God rejects tears at the altar that conceal unfaithfulness and confronts people who question whether He will ever judge evil.

“Have we not all one father? hath not one God created us? why do we deal treacherously every man against his brother?” Men in Judah have married women who worship other gods. Others have divorced their Jewish wives to do it. Then they come to the temple weeping, wondering why God will not accept their offerings. God says it is “Because the LORD hath been witness between thee and the wife of thy youth, against whom thou hast dealt treacherously: yet is she thy companion, and the wife of thy covenant.” Marriage is a covenant, a sacred promise made before God. “The LORD, the God of Israel, saith that he hateth putting away,” which means divorce. So He warns twice, “take heed to your spirit, that ye deal not treacherously.” The section ends with another complaint. The people have “wearied the LORD” by saying that God is pleased with evildoers, and by asking, “Where is the God of judgment?”

**The Lord Promises a Coming Messenger (Malachi 3:1–6)** — God promises a messenger who will prepare the way before the Lord comes to His temple. His coming will purify the priesthood and expose injustice, adultery, false oaths, exploitation, and oppression, proving that God's patience is not approval of sin.

God answers their question about justice: “Behold, I will send my messenger, and he shall prepare the way before me: and the Lord, whom ye seek, shall suddenly come to his temple.” The Gospels say this messenger was John the Baptist, who prepared the way for Jesus (Mark 1:2; Luke 7:27). But His coming will not be easy: “who may abide the day of his coming? and who shall stand when he appeareth? for he is like a refiner's fire, and like fullers' soap.” A refiner melts metal to burn away impurities, and a fuller uses strong soap to clean cloth. God will purify the priests “as gold and silver,” so their offerings will please Him again. He will also be “a swift witness” against sorcerers, adulterers, liars, and those who cheat workers out of their pay, mistreat widows and orphans, and turn away foreigners. Then He gives the reason Israel still exists: “For I am the LORD, I change not; therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed.”

**God Calls His People to Return Through Faithfulness (Malachi 3:7–12)** — God calls Judah to return by bringing the required tithes and offerings that it has withheld. Within Israel's covenant, He promises blessing, protected crops, and a land recognized by other nations as blessed when the people obey.

“Return unto me, and I will return unto you, saith the LORD of hosts.” The people ask, “Wherein shall we return?” God gives a startling answer: “Will a man rob God? Yet ye have robbed me. But ye say, Wherein have we robbed thee? In tithes and offerings.” A tithe was a tenth of their harvest and income, which the law commanded them to give to support the temple and the Levites. Then God makes a challenge He makes nowhere else: “Bring ye all the tithes into the storehouse, that there may be meat in mine house, and prove me now herewith, saith the LORD of hosts, if I will not open you the windows of heaven, and pour you out a blessing, that there shall not be room enough to receive it.” He will stop the locusts from destroying their crops. “And all nations shall call you blessed: for ye shall be a delightsome land.”

**God Remembers Those Who Fear Him (Malachi 3:13–18)** — While some claim that serving God has no benefit because the wicked prosper, those who fear the Lord speak together and are remembered. God calls them His treasured possession and promises that the distinction between righteous and wicked will become clear.

Some people complain, “It is vain to serve God: and what profit is it that we have kept his ordinance?” They see proud and wicked people doing well and decide that obeying God is pointless. But not everyone feels that way. “Then they that feared the LORD spake often one to another: and the LORD hearkened, and heard it, and a book of remembrance was written before him for them that feared the LORD.” God has not forgotten the faithful. “And they shall be mine, saith the LORD of hosts, in that day when I make up my jewels,” His special treasure. A day is coming when everyone will clearly see the difference “between the righteous and the wicked, between him that serveth God and him that serveth him not.”

**The Coming Day of Judgment and Healing (Malachi 4:1–6)** — Malachi ends with judgment for the proud and wicked, healing for those who fear the Lord, and a call to remember Moses's law. God also promises Elijah before the great day of the Lord, calling hearts toward reconciliation and warning of a curse without turning.

“For, behold, the day cometh, that shall burn as an oven; and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly, shall be stubble.” The fire will leave them “neither root nor branch.” But for those who honor God, the same day will be like a sunrise: “But unto you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings; and ye shall go forth, and grow up as calves of the stall,” jumping like young calves let out of the barn. Malachi tells the people to remember the law God gave Moses. Then come the last words of the Old Testament: “Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD: And he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their fathers, lest I come and smite the earth with a curse.” After this, no prophet speaks in Israel for about four hundred years. Then John the Baptist appears in the wilderness, and Jesus says of him, “this is Elias, which was for to come” (Matthew 11:14). Elias is the Greek form of the name Elijah.
