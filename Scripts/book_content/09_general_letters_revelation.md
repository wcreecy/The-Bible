# Hebrews
When it happened: Most likely written before the temple in Jerusalem was destroyed in AD 70, since it speaks of the priests' sacrifices as still being offered (Hebrews 8:4; 10:1–2)—probably in the AD 60s.
When it was written: The writer knows "our brother Timothy" (Hebrews 13:23). Suggested authors include Paul, Barnabas, and Apollos; the KJV's traditional title credits Paul. The writer calls it "the word of exhortation" (Hebrews 13:22), and it reads more like a sermon than a letter.
Where: Written to Jewish believers whose location is not stated; the writer sends greetings from "They of Italy" (Hebrews 13:24).
The world at the time: The readers had already endured persecution and the loss of their possessions (Hebrews 10:32–34). Under continuing pressure, some were in danger of drifting away and turning back to the old covenant's sacrifices.

**Hebrews 1-2** — "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son." The Son is the brightness of God's glory; through him God made the worlds, and after he had purged our sins he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high. He is greater than the angels. Yet he was made a little lower than the angels and shared our flesh and blood, so that through death he might destroy the devil and become a merciful and faithful high priest.

**Hebrews 3-4** — Jesus is greater than Moses, as a son over a house is greater than a servant in it. The writer warns his readers not to harden their hearts as Israel did in the wilderness, but to enter God's rest. "For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword." Because we have a great high priest who "was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin," we can "come boldly unto the throne of grace."

**Hebrews 5-7** — Christ is a priest for ever "after the order of Melchisedec," the priest-king who blessed Abraham. The writer warns against falling away and urges his readers on to maturity, anchored by God's sure promise to Abraham. Unlike the Levitical priests, who died, Christ "ever liveth to make intercession."

**Hebrews 8-10** — Christ serves in the true tabernacle and is the mediator of the new covenant that Jeremiah foretold. The old sacrifices were offered again and again, but Christ entered the holy place once "by his own blood," and "without shedding of blood is no remission." "By the which will we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all." So they should draw near with a true heart, hold fast without wavering, and not forsake "the assembling of ourselves together." They had once endured persecution and joyfully accepted the loss of their goods; they must not throw away their confidence now.

**Hebrews 11** — "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." The writer recalls the faithful: Abel, Enoch, and Noah; Abraham, who went out "not knowing whither he went"; Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Rahab, and many more, who "all died in faith, not having received the promises." "But without faith it is impossible to please him."

**Hebrews 12-13** — Surrounded by "so great a cloud of witnesses," believers should lay aside every weight "and let us run with patience the race that is set before us, Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith." God disciplines those he loves. The letter closes with practical instruction—brotherly love, hospitality, faithfulness in marriage, contentment—and the reminder that "Jesus Christ the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever." It sends word that "our brother Timothy is set at liberty."

# James
When it happened: Possibly one of the earliest New Testament writings, from the AD 40s to early 60s. According to the Jewish historian Josephus, James was put to death around AD 62.
When it was written: James writes to "the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad" (James 1:1), likely Jewish believers living outside the land of Israel.
Where: Sent "to the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad" (James 1:1)—Jewish believers living outside the land of Israel.
The world at the time: The readers were facing trials, and the rich were oppressing the poor, even dragging them into court (James 2:6; 5:1–6). Some assemblies were showing favoritism to wealthy visitors.

**James 1** — "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ," wrote to the twelve tribes scattered abroad: "My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience." Anyone who lacks wisdom should ask God, who gives generously, and every good and perfect gift comes from above. "Let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath," and "be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only." Pure religion is to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction and to keep oneself unspotted from the world.

**James 2** — James warned against honoring a rich man with a gold ring while sending a poor man in shabby clothes to sit on the floor. Loving one's neighbour is "the royal law." Faith without works is dead: if a brother or sister has no clothes or food and you only say, "be ye warmed and filled," what good is it? Abraham and Rahab showed their faith by what they did. "For as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also."

**James 3-4** — The tongue is small, like a ship's helm or a spark that sets a great forest on fire, and it "can no man tame." The wisdom from above is "first pure, then peaceable, gentle." Fighting comes from selfish desires. "Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you." No one should boast about tomorrow: "For what is your life? It is even a vapour."

**James 5** — James warned the rich who had held back their workers' wages. Believers should be patient until the coming of the Lord, as a farmer waits for the rain, remembering the patience of Job. The sick should call for the elders to pray over them and anoint them, for "The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much," as Elijah's prayers stopped and then restored the rain. Whoever turns a sinner from his error saves a soul from death.

# 1 Peter
When it happened: Likely written in the early AD 60s, before Peter's death in Rome under the emperor Nero, traditionally around AD 64–68.
When it was written: Silvanus helped write it (1 Peter 5:12). Peter sends greetings from "Babylon" (1 Peter 5:13), probably a name for Rome.
Where: Sent "to the strangers scattered throughout Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1 Peter 1:1)—across what is now northern and western Turkey.
The world at the time: Believers were being slandered and mistreated by their neighbors because of their faith in Christ (1 Peter 4:12–16), and lived as outsiders in their own communities.

**1 Peter 1-2** — "Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ," wrote to believers living as strangers across Asia Minor. He blessed God, who "hath begotten us again unto a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, To an inheritance incorruptible." Though for a season they suffered trials, their tested faith was more precious than gold. They had been redeemed "with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish," and were called to be holy. They were "a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people," living stones built into a spiritual house.

**1 Peter 2-3** — As strangers and pilgrims, they were to live honorably among unbelievers, submit to rulers, and honour the king. Servants who suffered unjustly could look to Christ, "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree... by whose stripes ye were healed." Peter gave instruction to wives and husbands, urged everyone not to repay evil for evil, and to "be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear."

**1 Peter 4-5** — They should not be surprised at the fiery trial, but rejoice to share in Christ's sufferings, and above all love one another, "for charity shall cover the multitude of sins." As a fellow elder, Peter told the elders to feed the flock willingly, and told everyone to be clothed with humility, "Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you." "Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour." He wrote by Silvanus and sent greetings from the church at Babylon and from Marcus, his son.

# 2 Peter
When it happened: Written shortly before Peter's death, which he knew was near (2 Peter 1:14)—traditionally in the mid-AD 60s.
When it was written: Peter calls it his second letter to the same readers (2 Peter 3:1), and it speaks of Paul's letters as already known (2 Peter 3:15–16).
Where: Sent to those who "have obtained like precious faith" (2 Peter 1:1), likely the same believers in Asia Minor as 1 Peter.
The world at the time: False teachers were exploiting the churches, and scoffers were mocking the promise of Christ's return because time had passed and nothing seemed to change (2 Peter 3:3–4).

**2 Peter 1** — "Simon Peter, a servant and an apostle of Jesus Christ," wrote knowing that he must soon put off this earthly tabernacle, "even as our Lord Jesus Christ hath shewed me." God's power has given us "all things that pertain unto life and godliness," so believers should add to their faith virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, godliness, brotherly kindness, and charity. Peter reminded them that he had not followed "cunningly devised fables," but had seen Christ's majesty with his own eyes on the holy mount, when the voice from heaven said, "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." True prophecy came as "holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost."

**2 Peter 2** — False teachers would arise among them, bringing in destructive heresies and exploiting people with deceptive words. Just as God judged the angels who sinned, the ancient world in the flood, and Sodom and Gomorrah, while rescuing Noah and Lot, he knows how to deliver the godly and to keep the unjust for the day of judgment.

**2 Peter 3** — In the last days scoffers would ask, "Where is the promise of his coming?" Peter answered that "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years," and that the Lord is "not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance." The day of the Lord will come as a thief, and the heavens will pass away, but "we, according to his promise, look for new heavens and a new earth, wherein dwelleth righteousness." He noted that in the letters of "our beloved brother Paul" some things are hard to understand, and closed: "But grow in grace, and in the knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ."

# 1 John
When it happened: Likely written around AD 85–95.
When it was written: The writer speaks as an eyewitness of Jesus (1 John 1:1–3). Its style and themes closely match John's Gospel.
Where: Probably sent to churches in and around Ephesus.
The world at the time: A group had left the fellowship (1 John 2:19) and was teaching that Jesus Christ had not truly come in the flesh (1 John 4:2–3). John wrote to reassure the believers who remained.

**1 John 1-2** — The writer begins with what "we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes... and our hands have handled, of the Word of life," so that his readers may share fellowship with them and with the Father and his Son. "God is light, and in him is no darkness at all." "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins," and if anyone sins, "we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous." Those who know God keep his commandments and love their brother. Some had gone out from them, "but they were not of us"; they denied that Jesus is the Christ.

**1 John 3-4** — "Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God." God's children do not keep on sinning; they love one another, not "in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth." They must test the spirits: "Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God." "Beloved, let us love one another: for love is of God... for God is love." "Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins." "There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear."

**1 John 5** — Whoever believes that Jesus is the Christ is born of God, "and this is the victory that overcometh the world, even our faith." "God hath given to us eternal life, and this life is in his Son." John wrote these things "that ye may know that ye have eternal life," and closed, "Little children, keep yourselves from idols."

# 2 John
When it happened: Written in the same period as 1 John, around AD 85–95.
When it was written: A short letter from "The elder" to "the elect lady and her children" (2 John 1:1), probably a church and its members.
Where: Sent "unto the elect lady and her children" (2 John 1:1)—either a particular woman and her family or, as many think, a local church and its members.
The world at the time: Travelling teachers depended on believers' hospitality, and some of them were deceivers who denied that Jesus Christ had come in the flesh.

**2 John 1:1-6** — "The elder unto the elect lady and her children, whom I love in the truth." He rejoiced to find some of her children walking in truth, and reminded her of the commandment they had heard from the beginning, "that we love one another." "And this is love, that we walk after his commandments."

**2 John 1:7-13** — "For many deceivers are entered into the world, who confess not that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh." She was not to welcome such a teacher into her house or wish him well. The elder had much more to write but hoped to visit and speak face to face, "that our joy may be full."

# 3 John
When it happened: Written in the same period as 1 and 2 John, around AD 85–95.
When it was written: A personal letter from "The elder" to his friend Gaius (3 John 1:1) about welcoming travelling teachers.
Where: A local church where Gaius was a member and where Diotrephes had taken control.
The world at the time: Missionaries travelling from church to church relied on believers to house and support them, and welcoming them was a way of sharing in their work.

**3 John 1:1-8** — "The elder unto the wellbeloved Gaius, whom I love in the truth." He wished Gaius good health, "even as thy soul prospereth," and rejoiced to hear that he walked in the truth: "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth." He praised Gaius for faithfully welcoming travelling brethren who had gone out for the Lord's name, so "that we might be fellowhelpers to the truth."

**3 John 1:9-14** — But Diotrephes, "who loveth to have the preeminence," refused to receive the elder or the brethren and put out of the church those who did. "Follow not that which is evil, but that which is good." Demetrius had a good report from everyone. The elder hoped to see Gaius soon and speak face to face.

# Jude
When it happened: Likely written between the AD 60s and 80s.
When it was written: Jude quotes a prophecy of Enoch (Jude 1:14–15) found in the book of 1 Enoch, a Jewish writing outside the Bible.
Where: The readers are not identified beyond "them that are sanctified by God the Father" (Jude 1:1).
The world at the time: Ungodly men had slipped into the churches, using God's grace as an excuse for immorality. Jude's warning closely parallels 2 Peter 2.

**Jude 1:1-4** — "Jude, the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James," had planned to write about their common salvation, but felt he must urge them to "earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints." Certain ungodly men had crept in unnoticed, "turning the grace of our God into lasciviousness, and denying the only Lord God, and our Lord Jesus Christ."

**Jude 1:5-16** — Jude reminded them how the Lord destroyed the unbelieving Israelites after saving them from Egypt, kept the angels who left their first estate in chains, and judged Sodom and Gomorrah. He compared the false teachers to Cain, Balaam, and Core, called them "clouds they are without water" and "wandering stars," and recalled Enoch's prophecy that the Lord would come with ten thousands of his saints to execute judgment.

**Jude 1:17-25** — Believers should remember the apostles' warnings, build themselves up in their most holy faith, pray in the Holy Ghost, "Keep yourselves in the love of God," and show compassion to others. Jude closed with praise: "Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy, To the only wise God our Saviour, be glory and majesty, dominion and power, both now and ever. Amen."

# Revelation
When it happened: John was on the island of Patmos "for the word of God, and for the testimony of Jesus Christ" (Revelation 1:9). Early church tradition dates the book near the end of the emperor Domitian's reign, around AD 95; some scholars prefer the late AD 60s.
When it was written: It is written as a letter to seven churches in the province of Asia, recording visions John was told to write down: "What thou seest, write in a book, and send it unto the seven churches" (Revelation 1:11).
Where: The island of Patmos in the Aegean Sea, writing to seven churches in the Roman province of Asia: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamos, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, and Laodicea.
The world at the time: Believers in the Roman Empire faced persecution—Antipas had already been killed at Pergamos (Revelation 2:13)—and pressure to compromise with the surrounding culture and its worship.

**Revelation 1** — "The Revelation of Jesus Christ" was given to his servant John, who was on the island of Patmos. On the Lord's day John heard behind him a great voice like a trumpet and turned to see one like the Son of man among seven golden candlesticks, with eyes like a flame of fire and a face shining like the sun. John fell at his feet as dead, but he laid his right hand on John and said, "Fear not; I am the first and the last: I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore."

**Revelation 2-3** — Christ sent messages to seven churches in Asia. Ephesus had left its first love. Smyrna would suffer: "be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life." Pergamos and Thyatira tolerated false teaching. Sardis had a name for being alive but was dead. Philadelphia had kept his word. Laodicea was lukewarm: "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him." Each message held a promise "To him that overcometh."

**Revelation 4-5** — A door opened in heaven, and John saw a throne encircled by a rainbow, with twenty-four elders and four living creatures crying, "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty, which was, and is, and is to come." No one was found worthy to open the sealed book in the hand of the one on the throne—until a Lamb, as it had been slain, came and took it, and all heaven sang, "Worthy is the Lamb that was slain."

**Revelation 6-11** — As the Lamb opened the seals, four horsemen rode out, martyrs cried out from under the altar, and the sky was shaken. One hundred forty-four thousand from the tribes of Israel were sealed, and John saw "a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations" standing before the throne. Seven angels sounded seven trumpets, bringing judgments on the earth, the sea, the rivers, and the sky. Two witnesses prophesied, were killed, and rose again. At the seventh trumpet, great voices in heaven declared, "The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ; and he shall reign for ever and ever."

**Revelation 12-14** — John saw a woman clothed with the sun giving birth to a son, and a great red dragon, "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan," who was cast out of heaven and made war with her offspring. A beast rose out of the sea and another out of the earth, demanding worship and forcing people to take the mark of the beast, whose number is six hundred threescore and six. But the Lamb stood on mount Sion with the 144,000, and angels proclaimed the everlasting gospel and the fall of Babylon.

**Revelation 15-18** — Seven angels poured out seven vials of the wrath of God. John saw the great harlot, Babylon, drunk with the blood of the saints, riding on a scarlet beast. "Babylon the great is fallen," and the kings, merchants, and sailors of the earth mourned her sudden destruction.

**Revelation 19-20** — Heaven rejoiced, "Alleluia: for the Lord God omnipotent reigneth," and announced the marriage supper of the Lamb. Heaven opened, and a rider on a white horse, called Faithful and True, with the name "KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS," defeated the beast and the false prophet. Satan was bound for a thousand years, released for a little season, and then cast into the lake of fire. The dead, small and great, stood before a great white throne and were judged.

**Revelation 21-22** — John saw "a new heaven and a new earth," and the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God. "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying." The city had walls of jasper, gates of pearl, and a street of gold; it needed no sun, "for the glory of God did lighten it, and the Lamb is the light thereof." A pure river of water of life flowed from the throne, with the tree of life on either side. "And the Spirit and the bride say, Come." The Bible closes with Christ's promise, "Surely I come quickly," and John's answer, "Even so, come, Lord Jesus."
