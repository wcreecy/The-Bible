# Isaiah
When it happened: Isaiah prophesied "in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah" (Isaiah 1:1), roughly 740–700 BC. Chapters 40–66 speak to the people in Babylonian exile and name Cyrus of Persia (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1).
When it was written: Many scholars think chapters 40–66 come from later prophets in Isaiah's tradition during and after the exile; others hold that the whole book comes from Isaiah himself.
Where: Jerusalem and Judah, with prophecies concerning Assyria, Babylon, Egypt, Moab, Tyre, and other nations.
The world at the time: The Assyrian Empire was expanding westward. Syria and the northern kingdom of Israel attacked Judah under King Ahaz (Isaiah 7), Assyria destroyed Samaria in 722 BC, and in 701 BC Sennacherib invaded Judah and surrounded Jerusalem.

**God Calls Judah to Repent and Return (Isaiah 1:1–5:30)** — Isaiah warns Judah that religious ceremonies cannot replace obedience. God calls his people to reject injustice, defend the vulnerable, and return to him, while promising forgiveness and warning that pride, idolatry, oppression, and corrupt leadership will bring judgment.

Isaiah was a prophet in Jerusalem for about forty years, during the reigns of four kings of Judah. He opens his book like a courtroom, calling heaven and earth as witnesses. God has raised children, and they have rebelled against him. Even animals know better: “The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel doth not know.”

The people still offer sacrifices and keep the feasts, but God is sick of it, because their hands are full of blood. he tells them what he really wants: “learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.” Then he makes an amazing offer: “Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow.”

Isaiah looks ahead to a day when all nations will stream to God's house in Jerusalem to learn his ways. “They shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” Micah records almost the same words.

But first, judgment must come on proud Jerusalem. In chapter 5, Isaiah sings a sad song about a vineyard. The owner did everything to care for it, but it grew only wild, sour grapes. The vineyard is Israel. Then come six warnings that begin with “Woe,” against greed, drunkenness, and twisted values: “Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light, and light for darkness.”

**Isaiah Sees God's Holiness and Receives His Calling (Isaiah 6:1–13)** — Isaiah sees the Lord enthroned in holiness, confesses his uncleanness, receives cleansing from the altar, and accepts a difficult mission to a people who will largely refuse to understand. Judgment will leave the land desolate, but a holy remnant will remain.

Isaiah tells how God called him. “In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple.” Above him stand seraphim, fiery angels with six wings. They call to one another, “Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory.” The doorposts shake, and the temple fills with smoke.

Isaiah is terrified. “Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips: for mine eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts.” Then one of the seraphim flies to him with a burning coal from the altar. He touches Isaiah's mouth with it and says that his sin is taken away.

Then Isaiah hears God's voice: “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” Isaiah answers, “Here am I; send me.” But his mission will be hard. The people will hear but not understand. They will keep refusing until the cities lie empty and the land is ruined. Even so, like a tree that has been cut down, a stump will remain, and from it a holy seed will grow.

**God Promises a Sign During Judah's Crisis (Isaiah 7:1–9:7)** — During threats from Syria and northern Israel, Isaiah calls Ahaz to trust God. He announces Immanuel as a sign, warns of Assyria, and points to a future child and righteous ruler whose reign will bring light, justice, and peace.

Syria and the northern kingdom of Israel join forces to attack Jerusalem. When King Ahaz hears it, his heart shakes “as the trees of the wood are moved with the wind.” God sends Isaiah to meet the king, along with Isaiah's son Shearjashub, whose name means a remnant shall return. Isaiah tells Ahaz not to fear these two kings, who are only “smoking firebrands,” sticks that are almost burned out. But he warns, “If ye will not believe, surely ye shall not be established.”

God offers Ahaz any sign he wants. Ahaz refuses, pretending to be humble, because he has already decided to trust Assyria instead of God. So God gives a sign anyway: “Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel.” Immanuel means God with us. Before this child is old enough to know right from wrong, the two enemy kings will be gone. Matthew later says this promise came true in the birth of Jesus. Isaiah also names his own newborn son Mahershalalhashbaz, meaning quick to the plunder, as a sign that Assyria will soon conquer Damascus and Samaria.

Isaiah warns that Assyria will flood into Judah too. But after the darkness comes a great light: “The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light.” Then comes a famous promise: “For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.” He will sit on David's throne, and his kingdom will never end.

**Israel's Pride and Assyria's Arrogance Face Judgment (Isaiah 9:8–10:34)** — God condemns northern Israel's pride, injustice, and persistent rebellion. Assyria will be used as an instrument of judgment, but its king's cruelty and arrogance will also be judged; a remnant will return to the Lord.

God sends warning after warning to the northern kingdom of Israel. They are proud and say they will rebuild with stronger materials. Their leaders mislead the people, and their judges make unfair laws to rob the poor and the widows. After each warning, the same sad refrain repeats: “For all this his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still.”

Then God speaks to Assyria, the great empire that is attacking nation after nation. “O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger.” God is using Assyria like a stick to punish his people. But the king of Assyria does not know this. He thinks he is winning by his own power and boasts that no one can stop him. God asks, “Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith?” An axe cannot brag against the one who swings it. So God will cut Assyria down like a forest. And he promises that “The remnant shall return,” a small group of Israel who will truly trust the LORD.

**A Righteous King Brings Justice and Peace (Isaiah 11:1–12:6)** — A shoot from Jesse's family will rule through the Spirit of the Lord, judging with righteousness and defending the oppressed. Isaiah pictures peace, the gathering of scattered Israel and Judah, and joyful praise for God's salvation.

After the forest of Assyria is cut down, Isaiah describes a new shoot growing from a stump. “And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots.” Jesse was King David's father, so this is a promise of a king from David's family. “And the spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might.” He will judge the poor fairly and defend the weak.

His kingdom will bring peace even among animals: “The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid,” “and a little child shall lead them.” No one will hurt or destroy, “for the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea.” God will gather his scattered people from every corner of the earth.

Chapter 12 is a short song of praise for that day. “Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust, and not be afraid.” “Therefore with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation.”

**Babylon and Other Nations Receive Warnings of Judgment (Isaiah 13:1–17:14)** — Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, and northern Israel receive warnings that pride, cruelty, and reliance on earthly power cannot escape God's judgment. Even so, a remnant will turn toward its Maker.

Now Isaiah delivers messages against the nations around Israel. First comes Babylon, which would one day destroy Jerusalem. Isaiah says the Medes will conquer it, and it will become like Sodom and Gomorrah, a ruin where only wild animals live.

In chapter 14, Isaiah sings a mocking song about the king of Babylon. When he dies, the kings already in the grave rise up to greet him: “Art thou also become weak as we?” Isaiah cries, “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” The king had said in his heart, “I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God.” “I will be like the most High.” But he is brought down to the pit. Many Christians have also seen in these words a picture of Satan's fall.

Then Isaiah announces that God will break Assyria in his own land. He warns the Philistines not to celebrate, and he weeps over the destruction of Moab. Damascus, the capital of Syria, will become a heap of ruins, and Israel's northern kingdom will be stripped like an olive tree after harvest, with only a few olives left at the top.

**God's Authority Reaches Every Nation (Isaiah 18:1–23:18)** — Messages to Ethiopia, Egypt, Babylon, Arabia, Jerusalem, and Tyre show that wealth, trade, military power, and national pride remain under God's authority. Egypt and Assyria are ultimately pictured sharing God's blessing with Israel.

Isaiah's messages reach even farther. He speaks to Ethiopia, a land of tall people beyond the rivers, and says they too will one day bring gifts to the LORD at Mount Zion. Egypt will fall into confusion and civil war, and its river Nile will dry up. But then comes a surprising promise. One day Egypt and Assyria, Israel's two great enemies, will worship the LORD together with Israel. God will say, “Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel mine inheritance.”

To warn Judah not to trust Egypt, God tells Isaiah to walk around barefoot and wearing only a loincloth for three years. It is a sign that Assyria will lead Egyptian captives away the same way.

Isaiah announces that Babylon will fall: “Babylon is fallen, is fallen.” He warns Edom, Arabia, and even Jerusalem, whose people are partying instead of repenting, saying, “let us eat and drink; for to morrow we shall die.” He condemns Shebna, a proud palace official, and gives his position to a faithful man, Eliakim. Finally, he mourns over Tyre, the rich trading city on the coast, whose ships will wail when it falls.

**The Whole Earth Faces Judgment and Restoration (Isaiah 24:1–27:13)** — Isaiah expands the vision to worldwide judgment for human unfaithfulness, while also promising praise, salvation, victory over death, peace for those who trust God, and the gathering of scattered Israelites to worship in Jerusalem.

These chapters widen the view to the whole world. “Behold, the LORD maketh the earth empty, and maketh it waste.” The earth has been polluted by its people, because they broke God's laws and his everlasting covenant. Judgment will fall on everyone, rich and poor, priest and people alike.

But after the judgment comes a great feast. On Mount Zion, God will prepare “a feast of fat things” for all peoples. “He will swallow up death in victory; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from off all faces.” Paul and the book of Revelation both repeat these words. The people will say, “Lo, this is our God; we have waited for him, and he will save us.”

In chapter 26, God's people sing about trusting him: “Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee.” They even hope for resurrection: “Thy dead men shall live, together with my dead body shall they arise.” God will defeat leviathan, the great sea monster that stands for evil. And a great trumpet will sound to call his scattered people home to worship at Jerusalem.

**Judah Is Warned Against False Security (Isaiah 28:1–31:9)** — Isaiah rebukes drunken leaders, hypocritical worship, and Judah's trust in alliances with Egypt. God offers Zion a sure foundation and calls his people to return, rest, and trust him rather than horses, chariots, and political schemes.

Isaiah warns the leaders of Israel and Judah that they are trusting the wrong things. The leaders of the north are drunk and proud. The rulers of Jerusalem think they have made a deal that will protect them from death. But God says He will lay a new foundation in Zion: “Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation: he that believeth shall not make haste.” The New Testament applies this stone to Christ.

Isaiah grieves that the people's worship is empty: “this people draw near me with their mouth, and with their lips do honour me, but have removed their heart far from me.” Jesus later quoted these words to the Pharisees. They also try to hide their plans from God, as if the clay could tell the potter that he did not make it.

The leaders of Judah are sending messengers to Egypt for military help against Assyria. Isaiah calls them “rebellious children” who make plans without asking God. Egypt will be useless. Instead, God says, “In returning and rest shall ye be saved; in quietness and in confidence shall be your strength.” But they refuse. Still, God waits to be gracious to them, and He promises, “This is the way, walk ye in it.” God himself will defend Jerusalem “as birds flying,” and Assyria will fall by a sword that is not human.

**God Promises Righteous Rule and Renewal (Isaiah 32:1–35:10)** — A righteous king and just leaders will bring protection and clarity. After warnings to the complacent and judgment on hostile powers, Isaiah promises the Spirit's renewal, healing, a highway of holiness, and joyful return for the redeemed.

Isaiah describes a righteous king and his officials who will protect the people like “the shadow of a great rock in a weary land.” Fools will no longer be called noble. But first, the careless, comfortable women of Jerusalem must mourn, until the Spirit is poured out from on high and the desert becomes a fruitful field.

In chapter 33, the people pray, “O LORD, be gracious unto us; we have waited for thee.” Isaiah asks who can live near God's consuming fire. The answer is the person who walks rightly and refuses bribes. “Thine eyes shall see the king in his beauty.” Chapter 34 describes God's fierce judgment on Edom, which becomes a wasteland.

Then chapter 35 bursts into joy. The desert will bloom: “the desert shall rejoice, and blossom as the rose.” Isaiah encourages the weak and fearful: “Say to them that are of a fearful heart, Be strong, fear not: behold, your God will come.” When He comes, “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped. Then shall the lame man leap as an hart.” Jesus did these very things in his ministry. A highway called “The way of holiness” will run through the desert, and “the ransomed of the LORD shall return, and come to Zion with songs and everlasting joy upon their heads.”

**Hezekiah Trusts God During Assyria's Invasion (Isaiah 36:1–37:38)** — When Sennacherib threatens Jerusalem, Hezekiah seeks the Lord and asks Isaiah to pray. God promises that Assyria will not capture the city; the angel of the Lord strikes the invading camp, and Sennacherib returns to Nineveh.

These chapters tell a true story, also found in 2 Kings. In Hezekiah's fourteenth year, Sennacherib, king of Assyria, captures the walled cities of Judah. He sends his commander, the Rabshakeh, with a large army to Jerusalem. Standing outside the city wall, the Rabshakeh shouts in Hebrew so all the people can hear. He mocks their hope in Egypt and in the LORD, and he says, “Let not Hezekiah deceive you.” No god of any nation has ever saved its people from Assyria, so why should Judah's God be any different?

Hezekiah tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth, and goes into the temple. He sends officials to Isaiah, who answers, “Be not afraid of the words that thou hast heard.” Then Sennacherib sends Hezekiah a threatening letter. Hezekiah takes it into the temple and spreads it out before the LORD. He prays, “O LORD of hosts, God of Israel... thou art the God, even thou alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth.” He asks God to save them, “that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that thou art the LORD, even thou only.”

Isaiah sends God's answer. The king of Assyria has insulted the Holy One of Israel, so God will put a hook in his nose and turn him back the way he came. he will not shoot one arrow into Jerusalem. “For I will defend this city to save it for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake.” That night, the angel of the LORD strikes the Assyrian camp, and 185,000 soldiers are found dead in the morning. Sennacherib goes home to Nineveh, where his own sons kill him while he worships in the temple of his god.

**Hezekiah Receives Healing and a Warning (Isaiah 38:1–39:8)** — God extends Hezekiah's life by fifteen years after his prayer and gives a confirming sign. After Hezekiah shows Babylonian envoys his treasures, Isaiah warns that Judah's wealth and some of its descendants will one day be taken to Babylon.

Hezekiah becomes sick and is about to die. Isaiah tells him, “Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live.” Hezekiah turns his face to the wall, prays, and weeps bitterly. Then God sends Isaiah back with a new message: “I have heard thy prayer, I have seen thy tears: behold, I will add unto thy days fifteen years.” As a sign, God makes the shadow on the stairway go back ten steps. Hezekiah writes a song of thanks. He describes how he felt as death came near, like a tent being taken down. Then he thanks God: “thou hast cast all my sins behind thy back.”

Soon after, the king of Babylon sends messengers with letters and a gift, because he has heard Hezekiah was sick. Hezekiah is flattered and shows them everything in his treasure house and his armory. Isaiah asks what the visitors saw. Hezekiah answers, “All that is in mine house have they seen.” Isaiah warns that a day is coming when everything in the palace will be carried away to Babylon, and some of Hezekiah's own descendants will serve in the Babylonian king's palace. Hezekiah answers that the word is good, because at least there will be peace in his own lifetime.

**God Comforts His People and Reveals His Greatness (Isaiah 40:1–42:25)** — God announces comfort after captivity, reveals his unmatched power, and promises to shepherd his people. he introduces a servant who will establish justice and be a light to the nations, while confronting Israel's blindness and disobedience.

Chapter 40 begins a new part of Isaiah. It looks ahead to the time when Judah will be in exile in Babylon, and it is full of comfort. “Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God.” Jerusalem's time of punishment is over. A voice calls out, “The voice of him that crieth in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the LORD.” All four Gospels say John the Baptist was this voice. People are like grass that withers, “but the word of our God shall stand for ever.” God will care for his people “like a shepherd: he shall gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom.”

Isaiah then describes how great God is. he has measured the oceans in the palm of his hand. The nations are like a drop in a bucket to him. No idol can compare to him. “Hast thou not known? hast thou not heard, that the everlasting God, the LORD, the Creator of the ends of the earth, fainteth not, neither is weary?” he gives strength to the weak: “But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint.”

God tells Israel, “Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God.” he challenges the idols to predict the future, and they cannot. Then he introduces his servant: “Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth; I have put my spirit upon him.” The servant will bring justice to the nations gently: “A bruised reed shall he not break.” Matthew says this describes Jesus.

**God Promises Redemption and the End of Captivity (Isaiah 43:1–45:25)** — God promises to redeem Israel, make a way through the wilderness, and blot out sin. he exposes idols as powerless and names Cyrus as the ruler appointed to restore Jerusalem and the temple, declaring that he alone is God and Savior.

God speaks tenderly to his people: “Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine. When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee.” he will bring them home from every direction. he tells them, “Remember ye not the former things... Behold, I will do a new thing.” And he promises forgiveness: “I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake.”

Isaiah then mocks people who make idols. A man cuts down a tree. He burns half of it to cook his food and warm himself. Then he carves the other half into a god and bows down to it, saying, “Deliver me; for thou art my god.” He does not stop to think how foolish it is. But the LORD says, “I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God.”

Then Isaiah does something remarkable. He names the king who will set the exiles free, about 150 years before he lived. God says of Cyrus, the future king of Persia, “He is my shepherd,” and He will say to Jerusalem, “Thou shalt be built.” God calls Cyrus “his anointed” and promises to open doors before him, even though Cyrus does not know him. God says, “I form the light, and create darkness.” he calls the whole world to turn to him: “Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth: for I am God, and there is none else.” One day “unto me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear.”

**Babylon's Idols Fall Before God's Power (Isaiah 46:1–48:22)** — Babylon's gods are helpless burdens, unlike the Lord who carries Israel. Babylon's pride will be humbled, and God calls his stubborn people to listen, leave captivity, remember his redemption, and recognize that the wicked have no true peace.

Isaiah pictures the idols of Babylon, Bel and Nebo, being loaded onto carts and carried away. The idols cannot even save themselves, and they become a heavy burden for tired animals. But God is the opposite. he has carried his people since birth, and he promises, “even to your old age I am he; and even to hoar hairs will I carry you.” Hoar hairs means gray hair.

In chapter 47, proud Babylon is pictured as a pampered queen who said, “I shall be a lady for ever.” She will be forced to sit in the dust and grind grain like a slave. Her magic spells and her astrologers who watch the stars will not be able to save her. “None shall save thee.”

In chapter 48, God speaks to stubborn Israel. he told them what would happen long before it happened, so they could not give credit to their idols. he has tested them “in the furnace of affliction.” God says that if they had only listened, “then had thy peace been as a river.” Now he calls them to leave Babylon: “Go ye forth of Babylon, flee ye from the Chaldeans.” But he warns, “There is no peace, saith the LORD, unto the wicked.”

**God's Servant Brings Salvation Beyond Israel (Isaiah 49:1–50:11)** — God's servant is called to restore Israel and become a light so salvation reaches the ends of the earth. Zion is assured that God has not forgotten her, and the obedient servant trusts God through rejection and suffering.

God's servant speaks. God called him before he was born and made his mouth like a sharp sword. He feels as if his work has been for nothing, but God gives him an even bigger mission. It is not enough for him to bring Israel back to God. “I will also give thee for a light to the Gentiles, that thou mayest be my salvation unto the end of the earth.”

Zion feels abandoned and says, “The LORD hath forsaken me, and my Lord hath forgotten me.” God answers with one of the most tender promises in the Bible: “Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands.” Kings and queens will help bring her children home.

In chapter 50, the servant describes how he will suffer. He listens to God every morning and does not turn back. “I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair: I hid not my face from shame and spitting.” Jesus suffered these very things before his crucifixion. But the servant trusts God completely: “For the Lord GOD will help me; therefore shall I not be confounded: therefore have I set my face like a flint.”

**God Promises Everlasting Salvation to Zion (Isaiah 51:1–52:12)** — God calls his people to remember Abraham, trust his enduring salvation, and awaken from judgment. Zion is promised comfort, freedom, restoration, and messengers declaring peace as the people depart Babylon under God's protection.

God tells his people to remember where they came from. “Look unto Abraham your father, and unto Sarah that bare you.” God made one man into a great nation, so he can surely comfort Zion and make her deserts like the garden of Eden. His salvation will last forever, even when the heavens vanish like smoke and the earth wears out like an old coat.

Isaiah calls on God's power: “Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD.” God answers that he is the one who comforts them, so why should they be afraid of mere humans who die? Jerusalem has drunk the cup of God's anger until she staggered. But now God takes that cup out of her hand and gives it to her enemies.

Then Isaiah calls to Jerusalem: “Awake, awake; put on thy strength, O Zion; put on thy beautiful garments.” God's people were sold for nothing, and they will be redeemed without money. A messenger comes running over the hills with the news: “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace.” The watchmen on the walls shout for joy, for “all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God.”

**The Suffering Servant Bears the Sins of Many (Isaiah 52:13–53:12)** — The servant is rejected, suffers innocently, and bears the sins of others like a lamb led to slaughter. God vindicates and exalts him, declaring that he makes intercession for transgressors and brings justification to many.

This is the most famous passage in Isaiah, often called the Suffering Servant. It begins with a surprise. God's servant will be lifted up and greatly honored. Yet his face is so disfigured by suffering that people are shocked when they see him. “He hath no form nor comeliness; and when we shall see him, there is no beauty that we should desire him. He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief.”

People thought God was punishing him for his own sins. But they were wrong. “Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows.” “But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.”

He does not fight back or defend himself: “he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.” He is killed and buried with the rich, even though he did nothing wrong. Yet his death is not the end. It was God's plan for him to be an offering for sin. Afterward “he shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days.” Through him, many will be made righteous, because “he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.” In Acts 8, Philip explains this very passage to an Ethiopian official as a description of Jesus.

**God Offers Mercy and an Everlasting Covenant (Isaiah 54:1–55:13)** — God promises Zion restoration, lasting compassion, protection, and an enduring covenant. he invites the thirsty to receive his provision freely, calls the wicked to return for mercy, and declares that his word will accomplish his purpose.

Right after the servant's suffering comes a burst of joy. Jerusalem, pictured as a woman who could not have children, is told to sing, because she will have more children than she can count. She must make her tent bigger. God, her husband, abandoned her for only a short moment, “but with great mercies will I gather thee.” His promise is firm: “For the mountains shall depart, and the hills be removed; but my kindness shall not depart from thee.” “No weapon that is formed against thee shall prosper.”

Then chapter 55 opens with an invitation to everyone: “Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price.” God's gifts are free. “Seek ye the LORD while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near: Let the wicked forsake his way... for he will abundantly pardon.”

God explains why his ways are hard to understand: “For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways.” His word is like rain that makes the earth grow: “So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void.” His people will go out with joy, “and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands.”

**True Worship Welcomes the Faithful and Demands Justice (Isaiah 56:1–58:14)** — God welcomes faithful outsiders and calls his house a house of prayer for all peoples. he condemns selfish leaders and fasting without mercy, declaring that true worship requires justice, care for the oppressed, generosity, and honoring the Sabbath.

God welcomes outsiders. Foreigners and eunuchs who were once kept out of the temple are now invited in, if they keep his covenant. “For mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people.” Jesus quoted this when he drove the money changers out of the temple.

But Israel's leaders are like blind watchmen and lazy watchdogs who only care about themselves. The people are worshiping idols on every high hill. Yet God, the high and holy One, also lives “with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit,” to revive the humble. he says, “Peace, peace to him that is far off, and to him that is near.”

In chapter 58, the people complain that God does not notice when they fast. God answers that their fasting is fake. On the same days they fast, they fight and mistreat their workers. Then he describes the kind of fast He wants: “to loose the bands of wickedness,” “to let the oppressed go free,” “to deal thy bread to the hungry,” to bring the homeless poor into your house, and to clothe the naked. “Then shall thy light break forth as the morning.” If they keep the Sabbath as a delight and honor God, “Then shalt thou delight thyself in the LORD.”

**Sin Separates the People, but God Promises Redemption (Isaiah 59:1–21)** — Isaiah declares that injustice, violence, and lies have separated the people from God. When no human deliverer is found, the Lord himself acts in righteousness, promises judgment on evil, and sends a Redeemer to those who turn from sin.

The people wonder why God does not save them. Isaiah answers, “Behold, the LORD'S hand is not shortened, that it cannot save; neither his ear heavy, that it cannot hear: But your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you.” Their hands are stained with blood and their lips speak lies. “Their feet run to evil.” No one calls for justice, and truth has fallen in the street.

The people admit their guilt: they wait for light, but they walk in darkness, groping along the wall like blind men. God sees that there is no justice and no one to help, so He acts himself. He puts on righteousness like armor and salvation like a helmet, and he comes to repay his enemies. “The Redeemer shall come to Zion, and unto them that turn from transgression in Jacob.” God promises that his Spirit and his words will never leave his people or their children.

**Zion's Light Draws the Nations (Isaiah 60:1–62:12)** — God promises that Zion will rise from darkness as nations bring their people and wealth to her. The anointed messenger brings good news, healing, liberty, and comfort; Jerusalem will be known for salvation, righteousness, and God's delight.

Isaiah sees a bright future for Jerusalem. “Arise, shine; for thy light is come, and the glory of the LORD is risen upon thee.” Darkness covers the earth, but God's glory shines on his city. “And the Gentiles shall come to thy light, and kings to the brightness of thy rising.” People will bring wealth from distant lands, including gold and frankincense on camels. The city's gates will always be open. In the end, the city will not even need the sun, “but the LORD shall be unto thee an everlasting light.”

In chapter 61, someone filled with the Spirit speaks: “The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek; he hath sent me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives.” He comes “to comfort all that mourn” and to give “beauty for ashes.” Jesus read this passage in the synagogue at Nazareth and said, “This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears” (Luke 4:21).

In chapter 62, Isaiah refuses to be silent until Jerusalem's righteousness shines like a lamp. She will receive a new name. She will no longer be called Forsaken. Instead, God will delight in her as a bridegroom delights in his bride. Watchmen will stand on her walls and pray day and night. The people will be called “The holy people, The redeemed of the LORD.”

**God Judges Evil and Remembers His Mercy (Isaiah 63:1–64:12)** — Isaiah presents God's judgment against hostile evil, then remembers his kindness to Israel and pleads for mercy. The people confess sin, call God their Father and potter, and mourn Jerusalem and the burned temple.

Isaiah sees a warrior coming from Edom with his clothes stained red. He asks why his garments are red like someone who has been treading grapes in a winepress. The warrior answers that he has trampled the nations in his anger, because no one else would help. This is the LORD himself bringing judgment and salvation.

Then Isaiah remembers God's kindness to Israel in the past. “In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them.” But they rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit. Isaiah asks where the God is who led them through the sea.

In chapter 64, Isaiah prays, “Oh that thou wouldest rend the heavens, that thou wouldest come down.” He confesses the people's sin honestly: “But we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags.” Even their best deeds are not clean before God. Yet he appeals to God as their Father: “But now, O LORD, thou art our father; we are the clay, and thou our potter; and we all are the work of thy hand.” He begs God not to stay angry forever, because Jerusalem and the temple lie in ruins.

**God Promises New Heavens and a New Earth (Isaiah 65:1–25)** — God judges persistent rebellion but preserves servants who seek him. He promises new heavens and a new earth marked by joy, long life, secure work, answered prayer, and peace throughout creation.

God answers Isaiah's prayer. he says he has been ready to be found all along, holding out his hands to a rebellious people who keep worshiping idols. Those who turn away from him will be judged. But He will not destroy everyone. Just as people keep a cluster of grapes that still has good juice, God will save the faithful among his people.

Then God makes a stunning promise: “For, behold, I create new heavens and a new earth: and the former shall not be remembered, nor come into mind.” Jerusalem will be a place of joy, and “the voice of weeping shall be no more heard in her.” People will live long lives, build houses and live in them, and plant vineyards and eat their fruit. God will answer his people even before they call. And the peace Isaiah described earlier returns: “The wolf and the lamb shall feed together, and the lion shall eat straw like the bullock.” “They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain, saith the LORD.” The book of Revelation picks up this promise of a new heaven and a new earth.

**God's Glory Is Revealed Among All Nations (Isaiah 66:1–24)** — God rejects empty worship and honors the humble who tremble at his word. He promises Jerusalem's comfort and restoration, gathers nations to see his glory, and closes with the enduring contrast between universal worship and judgment for rebellion.

In the last chapter, God says, “The heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool: where is the house that ye build unto me?” No building can hold him. What He looks for is a humble heart: “to this man will I look, even to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word.” Religious ceremonies without a humble heart are worthless to him.

God promises that Zion will give birth to a whole nation in a single day, and He will comfort his people “As one whom his mother comforteth.” Then he will come with fire to judge the wicked. he will gather all nations and languages, and they will see his glory. He will send messengers to distant lands that have never heard of him, and they will bring his people home from every nation as an offering to the LORD. Some of these foreigners will even become priests and Levites.

All people will come to worship before God. But the book ends with a solemn warning. Those who rebelled against him will face judgment, “for their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched.” Jesus used these words to describe hell (Mark 9:48). Isaiah's book ends with both the hope of a new creation and a warning not to reject God.

# Jeremiah
When it happened: From the thirteenth year of King Josiah (627 BC) through the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, and afterward among the Jews who fled to Egypt (Jeremiah 1:2–3; 43–44).
When it was written: Jeremiah dictated his messages to his scribe Baruch, and when King Jehoiakim burned the scroll, Jeremiah dictated it again with more added (Jeremiah 36).
Where: Anathoth, Jerusalem, and later Tahpanhes in Egypt.
The world at the time: Assyria collapsed, and Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar defeated Egypt at Carchemish in 605 BC (Jeremiah 46:2). Judah's last kings wavered between Egypt and Babylon until Babylon destroyed Jerusalem.

**God Calls Jeremiah to Warn Judah (Jeremiah 1:1–19)** — God appoints Jeremiah before his birth to warn nations of judgment. Though Jeremiah feels too young, God places his words in the prophet's mouth, reveals disaster from the north, and promises to strengthen and deliver him against opposition.

Jeremiah is a young man from a family of priests in Anathoth, a village near Jerusalem. God calls him to be a prophet during the reign of good King Josiah, and he keeps preaching for about forty years, until Jerusalem falls to Babylon. God tells him, “Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee, and I ordained thee a prophet unto the nations.”

Jeremiah is afraid: “Ah, Lord GOD! behold, I cannot speak: for I am a child.” God answers, “Say not, I am a child: for thou shalt go to all that I shall send thee.” Then God touches his mouth and says, “Behold, I have put my words in thy mouth.” His job will be both to tear down and to build up nations.

God shows him two pictures. First, the branch of an almond tree. In Hebrew, the word for almond sounds like the word for watching, and God says, “I will hasten my word to perform it,” meaning he is watching over his word. Second, a boiling pot tipped toward the south. Disaster will pour out from the north on Judah because the people have abandoned God. Jeremiah's message will make him many enemies, but God promises, “I have made thee this day a defenced city, and an iron pillar, and brasen walls.” “They shall fight against thee; but they shall not prevail against thee; for I am with thee.”

**Judah Abandons the God Who Delivered Her (Jeremiah 2:1–4:4)** — God accuses Judah of abandoning the living water of his covenant for useless idols. Although Judah ignored northern Israel's example of judgment, God repeatedly calls the people to acknowledge sin, practice justice, remove idols, and return.

God remembers the early days of his relationship with Israel, like a young bride's love: “I remember thee, the kindness of thy youth, the love of thine espousals, when thou wentest after me in the wilderness.” But now Israel has turned away. God says this is something no other nation has done. Even pagans do not trade their gods, but Israel has traded the true God for idols. “For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water.” A cistern is a tank for collecting rainwater.

God compares Judah to a wild animal chasing after lovers and to an unfaithful wife. “Can a maid forget her ornaments, or a bride her attire? yet my people have forgotten me days without number.” Judah saw God punish the northern kingdom of Israel for its idolatry, yet Judah did the same thing and was even worse, because she only pretended to return to God.

Still, God pleads with them to come back: “Return, thou backsliding Israel.” “Turn, O backsliding children, saith the LORD; for I am married unto you.” he promises, “I will give you pastors according to mine heart.” But they must truly change: “Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns. Circumcise yourselves to the LORD.”

**Judgment Approaches a Nation Refusing Correction (Jeremiah 4:5–6:30)** — Jeremiah warns that an invading army from the north will devastate Judah because leaders and people reject truth, justice, and correction. False prophets promise peace, but the nation refuses the old paths of righteousness.

Jeremiah sounds the alarm: “Blow ye the trumpet in the land.” An enemy is coming from the north like a lion out of the thicket. Jeremiah is in agony as he sees the coming disaster: “My bowels, my bowels! I am pained at my very heart.” He has a vision of the land completely ruined: “I beheld the earth, and, lo, it was without form, and void.” It is like creation being undone.

God challenges Jeremiah to search Jerusalem: “Run ye to and fro through the streets of Jerusalem... if ye can find a man, if there be any that executeth judgment, that seeketh the truth; and I will pardon it.” But he finds that both the poor and the leaders have turned from God. “A wonderful and horrible thing is committed in the land; The prophets prophesy falsely, and the priests bear rule by their means; and my people love to have it so.”

The false prophets keep telling everyone that all is well. “They have healed also the hurt of the daughter of my people slightly, saying, Peace, peace; when there is no peace.” God calls the people back to the right road: “Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls. But they said, We will not walk therein.” Jeremiah is like a metal tester trying to purify silver, but the impurities will not come out. They will be called “Reprobate silver,” or rejected silver.

**The Temple Cannot Protect an Unfaithful People (Jeremiah 7:1–10:25)** — Jeremiah confronts false confidence in the temple, declaring that worship cannot protect people who steal, oppress, commit violence, and serve idols. He mourns Judah's refusal to repent and contrasts handmade idols with the living Creator.

God sends Jeremiah to stand at the gate of the temple and preach to the crowds coming to worship. The people think the temple will protect them no matter how they live. Jeremiah says, “Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these.” They steal, murder, commit adultery, and worship Baal, and then come to the temple and say they are safe. God asks, “Is this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes?” Jesus later quoted these words. God reminds them of Shiloh, where the tabernacle once stood, which he destroyed. he tells Jeremiah not even to pray for this people. Whole families are baking cakes for the “queen of heaven,” a pagan goddess, and they burn their children in the valley of Topheth.

Jeremiah weeps over the coming judgment. The people do not realize how late it is: “The harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved.” He asks, “Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there?” Gilead was famous for healing ointment. He wishes he could cry enough: “Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people!”

God tells people what is really worth boasting about: “Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, neither let the mighty man glory in his might... But let him that glorieth glory in this, that he understandeth and knoweth me.” In chapter 10, Jeremiah mocks idols, which are like scarecrows in a cucumber field. They cannot speak or walk and must be carried. But the LORD is the true and living God. Jeremiah prays humbly, “O LORD, I know that the way of man is not in himself: it is not in man that walketh to direct his steps.”

**Judah Breaks the Covenant and Rejects Jeremiah (Jeremiah 11:1–12:17)** — God recalls Judah's broken covenant and reveals a plot by Jeremiah's hometown against him. The prophet asks why wicked people prosper, while God warns of harder trials, announces judgment, and promises eventual compassion and restoration.

God reminds Jeremiah of the covenant he made with Israel when he brought them out of Egypt. They have broken it, just as their ancestors did, so disaster is coming. Then Jeremiah discovers a shocking plot. The men of his own hometown, Anathoth, are planning to kill him. “But I was like a lamb or an ox that is brought to the slaughter; and I knew not that they had devised devices against me.” They want to silence him so he will stop prophesying.

Jeremiah asks God the question many people ask: “Wherefore doth the way of the wicked prosper?” Why do evil people seem to do so well? God's answer is surprising. Things will get even harder for Jeremiah. “If thou hast run with the footmen, and they have wearied thee, then how canst thou contend with horses?” Even his own brothers have betrayed him. But God also promises that after punishing his people and the nations around them, he will have compassion and bring them back to their land.

**Jeremiah's Actions Show Judah's Coming Ruin (Jeremiah 13:1–15:21)** — A ruined linen girdle and jars of wine portray Judah's pride and coming captivity. Drought, false promises of peace, and Jeremiah's grief reveal that judgment will not be removed, though God promises to sustain his rejected prophet.

God gives Jeremiah strange object lessons. First, he tells him to buy a linen belt and wear it. Then he tells him to go and hide it in a crack in a rock by the Euphrates River. Many days later, Jeremiah digs it up, and it is rotten and “profitable for nothing.” God explains that Judah was meant to cling to him like a belt, but their pride has ruined them in the same way. Then God says their sin is so much a part of them that they cannot change on their own: “Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots? then may ye also do good, that are accustomed to do evil.”

A terrible drought comes, and Jeremiah prays for the people. But God says that even “Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my mind could not be toward this people.” Judgment will come by sword, famine, disease, and exile.

Jeremiah feels the weight of his lonely calling: “Woe is me, my mother, that thou hast borne me a man of strife and a man of contention to the whole earth!” Yet he still loves God's word: “Thy words were found, and I did eat them; and thy word was unto me the joy and rejoicing of mine heart.” God promises that if Jeremiah stays faithful, he will make him like a bronze wall the people cannot break.

**Jeremiah Warns of Exile and Calls for Trust (Jeremiah 16:1–17:27)** — Jeremiah's restricted personal life becomes a sign of coming disaster. God contrasts confidence in human strength with trust in him, exposes the deceitfulness of the heart, and calls Judah to honor the Sabbath or face judgment.

God tells Jeremiah not to marry or have children, because terrible days are coming to the families of Judah. He must not go to funerals or to parties either. His lonely life will be a living sign of the sorrow that is coming. Yet God also promises a future rescue even greater than the Exodus. People will no longer say that God brought Israel out of Egypt, but “The LORD liveth, that brought up the children of Israel from the land of the north.”

God says Judah's sin is deeply carved into them: “The sin of Judah is written with a pen of iron, and with the point of a diamond.” Then he contrasts two kinds of people. “Cursed be the man that trusteth in man.” He is like a dry bush in the desert. “Blessed is the man that trusteth in the LORD,” for “he shall be as a tree planted by the waters,” that does not worry in a drought. Jeremiah describes the human heart: “The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it? I the LORD search the heart.” He prays, “Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed.” Finally, God sends him to the city gates to warn the people to stop carrying loads and doing business on the Sabbath.

**The Potter Reveals God's Authority Over Nations (Jeremiah 18:1–20:18)** — At the potter's house, God shows his authority to reshape nations in response to repentance or rebellion. Judah refuses to turn, Jeremiah breaks a bottle to symbolize coming destruction, and persecution drives him to lament while God's word still burns within him.

God sends Jeremiah to a potter's house. He watches the potter shaping clay on the wheel. When the pot is spoiled in his hands, the potter squashes the clay and makes it into another vessel. God says, “as the clay is in the potter's hand, so are ye in mine hand, O house of Israel.” If a nation turns from evil, God will change his plans to bring disaster. But if it does evil, he will change his plans to bless it. When Jeremiah gives this message, the people make plans to attack him: “let us smite him with the tongue.”

Next God tells Jeremiah to buy a clay jar and take some leaders to the valley where children were sacrificed. There he smashes the jar in front of them and says, “Even so will I break this people and this city, as one breaketh a potter's vessel.”

When Pashur, the priest in charge of the temple, hears this, he has Jeremiah beaten and locked in the stocks overnight. When Pashur lets him out, Jeremiah gives him a new name meaning terror on every side. Then Jeremiah pours out his feelings to God honestly. He feels tricked: “O LORD, thou hast deceived me, and I was deceived.” Everyone mocks him. He tries to stop speaking for God, “But his word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was weary with forbearing, and I could not stay.” In his despair, he even curses the day he was born.

**Judah's Kings Hear the Warning of Destruction (Jeremiah 21:1–23:40)** — Jeremiah tells Judah's kings that Jerusalem will fall and condemns rulers who exploit rather than protect. God promises to gather a remnant and raise a righteous Branch from David's line, while false prophets who promise peace face judgment.

As Babylon's army surrounds Jerusalem, King Zedekiah sends messengers asking Jeremiah to pray that God will do a miracle and make the enemy leave. Jeremiah's answer is hard. God himself will fight against the city. “Behold, I set before you the way of life, and the way of death.” Those who stay in the city will die, but those who surrender to the Babylonians will live.

Jeremiah then gives messages to Judah's kings. He condemns Jehoiakim for building a fancy palace by forcing his workers to labor without pay. He reminds him of his father Josiah, who “judged the cause of the poor and needy.” God says that is what it truly means to know him. Of Jehoiachin, also called Coniah, God says that even if he were the signet ring on God's hand, God would pull it off. “Write ye this man childless,” for none of his sons will sit on David's throne.

In chapter 23, God condemns Judah's leaders: “Woe be unto the pastors that destroy and scatter the sheep of my pasture!” but he promises a new king: “Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will raise unto David a righteous Branch,” and his name will be “THE LORD OUR RIGHTEOUSNESS.” God also condemns the false prophets who claim to speak for him: “I have not sent these prophets, yet they ran.” They share their own dreams, but God's true word is different: “Is not my word like as a fire? saith the LORD; and like a hammer that breaketh the rock in pieces?”

**Two Baskets of Figs Reveal Different Futures (Jeremiah 24:1–10)** — Good figs represent Judeans already exiled to Babylon, whom God will watch over and restore. Bad figs represent Zedekiah and those remaining in Jerusalem or fleeing to Egypt, who will face further disaster for continued unfaithfulness.

After King Jehoiachin and many of Judah's leaders and craftsmen have been taken to Babylon, God shows Jeremiah two baskets of figs in front of the temple. One basket has very good figs, like the first ripe ones of the season. The other has figs so rotten they cannot be eaten. God explains the vision. The good figs are the people already carried into exile. God will watch over them and bring them back. “And I will give them an heart to know me, that I am the LORD: and they shall be my people, and I will be their God: for they shall return unto me with their whole heart.” The bad figs are King Zedekiah, his officials, and the people who stayed in Jerusalem or fled to Egypt. They think they are the lucky ones, but disaster will follow them.

**Seventy Years of Captivity Are Announced (Jeremiah 25:1–38)** — After years of ignored warnings, Jeremiah announces seventy years of Babylonian domination for Judah and surrounding nations. Babylon itself will later be judged as the cup of God's wrath reaches rulers and peoples across the earth.

In the fourth year of King Jehoiakim, the same year Nebuchadnezzar becomes king of Babylon, Jeremiah reminds the people that for twenty-three years he has spoken God's word to them, “rising early and speaking; but ye have not hearkened.” Because they would not listen, God will send “Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, my servant,” to conquer the land. “And these nations shall serve the king of Babylon seventy years.” After the seventy years, God will punish Babylon too. Daniel later read this very prophecy while in exile (Daniel 9:2).

Then God gives Jeremiah a picture of judgment. He is to take a cup of wine that stands for God's anger and make all the nations drink from it, starting with Jerusalem and Judah and then Egypt, the Philistines, Edom, Moab, Ammon, and many others, and finally Babylon itself. They will stagger like drunks. The LORD will roar from on high, and his judgment will reach the ends of the earth.

**Jeremiah Is Threatened for Warning the Temple (Jeremiah 26:1–24)** — Jeremiah's warning that the temple could become like Shiloh leads priests and prophets to demand his death. Officials and elders defend his right to speak, recalling Micah, while Ahikam protects Jeremiah from Jehoiakim's violence.

Early in Jehoiakim's reign, God sends Jeremiah to the temple courtyard to warn the people that if they do not listen, God will make the temple like Shiloh, which lay in ruins. When he finishes, the priests, prophets, and people grab him and shout, “Thou shalt surely die.” They put him on trial in front of the royal officials. The priests and prophets say, “This man is worthy to die.”

Jeremiah does not back down. He says God sent him, and if they kill him, they will be guilty of innocent blood. The officials and people are convinced: “This man is not worthy to die.” Some of the elders remind everyone that a hundred years earlier, the prophet Micah said almost the same thing in King Hezekiah's day. Hezekiah did not kill him. Instead, he repented, and God spared the city.

But not every prophet escapes. Another prophet, Urijah, preached the same message. When King Jehoiakim tried to kill him, he fled to Egypt. The king had him brought back and killed with the sword. Jeremiah survives only because a powerful official, Ahikam the son of Shaphan, protects him.

**God Commands the Nations to Submit to Babylon (Jeremiah 27:1–29:32)** — Jeremiah wears a yoke to command submission to Babylon and exposes false predictions of quick deliverance. He tells exiles to build homes, raise families, and seek Babylon's peace while waiting for God's promised restoration after seventy years.

God tells Jeremiah to make a wooden yoke, like the one oxen wear, and put it on his own neck. Then he sends messages to the kings of nearby nations whose ambassadors have come to Jerusalem to plan a rebellion. God has given all these lands to Nebuchadnezzar. Any nation that submits to Babylon will stay in its land. To Zedekiah he says, “serve the king of Babylon, and live.”

A false prophet named Hananiah stands up in the temple and contradicts Jeremiah. He claims that within two years God will break Babylon's yoke and bring back the exiles and the temple treasures. Then he takes the yoke off Jeremiah's neck and breaks it. Jeremiah walks away. Later God sends him back with a message: Hananiah has broken a wooden yoke, but God will put a yoke of iron on these nations. And Jeremiah tells Hananiah, “this year thou shalt die, because thou hast taught rebellion against the LORD.” Two months later, Hananiah dies.

In chapter 29, Jeremiah writes a letter to the exiles in Babylon. False prophets there are telling them they will be home soon. But Jeremiah tells them to settle down: “Build ye houses, and dwell in them; and plant gardens, and eat the fruit of them.” They should marry and have children. “And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it.” Then comes a famous promise: “after seventy years be accomplished at Babylon I will visit you.” “For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, saith the LORD, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you an expected end.” “And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart.”

**God Promises Israel's Restoration and a New Covenant (Jeremiah 30:1–31:40)** — God promises to heal, gather, and rebuild Israel and Judah after captivity. he announces a new covenant in which his law will be written on their hearts, sins will be forgiven, and his commitment to his people will endure.

God tells Jeremiah, “Write thee all the words that I have spoken unto thee in a book.” These chapters are full of hope and are sometimes called the Book of Comfort. A terrible day is coming, “the time of Jacob's trouble; but he shall be saved out of it.” God will break the yoke of foreign rule and bring his people home. “For I will restore health unto thee, and I will heal thee of thy wounds.”

God speaks with deep love: “Yea, I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee.” Then Jeremiah hears weeping: “A voice was heard in Ramah, lamentation, and bitter weeping; Rahel weeping for her children.” Rachel, the mother of Israel, is pictured crying over her lost children. Matthew quotes this verse when Herod kills the babies of Bethlehem. But God comforts her: “Refrain thy voice from weeping, and thine eyes from tears.” Her children will come home.

Then God makes one of the greatest promises in the Old Testament: “Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah.” It will not be like the old covenant, which they broke. “I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people.” “They shall all know me, from the least of them unto the greatest of them, saith the LORD: for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more.” At the Last Supper, Jesus spoke of this new covenant in his blood, and Hebrews 8 quotes this whole passage. God says his promise to Israel is as sure as the sun, moon, and stars.

**Jeremiah Buys a Field as a Sign of Hope (Jeremiah 32:1–33:26)** — While Jerusalem is under siege and Jeremiah is confined, he buys a field in Anathoth as a sign that homes, fields, and vineyards will be owned again. God confirms both coming judgment and his everlasting covenant of restoration.

It is the tenth year of King Zedekiah. The Babylonian army has surrounded Jerusalem, and Jeremiah is locked in the court of the guard. Then God tells him that his cousin Hanameel will come and offer to sell him a field in Anathoth. It seems like a terrible deal, because the land is about to be conquered. But Jeremiah buys it anyway for seventeen shekels of silver. He signs the deed in front of witnesses and has Baruch seal the copies in a clay jar so they will last a long time. The message is clear: “Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land.”

Afterward, Jeremiah prays, “Ah Lord GOD! behold, thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee.” God answers with the same words: “Behold, I am the LORD, the God of all flesh: is there any thing too hard for me?” he will hand the city over to Babylon, but he will also bring his people back and make an everlasting covenant with them.

In chapter 33, while Jeremiah is still locked up, God invites him, “Call unto me, and I will answer thee, and shew thee great and mighty things, which thou knowest not.” he promises to heal Jerusalem and fill it again with joyful voices, weddings, and songs of thanks. he repeats his promise of a righteous Branch from David's family, and says, “David shall never want a man to sit upon the throne of the house of Israel.” his covenant with David is as certain as day and night.

**Judah Breaks Its Promise to Free Servants (Jeremiah 34:1–22)** — During the siege, Judah's leaders covenant to free Hebrew servants but then force them back into slavery. God condemns the betrayal and declares that Babylon will return, capture Jerusalem, and bring consequences upon the covenant breakers.

While Babylon's army attacks Jerusalem and Judah's last fortified towns, God tells Jeremiah to warn Zedekiah that he will be captured and taken to Babylon, though he will die in peace. During the siege, Zedekiah and the people make a covenant to set free all their Hebrew slaves, as God's law required. They seal it by cutting a calf in two and walking between the pieces, an ancient way of saying, may I be cut in pieces if I break this promise.

But when Babylon's army pulls back for a while, the people change their minds and force their former slaves back into service. God is angry: “ye have not hearkened unto me, in proclaiming liberty.” So he announces a different kind of liberty: “behold, I proclaim a liberty for you, saith the LORD, to the sword, to the pestilence, and to the famine.” Those who broke the covenant will be cut like the calf, and Babylon's army will return and burn the city.

**The Rechabites Demonstrate Faithful Obedience (Jeremiah 35:1–19)** — The Rechabites refuse wine because they have obeyed their ancestor's command for generations. God uses their faithfulness to expose Judah's refusal to hear his repeated warnings, while promising Jonadab's descendants an enduring place before him.

God tells Jeremiah to invite the family of the Rechabites into a room at the temple and offer them wine. The Rechabites are a clan descended from a man named Jonadab, who lived about 250 years earlier. They set bowls of wine in front of them, but the Rechabites refuse: “We will drink no wine: for Jonadab the son of Rechab our father commanded us.” For generations they have obeyed their ancestor's commands never to drink wine, build houses, or plant fields, but to live in tents. They only moved into Jerusalem to escape the Babylonian army.

God uses their example to shame Judah. The Rechabites faithfully obey a human ancestor, but God's people refuse to obey God, even though he has sent prophet after prophet. So disaster will come on Judah. But God makes a promise to the Rechabite family: “Jonadab the son of Rechab shall not want a man to stand before me for ever.”

**Jehoiakim Burns Jeremiah's Written Warnings (Jeremiah 36:1–32)** — Jeremiah dictates God's warnings to Baruch, who reads them publicly. Though officials fear the message, Jehoiakim cuts the scroll apart and burns it; God preserves Jeremiah and Baruch and commands a new scroll containing the same judgment.

In the fourth year of King Jehoiakim, God tells Jeremiah to write on a scroll everything he has spoken to him since the days of Josiah. Perhaps if the people hear all the warnings at once, they will turn back. Jeremiah dictates, and his assistant Baruch writes it all down. Jeremiah is banned from the temple, so he sends Baruch to read the scroll aloud there on a day of fasting.

Some royal officials hear it and are frightened. They tell Baruch, “Go, hide thee, thou and Jeremiah; and let no man know where ye be.” Then they take the scroll to the king. It is winter, and King Jehoiakim is sitting in his winter palace with a fire burning in the fireplace in front of him. As an official named Jehudi reads, “when Jehudi had read three or four leaves, he cut it with the penknife, and cast it into the fire,” until the whole scroll is burned. “Yet they were not afraid, nor rent their garments.” A few officials beg the king not to burn it, but he will not listen. He orders the arrest of Jeremiah and Baruch, “but the LORD hid them.”

God tells Jeremiah to take another scroll and write it all again. he also announces that Jehoiakim will have no son on David's throne, and that his dead body will be thrown out in the heat by day and the frost by night. So Jeremiah dictates again, and Baruch writes it all, “and there were added besides unto them many like words.” The king could burn the scroll, but he could not destroy God's word.

**Jeremiah Is Imprisoned During Jerusalem's Final Siege (Jeremiah 37:1–38:28)** — Jeremiah continues declaring that Egypt will not save Jerusalem and that surrender to Babylon will preserve life. He is beaten, imprisoned, and lowered into a muddy cistern until Ebedmelech rescues him, but Zedekiah still refuses God's warning.

Zedekiah is now king, but he and his officials still ignore God's word. When an Egyptian army marches north, the Babylonians lift the siege for a while. Jeremiah warns that they will be back and will burn the city. During the break, Jeremiah tries to leave Jerusalem to go to his family land in Benjamin. A guard at the gate arrests him, accusing him of deserting to the Babylonians. The officials beat him and throw him into a dungeon for many days. Then King Zedekiah secretly sends for him and asks, “Is there any word from the LORD? And Jeremiah said, There is.” He tells the king he will be handed over to Babylon. Zedekiah moves him to the court of the guard and gives him a loaf of bread every day “until all the bread in the city were spent.”

But some officials demand his death: “Let this man be put to death: for thus he weakeneth the hands of the men of war.” Zedekiah answers weakly, “the king is not he that can do any thing against you.” So they lower Jeremiah with ropes into a cistern, a deep pit for storing water. There is no water, only mud, “so Jeremiah sunk in the mire.”

Then an Ethiopian named Ebedmelech, an official in the palace, goes to the king and pleads for Jeremiah, saying he will starve to death. The king tells him to take thirty men and pull him out. Ebedmelech gathers old rags and worn-out clothes and lowers them down on ropes, telling Jeremiah to put them under his armpits so the ropes will not cut him. They pull him out of the pit. Zedekiah meets with Jeremiah secretly one last time. Jeremiah tells him again that if he surrenders, he and the city will be spared. Zedekiah is afraid of what people will think. Jeremiah begs him, “Obey, I beseech thee, the voice of the LORD.” But Zedekiah will not, and Jeremiah stays in the court of the guard until the day Jerusalem is captured.

**Jerusalem Falls and Jeremiah Is Released (Jeremiah 39:1–18)** — Babylon captures Jerusalem, blinds and carries away Zedekiah, burns the city, and breaks its walls. Jeremiah is protected and placed with Gedaliah, while Ebedmelech receives God's promise of survival because he trusted the Lord.

In the ninth year of Zedekiah, the Babylonian army surrounds Jerusalem again. A year and a half later, they break through the city wall. That night Zedekiah and his soldiers flee through a gate by the king's garden, but the Babylonians chase them and catch him on the plains of Jericho. They bring him to Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah. There the king of Babylon kills Zedekiah's sons in front of him, then puts out his eyes, binds him in chains, and takes him to Babylon. The Babylonians burn the palace and the houses, break down the walls of Jerusalem, and carry most of the people into exile, leaving only the poorest in the land.

Nebuchadnezzar gives special orders about Jeremiah: “Take him, and look well to him, and do him no harm.” Jeremiah is taken out of the court of the guard and placed in the care of Gedaliah, who will soon become governor. God also sends a message to Ebedmelech, the Ethiopian who rescued Jeremiah from the pit. He will not be killed in the fall of the city, “because thou hast put thy trust in me, saith the LORD.”

**The Survivors Struggle After Jerusalem's Destruction (Jeremiah 40:1–41:18)** — Gedaliah governs the remaining people in Judah and urges peace under Babylon, but Ishmael murders him and takes captives. Johanan rescues the captives, and the survivors fear Babylonian retaliation for the murder of its appointed governor.

Nebuzaradan, the captain of the Babylonian guard, finds Jeremiah in chains among the captives being taken to Babylon. He releases him and tells him he can go to Babylon or stay wherever he wants. Jeremiah chooses to stay with Gedaliah at Mizpah, among the poor people left in the land. Gedaliah encourages the people to settle down, harvest the fruit, and serve the king of Babylon. Jews who had fled to nearby countries begin coming back, and they gather a great harvest of wine and summer fruit.

Then a commander named Johanan warns Gedaliah that the king of the Ammonites has sent a man named Ishmael to murder him. Johanan offers to kill Ishmael secretly first. But Gedaliah does not believe him and refuses. Soon after, Ishmael, who is from the royal family, comes to Mizpah with ten men. While they are eating together, Ishmael and his men murder Gedaliah, along with the Jews and Babylonian soldiers who are with him.

The next day, eighty men arrive from the north on their way to worship at the ruined temple. Ishmael pretends to weep and lures them into the town. Then he kills most of them and throws their bodies into a large cistern. He takes the rest of the people at Mizpah captive and heads for Ammon. Johanan and his soldiers chase him and rescue the captives, but Ishmael escapes. Now Johanan and the people are terrified that Babylon will punish them for Gedaliah's murder, so they start heading toward Egypt.

**The Survivors Reject God's Command to Remain in Judah (Jeremiah 42:1–44:30)** — The survivors ask Jeremiah for God's direction, promise obedience, then reject his command to remain in Judah. They flee to Egypt, continue idolatry, and receive warnings that the disasters they fear will follow them there.

On the way, the leaders and all the people come to Jeremiah and beg him to pray and ask God what they should do. They promise, “whether it be good, or whether it be evil, we will obey the voice of the LORD our God.” Ten days later, God answers. If they stay in the land, God will build them up and protect them. “Be not afraid of the king of Babylon.” But if they go to Egypt, the very sword and famine they fear will catch up with them there.

The leaders respond, “Thou speakest falsely: the LORD our God hath not sent thee to say, Go not into Egypt.” They blame Baruch for turning Jeremiah against them. Then they take all the people, including Jeremiah and Baruch, and go down to Egypt anyway, to the city of Tahpanhes. There God tells Jeremiah to bury large stones at the entrance of Pharaoh's palace. Nebuchadnezzar will come and set his throne on those very stones.

In Egypt, the Jews keep worshiping other gods. Jeremiah warns them that this is exactly why Jerusalem was destroyed. But they flatly refuse to listen. The women say they will keep burning incense to the queen of heaven, because when they did it before, they had plenty of food. “But since we left off to burn incense to the queen of heaven... we have wanted all things, and have been consumed by the sword and by the famine.” Jeremiah announces that only a few of them will ever return to Judah. As a sign, God will hand Pharaoh Hophra over to his enemies.

**God Gives Baruch a Personal Promise (Jeremiah 45:1–5)** — God answers Baruch's distress by warning him not to seek greatness in a time of coming national disaster. Though judgment is near, God promises to preserve Baruch's life wherever he goes.

This short chapter is a personal message to Baruch, Jeremiah's faithful assistant. It goes back to the year Baruch wrote down Jeremiah's scroll. Baruch was discouraged: “Woe is me now! for the LORD hath added grief to my sorrow; I fainted in my sighing, and I find no rest.” God reminds him that he is about to tear down what he built, and then asks, “And seekest thou great things for thyself? seek them not.” Baruch should not hope for fame or success in such a time. But God gives him a promise: “but thy life will I give unto thee for a prey in all places whither thou goest.” He will survive.

**Egypt and Philistia Face God's Judgment (Jeremiah 46:1–47:7)** — Jeremiah foretells Egypt's defeat by Babylon and warns that its gods and military confidence cannot save it, though Egypt will later be inhabited again. Philistia also faces an invading northern force appointed by God for judgment.

The last part of Jeremiah contains messages to the nations. The first is about Egypt. Jeremiah describes the great battle at Carchemish, on the Euphrates River, where Nebuchadnezzar defeated Pharaoh Necho's army in the fourth year of Jehoiakim. The proud Egyptian army that rose like the flooding Nile is crushed. Jeremiah mocks them: “Go up into Gilead, and take balm, O virgin, the daughter of Egypt,” but no medicine can heal her. Egypt is like “a very fair heifer,” a beautiful young cow, but destruction is coming from the north. Yet God comforts his own people: “But fear not thou, O my servant Jacob.”

The second message is against the Philistines. Their cities will be overwhelmed like a flood. The people will be so terrified that fathers will not even look back for their children. They cry out, “O thou sword of the LORD, how long will it be ere thou be quiet?”

**Moab and Ammon Receive Warnings of Destruction (Jeremiah 48:1–49:6)** — Moab is judged for pride, trust in wealth, and confidence in Chemosh; Ammon is judged for occupying Gad's territory and trusting its resources. Both nations receive a later promise of restored fortunes after judgment.

Jeremiah's long message against Moab describes the destruction of its cities one by one. Moab has never been conquered or taken into exile. Jeremiah compares it to wine that has been left sitting too long: “Moab hath been at ease from his youth, and he hath settled on his lees, and hath not been emptied from vessel to vessel.” Now it will be poured out. Moab's god Chemosh will be carried into captivity along with his priests. The main reason for Moab's fall is its pride: “We have heard the pride of Moab, (he is exceeding proud).” Jeremiah weeps for Moab. Yet the message ends with a promise: “Yet will I bring again the captivity of Moab in the latter days.”

Then comes a short message against the Ammonites, who took over land belonging to Israel. Their capital, Rabbah, will become a heap of ruins. But God will restore the Ammonites in the future too.

**God Judges Edom, Damascus, Kedar, and Elam (Jeremiah 49:7–39)** — Edom's wisdom and mountain strongholds, Damascus's defenses, Kedar's desert distance, and Elam's military strength cannot prevent God's judgment. Elam, like other nations, is also promised future restoration.

Jeremiah turns to Edom, the nation descended from Esau. “Is wisdom no more in Teman?” Teman was a city famous for its wise men. Edom trusted in its high, rocky strongholds, but God says, “though thou shouldest make thy nest as high as the eagle, I will bring thee down from thence.” Much of this message is almost the same as the book of Obadiah. Edom will become like Sodom and Gomorrah.

Then Jeremiah speaks to Damascus, the capital of Syria, which will panic and be burned. He speaks to the desert tribes of Kedar and Hazor, whose tents and camels will be taken. Finally he speaks to Elam, a nation far to the east beyond Babylon. God will break the bow of its archers and scatter its people to every wind. But this message also ends with hope: “I will bring again the captivity of Elam, saith the LORD.”

**Babylon's Fall and Israel's Return Are Foretold (Jeremiah 50:1–51:64)** — Babylon, which destroyed Jerusalem and carried Judah captive, will itself fall for pride and cruelty. Israel and Judah will return to seek the Lord, and a scroll sunk in the Euphrates symbolizes Babylon's final downfall.

The longest message of all is against Babylon, the empire God used to punish Judah. Now Babylon itself will fall. “Babylon is taken, Bel is confounded.” Bel was its chief god. An enemy will come from the north and destroy it. At the same time, the people of Israel and Judah will come home weeping and seeking the LORD. God says, “Israel is a scattered sheep.” But he will bring them back to their pasture, because “Their Redeemer is strong; the LORD of hosts is his name.”

God compares Babylon to a golden cup that made the whole world drunk: “Babylon hath been a golden cup in the LORD'S hand.” But now, “Babylon is suddenly fallen and destroyed: howl for her.” God calls his people to escape: “Flee out of the midst of Babylon, and deliver every man his soul.” Babylon's mighty walls will be torn down and its gates burned.

In the fourth year of Zedekiah, a royal official named Seraiah traveled to Babylon. Jeremiah gave him a scroll with these prophecies against Babylon. When he got there, he was to read it aloud, then tie a stone to it and throw it into the Euphrates River, saying, “Thus shall Babylon sink, and shall not rise.” The section ends, “Thus far are the words of Jeremiah.”

**Jerusalem Falls and Jehoiachin Receives Mercy (Jeremiah 52:1–34)** — The book closes with Jerusalem's siege, the blinding and captivity of Zedekiah, the destruction of the temple, and the exile of Judah. Years later, Babylon's new king releases Jehoiachin and grants him honor and daily provision.

The book ends with a historical account of Jerusalem's fall, very much like 2 Kings 25. Zedekiah reigns eleven years and rebels against Babylon. Nebuchadnezzar besieges the city until the famine is so severe that there is no bread left. When the wall is broken, Zedekiah flees but is captured. At Riblah, his sons are killed in front of him, his eyes are put out, and he is taken in chains to Babylon, where he stays in prison until he dies.

The Babylonians burn the temple, the palace, and every important building in Jerusalem, and they break down the walls. They break apart the great bronze pillars and the bronze Sea from Solomon's temple and carry everything of value to Babylon. They execute the chief priest and other leaders. The book gives the number of people taken into exile in three different deportations, a total of 4,600, probably counting only the men.

The last paragraph offers a small sign of hope. Thirty-seven years after King Jehoiachin was taken to Babylon, a new Babylonian king, Evilmerodach, releases him from prison. He speaks kindly to him, gives him a seat of honor above the other captive kings, and lets him eat at the king's table for the rest of his life. David's royal line is still alive.

# Lamentations
When it happened: Just after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and the temple in 586 BC.
When it was written: The first four poems are acrostics, each verse or group of verses beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Their vivid detail suggests an eyewitness writing soon after the city fell. Jeremiah is known to have written laments (2 Chronicles 35:25).
Where: The ruins of Jerusalem.
The world at the time: The Babylonians had burned the temple and the city, many people had been killed or carried into exile, and those left behind faced famine. Neighboring Edom rejoiced at Jerusalem's fall (Lamentations 4:21).

**Jerusalem Mourns Its Destruction (Lamentations 1:1–22)** — Jerusalem, once full of worshipers and life, lies empty after Babylon's conquest. The city acknowledges that the Lord is righteous and that Judah rebelled, yet it mourns captivity, hunger, loss, and the cruelty of enemies who offer no comfort.

Lamentations is a book of five sad poems written soon after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC. Jewish tradition says Jeremiah wrote them. The first four poems are acrostics: each verse, or group of verses, begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from beginning to end. It is as if the writer is grieving from A to Z.

The first poem opens with a cry: “How doth the city sit solitary, that was full of people! how is she become as a widow!” Jerusalem is pictured as a woman weeping alone at night. “Among all her lovers she hath none to comfort her,” because the nations she trusted have betrayed her. Her people are in exile, and the roads to Zion are empty because no one comes to the feasts anymore.

Then Jerusalem herself speaks to everyone passing by: “Is it nothing to you, all ye that pass by? behold, and see if there be any sorrow like unto my sorrow.” She is honest about why this happened: “The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against his commandment.” She asks God to see her suffering and to judge her enemies, who are glad that she has fallen.

**God's Judgment Falls Upon His People (Lamentations 2:1–22)** — The destruction of Jerusalem and its temple is presented as God's judgment on Judah's sin. Leaders, priests, and prophets are brought low; children faint from hunger; false prophets are exposed; and the people are urged to pour out their desperate hearts before God.

The second poem describes the destruction as the work of God himself. The Lord has acted like an enemy against his own people. he has swallowed up Israel's palaces, thrown down Judah's fortresses, and even rejected his own altar and temple. “The LORD hath done that which he had devised; he hath fulfilled his word.” Long ago he warned them this would happen.

The writer is overwhelmed with grief: “Mine eyes do fail with tears.” Little children faint from hunger in the streets and ask their mothers where the bread is. The false prophets are partly to blame: “Thy prophets have seen vain and foolish things for thee: and they have not discovered thine iniquity,” meaning they never warned the people about their sin. Now enemies pass by, clap their hands, and mock: “Is this the city that men call The perfection of beauty, The joy of the whole earth?”

The poet urges the survivors to cry out to God: “pour out thine heart like water before the face of the Lord.” The poem ends with the horror of the siege, when women were so desperate that they ate their own children, and priests and prophets were killed inside the temple.

**Hope Appears in the Middle of Suffering (Lamentations 3:1–39)** — A suffering voice describes affliction and despair, then recalls that the Lord's compassion and faithfulness do not end. God is the speaker's portion and reason for hope; he disciplines but does not cast people away forever and does not approve injustice.

The third poem is the longest and the center of the book. It has sixty-six verses, three for each letter of the alphabet. A man speaks for the whole nation: “I am the man that hath seen affliction by the rod of his wrath.” He feels as though God has walled him in, led him into darkness, and shut out his prayers: “when I cry and shout, he shutteth out my prayer.”

Then, right in the middle of his pain, something changes. He remembers something that gives him hope. “This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope. It is of the LORD'S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.” The hymn “Great Is Thy Faithfulness” comes from these verses. He declares, “The LORD is my portion, saith my soul; therefore will I hope in him.”

He learns to wait patiently: “It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD.” Suffering will not last forever, “For the Lord will not cast off for ever,” and God does not enjoy causing pain: “For he doth not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men.”

**A Broken People Cry Out to God (Lamentations 3:40–66)** — The people are called to examine their ways, confess rebellion, and return to God. Amid tears and persecution, the speaker calls on the Lord, remembers that he heard and defended him, and asks God to see and judge the cruelty of enemies.

The poet calls the people to turn back to God: “Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD. Let us lift up our heart with our hands unto God in the heavens.” They confess that they have sinned and rebelled. He weeps without stopping over the destruction of his people.

Then he remembers a time when his enemies hunted him like a bird and threw him into a pit. Water rose over his head, and he thought he would die. “I called upon thy name, O LORD, out of the low dungeon.” And God came near and answered: “thou saidst, Fear not.” “Thou hast redeemed my life.” The poem ends with a prayer that God will see the wrongs done to him and repay his enemies for their cruelty.

**The Terrible Cost of Jerusalem's Fall (Lamentations 4:1–22)** — Jerusalem's former honor is replaced by famine, shame, and unbearable suffering. Priests and prophets share responsibility for corruption, false hope in foreign aid fails, and the captured king cannot protect the people; yet Zion's punishment is not promised to last forever.

The fourth poem compares what Jerusalem used to be with what it has become. “How is the gold become dim!” The precious children of Zion, once worth their weight in gold, are now treated like cheap clay pots. The famine during the siege was so terrible that babies' tongues stuck to the roofs of their mouths from thirst, and children begged for bread with no one to give it. Rich people who once wore fine clothes now dig through garbage. Those who died by the sword were better off than those who slowly starved. Even loving mothers boiled their own children for food.

The poet says Jerusalem's punishment was worse than Sodom's. Her prophets and priests had shed innocent blood. Now they wander the streets like blind men, and people shout at them to go away. The people kept looking for help from Egypt that never came, and the enemy hunted them down. Even King Zedekiah, “the anointed of the LORD,” was captured. The poem ends by warning Edom, which celebrated Jerusalem's fall, that it will soon drink the same cup of judgment, while Zion's punishment is coming to an end.

**Remembering the Ruins and Pleading for Restoration (Lamentations 5:1–22)** — Judah brings its disgrace, hunger, oppression, and ruined Jerusalem before God. Though joy has become mourning and Mount Zion is desolate, the people remember that the Lord reigns forever and plead for restoration while ending with unresolved sorrow.

The last poem is a prayer from the whole community. It is not an acrostic, but it still has twenty-two verses. “Remember, O LORD, what is come upon us: consider, and behold our reproach.” Their land has been handed over to foreigners. They are like orphans and widows. They must pay for their own water and firewood. Young men are forced to grind grain, and boys stagger under heavy loads of wood. “The joy of our heart is ceased; our dance is turned into mourning.” “Woe unto us, that we have sinned!”

Yet they know God still rules: “Thou, O LORD, remainest for ever; thy throne from generation to generation.” They ask, “Wherefore dost thou forget us for ever?” and pray, “Turn thou us unto thee, O LORD, and we shall be turned; renew our days as of old.” The book ends with an honest, unanswered question: “But thou hast utterly rejected us; thou art very wroth against us.” Lamentations does not end with a neat answer. It shows that it is right to bring even our deepest grief to God.

# Ezekiel
When it happened: From the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's captivity (593 BC; Ezekiel 1:2) to the twenty-seventh year (571 BC; Ezekiel 29:17). Many of the prophecies are dated to the day.
When it was written: Most of the book is Ezekiel's first-person record of his visions and messages (Ezekiel 1:1), likely written down during the exile in Babylon.
Where: Among the exiles by the river Chebar in Babylonia, at Tel-abib (Ezekiel 1:1–3; 3:15). In visions he was carried to Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Ezekiel was taken to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC. For his first six years of prophesying, Jerusalem still stood under King Zedekiah; then in 586 BC the exiles received news that the city had fallen (Ezekiel 33:21).

**Ezekiel Sees God's Glory and Is Called (Ezekiel 1:1–3:27)** — Among Judean exiles by the river Chebar in Babylon, Ezekiel sees a vision of God's glory: living creatures, wheels, and a throne above them. God calls the priest Ezekiel to be a watchman for Israel, gives him a scroll of lamentation to eat, and commands him to speak whether the people listen or refuse.

Ezekiel is a priest who was taken to Babylon in the first group of captives, about ten years before Jerusalem fell. He is living among the exiles by the river Chebar when, in the fifth year of exile, “the heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God.” A great storm cloud comes out of the north, flashing with fire. In it are four living creatures, each with four wings and four faces: “the face of a man, and the face of a lion,” an ox, and an eagle. Beside them are gleaming wheels, “a wheel in the middle of a wheel,” covered with eyes, that move wherever the creatures go. Above them is a throne like sapphire, and on it a figure that looks like a man, glowing with fire and surrounded by a rainbow. “This was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD.” Ezekiel falls on his face.

God calls him “Son of man,” a name he uses for Ezekiel more than ninety times, and sends him “to a rebellious nation that hath rebelled against me.” He must speak “whether they will hear, or whether they will forbear.” A hand holds out a scroll written on both sides with “lamentations, and mourning, and woe.” God tells him to eat it, and “it was in my mouth as honey for sweetness.”

Ezekiel sits stunned among the exiles for seven days. Then God says, “Son of man, I have made thee a watchman unto the house of Israel.” Like a guard on a city wall, he must warn people when danger is coming. If he fails to warn the wicked, “his blood will I require at thine hand.” God also makes Ezekiel unable to speak, except when God opens his mouth to give a message.

**Signs Announce Jerusalem's Coming Siege (Ezekiel 4:1–7:27)** — God directs Ezekiel to act out Jerusalem's siege, lie on his side for Israel and Judah's years of sin, and eat restricted food as a sign of famine. Shaving his head and dividing the hair portrays death, scattering, and a small remnant, while God declares that idolatry and violence have brought the end near.

Back in Jerusalem, the city still stands, and people think it will never fall. So God tells Ezekiel to act out its coming siege. He draws a map of Jerusalem on a clay brick and builds tiny siege walls and battering rams around it, with an iron pan as a wall between himself and the city. Then he lies on his left side for 390 days for the sins of Israel and on his right side for 40 days for Judah, “each day for a year.” He eats small, rationed meals of bread and water, as people will during a famine. God tells him to bake his bread over human dung, a picture of the unclean food the exiles will eat. When Ezekiel protests, “Ah Lord GOD! behold, my soul hath not been polluted,” God lets him use cow dung instead.

Next God tells him to shave his head and beard with a sword and divide the hair into three piles. He burns one third, chops one third with the sword, and scatters one third to the wind. A few hairs he tucks into his robe, and even some of those he burns. “This is Jerusalem,” God says. A third of its people will die of disease and famine, a third by the sword, and a third will be scattered.

God condemns the idols on the mountains of Israel and repeats a phrase found more than sixty times in Ezekiel: “ye shall know that I am the LORD.” Then comes the alarm: “An end, the end is come upon the four corners of the land.” Money will not help: “their silver and their gold shall not be able to deliver them in the day of the wrath of the LORD.”

**God's Glory Reveals Jerusalem's Idolatry (Ezekiel 8:1–11:25)** — In visions of Jerusalem, Ezekiel sees hidden idol worship in the temple and leaders leading the people astray. God marks those who grieve over the city's sins, brings judgment on the rest, and his glory departs from the temple, while promising that he will later gather and give his people a new heart and spirit.

A year later, while the elders of Judah are sitting in Ezekiel's house, God takes him “by a lock of mine head” and carries him in a vision to the temple in Jerusalem. There God shows him what is really going on. At the north gate stands an idol called “the image of jealousy.” Inside a hidden room, seventy elders are burning incense to pictures of animals and creeping things painted on the walls. They say, “The LORD seeth us not; the LORD hath forsaken the earth.” At the gate “there sat women weeping for Tammuz,” a pagan god. And in the inner court, about twenty-five men stand “with their backs toward the temple of the LORD,” bowing to the sun.

God sends a man dressed in linen through the city to “set a mark upon the foreheads of the men that sigh and that cry for all the abominations.” Then six executioners are sent to strike down everyone without the mark, “and begin at my sanctuary.”

Then Ezekiel sees something terrible. God's glory, which has lived in the temple since Solomon's day, begins to leave. It rises from the cherubim to the doorway, then moves to the east gate, and finally “went up from the midst of the city, and stood upon the mountain which is on the east side of the city.” God has left his house. Yet he promises the exiles that he himself will be “a little sanctuary” for them in the countries where they are scattered, and that one day, “I will take the stony heart out of their flesh, and will give them an heart of flesh.”

**Judgment Reaches Kings and People (Ezekiel 12:1–19:14)** — Ezekiel acts out exile and announces that Zedekiah will be captured. God rejects false prophets, teaches that each person is accountable for sin, and declares that even righteous people cannot save a corrupt land. Parables of a useless vine, an unfaithful wife, two eagles, and lions expose Jerusalem's rebellion and the fall of its rulers.

God tells Ezekiel to pack a bag as if he is going into exile, dig through the wall of his house in the evening, and leave with his face covered. It is a sign that King Zedekiah will try to escape from Jerusalem at night. He will be captured and taken to Babylon, “yet shall he not see it, though he shall die there.” That came true when the Babylonians blinded him.

False prophets keep promising safety: “Peace; and there was no peace.” They are like workers who cover a weak wall with whitewash so it looks strong. When God's storm comes, it will fall. God says Jerusalem is so sinful that even “Noah, Daniel, and Job” could save only themselves there.

In chapter 16, God tells the story of Jerusalem as an abandoned baby girl. he found her lying in a field, “polluted in thine own blood,” and said to her, “Live.” he raised her, married her, and gave her beautiful jewels and clothes. “But thou didst trust in thine own beauty, and playedst the harlot,” chasing other gods and even sacrificing her own children. She has become worse than Sodom, whose sin was “pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness,” for Sodom did not “strengthen the hand of the poor and needy.” Yet God promises, “I will establish unto thee an everlasting covenant.”

In chapter 18, God corrects a popular saying among the exiles: “The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge.” They are blaming their ancestors for their troubles. But God says, “the soul that sinneth, it shall die.” Each person is responsible for his own choices, and anyone who turns from evil will live. “For I have no pleasure in the death of him that dieth, saith the Lord GOD: wherefore turn yourselves, and live ye.”

**Israel's Long Rebellion Brings the End (Ezekiel 20:1–24:27)** — God recounts Israel's repeated rebellion from Egypt through the wilderness and the land, yet preserves his name among the nations. Ezekiel warns that Babylon's sword is coming, exposes Jerusalem's bloodshed and idolatry, and announces the siege through the sign of a boiling pot and the death of his wife.

When some elders come to ask God a question, he answers, “I will not be enquired of by you.” he recounts their history of rebellion from Egypt to the present. he spared them only “for my name's sake.” Then Ezekiel sings a song about God's sword: “A sword, a sword is sharpened.” He describes the king of Babylon standing at a fork in the road, deciding whether to attack Ammon or Jerusalem. He uses fortune-telling, and the answer points to Jerusalem. God also announces that the crown will be taken from Zedekiah: “I will overturn, overturn, overturn, it: and it shall be no more, until he come whose right it is.”

Jerusalem is full of violence and injustice, from its princes and priests to its common people. God says, “I sought for a man among them, that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it: but I found none.”

Then, on the very day Babylon's army surrounds Jerusalem, God tells Ezekiel to write down the date. That same day, God says, “I take away from thee the desire of thine eyes with a stroke: yet neither shalt thou mourn nor weep.” Ezekiel writes, “at even my wife died; and I did in the morning as I was commanded.” When the people ask why he does not mourn, he explains that God is about to take away the temple, “the desire of your eyes,” and they will not mourn either, but will waste away in their sins. “Thus Ezekiel is unto you a sign.”

**God Judges Israel's Neighboring Nations (Ezekiel 25:1–28:26)** — Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, and Sidon are judged for pride, violence, and delight in Jerusalem's ruin. Tyre's wealth and trade cannot save it, and its ruler's exalted claims are brought down. God promises that Israel will dwell securely after judgment on the nations around it.

Ezekiel now turns to the nations around Israel. Ammon cheered when the temple was destroyed. Moab said Judah was no different from any other nation. Edom and the Philistines took revenge on Judah while it was weak. Each of them will be judged.

The longest message is against Tyre, a rich trading city on an island off the coast. Tyre celebrated Jerusalem's fall, hoping to profit from it. God says he will bring nations against Tyre like waves of the sea and scrape it bare: “I will make thee like the top of a rock: thou shalt be a place to spread nets upon.” In chapter 27, Ezekiel sings a funeral song for Tyre, describing it as a magnificent ship loaded with goods from all over the world, until a storm sinks it in the middle of the sea.

The ruler of Tyre is so proud that he says, “I am a God, I sit in the seat of God.” God answers, “yet thou art a man, and not God.” Then Ezekiel speaks to the king of Tyre in unusual, exalted language: “Thou hast been in Eden the garden of God,” “the anointed cherub that covereth,” “perfect in thy ways from the day that thou wast created, till iniquity was found in thee.” Many readers have seen in these words a picture of Satan's fall, behind the proud human king.

**Egypt and Pharaoh Are Brought Low (Ezekiel 29:1–32:32)** — Egypt is compared to a great river creature that boasts of the Nile, and Pharaoh is pictured as a fallen cedar and a lion caught in a net. Babylon will humble Egypt and its allies, showing that military strength and wealth cannot stand against God's judgment.

God speaks against Egypt and its Pharaoh, “the great dragon that lieth in the midst of his rivers, which hath said, My river is mine own.” Pharaoh thinks he made the Nile himself. God will drag him out like a crocodile with a hook in his jaws. Egypt was “a staff of reed” to Israel, a walking stick that snapped when Israel leaned on it. So Egypt will be humbled and become “the basest of the kingdoms,” the lowest of all.

In the last-dated message in the book, God explains that Nebuchadnezzar's army worked very hard to conquer Tyre but got no reward, so God will give him Egypt as payment instead. In chapter 31, God compares Egypt to Assyria, which was once like a towering cedar in Lebanon that God cut down. Egypt will fall the same way. In chapter 32, Ezekiel sings a funeral song for Pharaoh, who is pictured going down “into the pit” to join other fallen nations like Assyria, Elam, and Edom.

**Ezekiel Becomes Watchman Again (Ezekiel 33:1–33)** — After news arrives that Jerusalem has fallen, God renews Ezekiel's duty as a watchman. he teaches personal responsibility, calls the wicked to turn and live, rebukes people who hear the prophet's words without obeying them, and condemns survivors in Judah's ruins who claim the land while still sinning.

God reminds Ezekiel that he is a watchman. “If the watchman see the sword come, and blow not the trumpet, and the people be not warned,” then the watchman is responsible. God also tells the exiles, who feel crushed by their sins, “I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live: turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die, O house of Israel?”

Then the news Ezekiel has been waiting for arrives. A survivor escapes from Jerusalem and comes to him with the message, “The city is smitten.” Jerusalem has fallen. The night before, God had opened Ezekiel's mouth, “and I was no more dumb.” Meanwhile, the few people left in the ruins of Judah are claiming the land for themselves, while they keep sinning.

The exiles now crowd around Ezekiel to hear him, but they treat him like entertainment. “Thou art unto them as a very lovely song of one that hath a pleasant voice... for they hear thy words, but they do them not.” But God says, “when this cometh to pass... then shall they know that a prophet hath been among them.”

**God Promises a True Shepherd and Restored Land (Ezekiel 34:1–36:38)** — God condemns Israel's shepherds, its leaders, for feeding themselves instead of caring for the flock. he promises to seek his sheep and set one shepherd, his servant David, over them. Edom is judged for hostility, while Israel is promised cleansing, a new heart, a new spirit, restored land, and renewed fruitfulness.

Now that the judgment has come, Ezekiel's messages turn to hope. First God condemns Israel's leaders, whom he calls shepherds: “Woe be to the shepherds of Israel that do feed themselves! should not the shepherds feed the flocks?” They did not care for the sick, bandage the injured, or look for the lost, “but with force and with cruelty have ye ruled them.” So God will take over: “Behold, I, even I, will both search my sheep, and seek them out.” “I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away.” he will set over them “one shepherd... even my servant David,” and send “showers of blessing.” Jesus later called himself the good shepherd.

God promises to judge Edom for its hatred and to make the mountains of Israel fruitful again. he will bring his people home, not because they deserve it, but for his own name's sake. Then he makes a wonderful promise: “Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean... A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my spirit within you.” The ruined land will become so beautiful that people will say, “This land that was desolate is become like the garden of Eden.”

**Dry Bones Live and Israel Is Reunited (Ezekiel 37:1–28)** — In a valley of dry bones, God shows Ezekiel that he can restore the whole house of Israel from hopeless exile. Breath enters the bones, and they become a great army. Two joined sticks portray Judah and Israel becoming one nation under one king from David's line, with God's sanctuary among them.

The Spirit of the LORD carries Ezekiel to a valley “full of bones,” and “lo, they were very dry.” They have been there a long time. God asks him, “Son of man, can these bones live? And I answered, O Lord GOD, thou knowest.” God tells him to preach to the bones: “O ye dry bones, hear the word of the LORD.”

As Ezekiel prophesies, “there was a noise, and behold a shaking, and the bones came together, bone to his bone.” Sinews and muscles grow on them, and skin covers them. But they are still dead. Then God tells him to call to the wind: “Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe upon these slain, that they may live.” “And the breath came into them, and they lived, and stood up upon their feet, an exceeding great army.”

God explains, “these bones are the whole house of Israel.” In exile, the people are saying, “Our bones are dried, and our hope is lost.” But God promises, “I will open your graves... and bring you into the land of Israel.” Then God tells Ezekiel to take two sticks. On one he writes Judah, and on the other Joseph, meaning the northern kingdom. When he holds them together, “they shall become one in thine hand.” The divided nation will be united again, “no more two nations,” under one king from David's line. God will make “an everlasting covenant with them,” and set his sanctuary among them forever.

**Gog Attacks but God Defends His People (Ezekiel 38:1–39:29)** — Ezekiel prophesies against Gog of Magog, who will come against a restored people living securely. God himself defeats the invading forces, displays his holiness among the nations, and promises that Israel will know him after he gathers and restores them.

Ezekiel describes a great future battle. A ruler called Gog, from the land of Magog, will gather a huge army from the north, with Persia, Ethiopia, Libya, and others, to attack Israel “in the latter days.” Israel will be living peacefully in “the land of unwalled villages,” with no walls or gates to protect them.

But God is in control the whole time. he says he is the one bringing Gog, “that the heathen may know me.” God will shake the land with a great earthquake, throw the invading army into confusion so that “every man's sword shall be against his brother,” and send “great hailstones, fire, and brimstone” from heaven. The invaders will fall on the mountains of Israel.

It will take Israel seven years to burn the enemy's weapons as firewood and seven months to bury all the dead. God will no longer hide his face from his people, “for I have poured out my spirit upon the house of Israel.” Christians have understood this battle in different ways, and the book of Revelation mentions Gog and Magog in its final battle.

**Ezekiel Is Shown a Future Temple (Ezekiel 40:1–43:27)** — In the twenty-fifth year of exile, Ezekiel is brought in a vision to Israel and shown a carefully measured temple, its courts, gates, rooms, altar, and sacred boundaries. The glory of the Lord returns from the east and fills the temple, and God gives instructions for the altar and offerings.

Fourteen years after Jerusalem fell, God takes Ezekiel in a vision to a very high mountain in Israel. There he meets a man who shines like bronze, holding a measuring rod and a linen measuring line. The man tells him, “behold with thine eyes, and hear with thine ears,” and report everything to Israel. Then he gives Ezekiel a tour of a new temple, measuring each gate, room, and courtyard, and finally the most holy place.

Then comes the moment the whole book has been building toward. Years before, Ezekiel watched God's glory leave the temple through the east gate. Now he sees it return the same way: “the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east: and his voice was like a noise of many waters: and the earth shined with his glory.” The glory enters through the east gate, “and, behold, the glory of the LORD filled the house.” God says this will be “the place of my throne... where I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel for ever.” he tells Ezekiel to describe the temple to the people, so they will be ashamed of their sins. Then God gives instructions for the altar and the sacrifices that will be offered there.

**Worship Is Ordered for a Holy Community (Ezekiel 44:1–46:24)** — God sets apart the eastern gate and gives duties for priests, Levites, the prince, and the people. Worship must reject former idolatry and honor God's holiness. Instructions govern offerings, Sabbaths, feasts, the prince's responsibilities, land, and sacred spaces.

Because God's glory entered through the east gate, that gate must stay shut forever: “This gate shall be shut, it shall not be opened, and no man shall enter in by it; because the LORD, the God of Israel, hath entered in by it.” Only the prince may sit there to eat before the LORD.

God gives rules for who may serve in the new temple. The Levites who led the people into idolatry will be demoted to gatekeepers and helpers. Only the priests descended from Zadok, who stayed faithful when others went astray, may come near God's altar. Their job is to teach God's people “the difference between the holy and profane.”

A special area of land will be set aside for the temple, the priests, the Levites, and the prince. The prince must rule justly and stop oppressing the people: “remove violence and spoil, and execute judgment and justice.” He must use honest weights and measures. Ezekiel also describes the offerings for the feasts and Sabbaths. When the people come to worship, those who enter by the north gate must leave by the south gate, and those who enter by the south must leave by the north, so everyone moves forward through God's house.

**The River Brings Life and the Land Is Divided (Ezekiel 47:1–48:29)** — Water flowing from the temple grows into a deep river that brings life wherever it goes, including healing for the Dead Sea. Ezekiel then describes the renewed land's boundaries and portions for the tribes, the sanctuary, priests, Levites, and prince.

The man brings Ezekiel back to the temple door, and he sees water flowing out from under the threshold toward the east. The man measures about a third of a mile, and the water is ankle-deep. Another third of a mile, and it is up to Ezekiel's knees. Then it reaches his waist. Then it becomes “a river that I could not pass over: for the waters were risen, waters to swim in.”

The river flows down to the Dead Sea, which is so salty that nothing can live in it. But this water heals it. “Every thing shall live whither the river cometh,” and fishermen will spread their nets along its shores. On both banks grow fruit trees whose leaves never wither, bearing new fruit every month, “and the leaf thereof for medicine.” The book of Revelation describes a river and trees like this in the New Jerusalem.

Then God gives the boundaries of the land and divides it among the twelve tribes in strips from north to south. Even foreigners who live among them and have children there will receive land, as if they were born Israelites. In the center is the holy portion for the temple, the priests, the Levites, the city, and the prince.

**The Restored City Is Called The Lord Is There (Ezekiel 48:30–35)** — The final vision names the city's twelve gates after Israel's tribes and gives the restored city its closing name: THE LORD IS THERE. The book ends with God's presence, holiness, judgment, restoration, and his faithfulness to his scattered people held together.

The new city has twelve gates, three on each side, and each gate is named after one of the tribes of Israel. The book of Revelation describes the New Jerusalem the same way. Then Ezekiel gives the city its new name, the last words of his book: “and the name of the city from that day shall be, The LORD is there.” Ezekiel began by watching God's glory leave Jerusalem because of the people's sin. He ends with a promise that God will live among his people forever.

# Daniel
When it happened: From the third year of King Jehoiakim (605 BC; Daniel 1:1) to the third year of Cyrus king of Persia (about 536 BC; Daniel 10:1).
When it was written: Chapters 7–12 record Daniel's visions in his own voice. Many scholars date the book's final form to the 160s BC, during the persecution under the Greek king Antiochus IV. Daniel 2:4–7:28 is written in Aramaic rather than Hebrew.
Where: Babylon, the palace at Shushan (Daniel 8:2), and the bank of the river Hiddekel, the Tigris (Daniel 10:4).
The world at the time: The book spans the Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar and its conquest by the Medes and Persians under Darius the Median and Cyrus.

**Daniel and His Friends Remain Faithful in Babylon (Daniel 1:1–21)** — Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon carries young people from Judah into exile, including Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. They are trained for the king's court and given Babylonian names, but Daniel asks not to defile himself with the royal food. God gives the four men knowledge and skill, and Daniel receives understanding in visions and dreams.

In 605 BC, King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon attacks Jerusalem and carries off some of the treasures from God's temple. He also takes some of the best young men from Judah's royal and noble families back to Babylon. They are handsome, smart, and quick to learn, and they will be trained for three years in Babylonian language and literature to serve in the king's palace. Among them are four friends: Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. The chief official gives them Babylonian names: Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego.

The king orders them to eat his rich food and drink his wine. “But Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the portion of the king's meat, nor with the wine which he drank.” The food may have been offered to idols or included meat that God's law called unclean. The official likes Daniel, but he is afraid the king will have him killed if the young men look unhealthy. So Daniel suggests a test: “Prove thy servants, I beseech thee, ten days; and let them give us pulse to eat, and water to drink.” Pulse means vegetables.

After ten days, the four friends look healthier than all the young men who ate the king's food. So they are allowed to keep their diet. “God gave them knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom: and Daniel had understanding in all visions and dreams.” When their training ends, the king finds them “ten times better than all the magicians and astrologers” in his whole kingdom.

**God Reveals the King's Dream to Daniel (Daniel 2:1–49)** — Nebuchadnezzar demands that his wise men tell both his dream and its meaning, a task they cannot perform. Daniel and his friends pray, and God reveals the mystery: a great image represents a sequence of kingdoms, while a stone made without hands becomes a kingdom that will never be destroyed. The king honors Daniel's God and promotes Daniel and his friends.

King Nebuchadnezzar has a dream that troubles him so much he cannot sleep. He calls his magicians and wise men and makes an impossible demand. They must tell him not only what the dream means, but what the dream was, or “ye shall be cut in pieces, and your houses shall be made a dunghill.” They protest that no one on earth can do that, only “the gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh.” The king is furious and orders every wise man in Babylon executed, including Daniel and his friends.

Daniel asks for time and asks his three friends to pray with him. That night, God shows Daniel the secret in a vision. Daniel praises God: “he removeth kings, and setteth up kings... He revealeth the deep and secret things.” Then he goes to the king and says, “there is a God in heaven that revealeth secrets.”

The king had seen “a great image,” a huge statue. “This image's head was of fine gold, his breast and his arms of silver, his belly and his thighs of brass, His legs of iron, his feet part of iron and part of clay.” Then “a stone was cut out without hands.” It struck the statue's feet and smashed the whole thing into dust, which blew away like chaff. “And the stone that smote the image became a great mountain, and filled the whole earth.”

Daniel explains. “Thou art this head of gold.” After Nebuchadnezzar's kingdom, other kingdoms will rise, each weaker than the one before. Many interpreters see them as Persia, Greece, and Rome. “And in the days of these kings shall the God of heaven set up a kingdom, which shall never be destroyed.” The king falls on his face before Daniel and says, “your God is a God of gods, and a Lord of kings.” He makes Daniel ruler over the whole province of Babylon and puts Daniel's three friends in charge of its affairs.

**Three Hebrews Are Delivered from the Furnace (Daniel 3:1–30)** — Nebuchadnezzar sets up a golden image and commands worship. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse, trusting God even if he does not deliver them. They are thrown into a blazing furnace, where a fourth figure appears with them; they emerge unharmed, and the king acknowledges the power of their God.

Nebuchadnezzar builds a gold statue ninety feet tall on the plain of Dura. He orders all his officials to come to its dedication. When the band plays, everyone must bow down and worship it, “And whoso falleth not down and worshippeth shall the same hour be cast into the midst of a burning fiery furnace.” The music plays, and everyone bows, except three Jewish men.

Some officials report Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego to the king. In a rage, Nebuchadnezzar gives them one more chance. But they answer calmly, “our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up.” Even if God does not save them, they will not bow.

The king is so angry that he orders the furnace heated “seven times more than it was wont to be heated.” It is so hot that the soldiers who throw the three men in are killed by the flames. Then the king jumps up in amazement: “Did not we cast three men bound into the midst of the fire?” “Lo, I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God.”

The king calls them to come out. The officials crowd around and see that the fire did not touch them. Not a hair was singed, their clothes were not scorched, and they did not even smell like smoke. Nebuchadnezzar says, “Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who hath sent his angel, and delivered his servants that trusted in him.” He makes a law that no one may speak against their God, “because there is no other God that can deliver after this sort,” and he promotes them.

**Nebuchadnezzar Learns Who Rules the Kingdoms (Daniel 4:1–37)** — Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a great tree cut down until he learns that the Most High rules human kingdoms. Daniel warns him to turn from sin, but the king later boasts in his power and is driven from people until he recognizes God's authority. His sanity and kingdom are restored when he praises the King of heaven.

This chapter is written by Nebuchadnezzar himself, as a letter to the whole world. He tells of a dream about a huge tree that reached to the sky and fed every creature. Then a holy messenger came down from heaven and shouted, “Hew down the tree.” Only a stump would be left, bound with iron and bronze. The message was “to the intent that the living may know that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men, and giveth it to whomsoever he will.”

Daniel is upset by what the dream means, but he tells the king the truth. The tree is Nebuchadnezzar. He will be driven away from people and will live like an animal, eating grass like cattle, until he learns that God rules over every kingdom. Daniel urges him to turn from his sins and show mercy to the poor, so that his peace might continue.

A year later, the king is walking on the roof of his palace, looking out over his magnificent city. He says, “Is not this great Babylon, that I have built... by the might of my power, and for the honour of my majesty?” “While the word was in the king's mouth,” a voice from heaven says, “The kingdom is departed from thee.” Right away, he loses his mind. He is driven out and eats grass like an ox. His body is wet with dew, “till his hairs were grown like eagles' feathers, and his nails like birds' claws.”

At the end of the appointed time, he looks up to heaven, “and mine understanding returned unto me, and I blessed the most High.” God gives him back his kingdom. He ends his letter, “Now I Nebuchadnezzar praise and extol and honour the King of heaven... and those that walk in pride he is able to abase.”

**Belshazzar Is Judged at His Feast (Daniel 5:1–31)** — Belshazzar uses vessels taken from Jerusalem's temple while praising idols. A hand writes a message on the wall, and Daniel explains that God has numbered the kingdom, weighed the king, and given Babylon to the Medes and Persians. Belshazzar is killed that night.

Many years later, Belshazzar is ruling Babylon. He throws a huge feast for a thousand of his nobles. Drunk on wine, he orders the gold and silver cups that Nebuchadnezzar took from God's temple in Jerusalem. He and his guests drink from them and “praised the gods of gold, and of silver, of brass, of iron, of wood, and of stone.”

Suddenly the fingers of a human hand appear and begin writing on the plaster wall. The king turns pale, and his knees knock together. None of his wise men can read the writing. Then the queen remembers Daniel, now an old man, and he is brought in. The king offers him purple robes, a gold chain, and a high position. Daniel answers, “Let thy gifts be to thyself, and give thy rewards to another; yet I will read the writing unto the king.”

Daniel reminds Belshazzar how God humbled Nebuchadnezzar. “And thou his son, O Belshazzar, hast not humbled thine heart, though thou knewest all this.” He has praised gods that cannot see or hear, and he has not honored the God who holds his very breath in his hand. Then Daniel reads the words: “MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN.” “MENE; God hath numbered thy kingdom, and finished it. TEKEL; Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting. PERES; Thy kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and Persians.” That very night, the Medes and Persians capture Babylon. “In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain.”

**Daniel Is Delivered from the Lions' Den (Daniel 6:1–28)** — Under Darius, Daniel's enemies use his faithfulness in prayer to trap him under a royal decree. Daniel continues praying to God and is thrown into a den of lions, but God shuts the lions' mouths. Darius recognizes God's power, while Daniel prospers during the reigns of Darius and Cyrus.

The new king, Darius, puts Daniel in charge of the whole kingdom along with two others. Daniel does so well that the king plans to put him over everyone. The other officials are jealous and look for something to accuse him of, but they can find nothing, “forasmuch as he was faithful.” They say, “We shall not find any occasion against this Daniel, except we find it against him concerning the law of his God.”

So they trick the king into signing a law that for thirty days, anyone who prays to any god or man except the king will be thrown into a den of lions. Under “the law of the Medes and Persians,” a law could never be changed once it was signed. Daniel knows about the law. But he goes home, where his windows open toward Jerusalem, and “he kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime.”

The officials catch him praying and report him. The king is very upset and tries all day to find a way to save Daniel, but he cannot. As Daniel is thrown into the den, the king says, “Thy God whom thou servest continually, he will deliver thee.” A stone is placed over the opening, and the king seals it. That night the king cannot eat or sleep.

At dawn, he rushes to the den and calls out, “O Daniel, servant of the living God, is thy God, whom thou servest continually, able to deliver thee from the lions?” Daniel answers, “My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions' mouths, that they have not hurt me.” The king is overjoyed. Daniel is lifted out without a scratch, “because he believed in his God.” Then the men who accused Daniel are thrown into the den with their families, and the lions attack them before they reach the bottom. King Darius sends a decree to the whole world that people must “tremble and fear before the God of Daniel: for he is the living God.”

**Daniel Sees Four Kingdoms and the Son of Man (Daniel 7:1–28)** — Daniel sees four beasts rising from the sea, followed by a heavenly court where the Ancient of days judges the arrogant power. One like the Son of man receives everlasting dominion. The vision explains that earthly kingdoms will pass away, while the saints of the Most High will receive the kingdom.

The second half of Daniel records visions God gave him. This first vision came during the reign of Belshazzar. Daniel sees “four great beasts came up from the sea.” The first is like a lion with eagle's wings. The second is like a bear with three ribs in its mouth. The third is like a leopard with four wings and four heads. The fourth is the most terrifying of all, “dreadful and terrible, and strong exceedingly,” with “great iron teeth” and ten horns. Then a little horn grows among them, with human eyes and “a mouth speaking great things.”

Then Daniel sees thrones set up in heaven, “and the Ancient of days did sit, whose garment was white as snow, and the hair of his head like the pure wool.” Fire streams out before him, and millions of angels serve him. “The judgment was set, and the books were opened.” The terrible beast is destroyed.

Then Daniel sees “one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven,” coming before the Ancient of days. He is given “dominion, and glory, and a kingdom, that all people, nations, and languages, should serve him: his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away.” Jesus often called himself “the Son of man,” and he used these words at his trial (Mark 14:62).

An angel explains that the four beasts are four kingdoms. The little horn is a king who “shall speak great words against the most High, and shall wear out the saints.” But his power will be taken away, “And the kingdom and dominion... shall be given to the people of the saints of the most High.”

**The Ram, the Goat, and the Coming Conflict (Daniel 8:1–27)** — Daniel sees a ram defeated by a swift goat whose great horn is broken and replaced. A later arrogant ruler opposes the holy people and interrupts the sanctuary service for a time. Gabriel explains that the vision concerns future kingdoms and a period of severe trouble.

Two years later, Daniel has another vision. He sees a ram with two horns, one longer than the other, charging in every direction. No animal can stand against it. Then a male goat with one large horn between its eyes races in from the west so fast that its feet do not touch the ground. It smashes the ram and breaks its horns. But at the height of its power, the goat's great horn breaks off, and four horns grow in its place. From one of them comes a little horn that grows very powerful. It attacks God's people, stops the daily sacrifices at the temple, and throws truth to the ground.

Daniel hears an angel ask how long this will last. The answer: “Unto two thousand and three hundred days; then shall the sanctuary be cleansed.” Then the angel Gabriel, named here for the first time in the Bible, explains: “The ram which thou sawest having two horns are the kings of Media and Persia. And the rough goat is the king of Grecia.” The great horn is its first king, Alexander the Great. After him, his empire splits into four. Later, a fierce king will arise who destroys many, but “he shall be broken without hand,” destroyed by God. Many identify him with Antiochus IV, a Greek king who desecrated the temple in 167 BC. Daniel is so overwhelmed that he is sick for days.

**Daniel Prays and Receives a Promise of Restoration (Daniel 9:1–27)** — While reading Jeremiah's prophecy of seventy years, Daniel confesses Israel's sin and asks God to restore Jerusalem and its sanctuary. Gabriel brings a message concerning seventy weeks, speaking of Jerusalem's rebuilding, an anointed one, coming desolation, and an appointed end; the passage's details have received differing interpretations.

In the first year of King Darius, Daniel is reading the book of Jeremiah. He learns that Jerusalem's ruin would last seventy years, and he realizes that time is almost up. So he turns to God in prayer, “with fasting, and sackcloth, and ashes.” He confesses the sins of his people: “We have sinned, and have committed iniquity, and have done wickedly, and have rebelled.” He does not ask God to act because Israel deserves it, but because of God's mercy: “for we do not present our supplications before thee for our righteousnesses, but for thy great mercies. O Lord, hear; O Lord, forgive.”

While Daniel is still praying, the angel Gabriel comes to him, “being caused to fly swiftly,” and says, “thou art greatly beloved.” He gives Daniel a message about seventy weeks, or seventy sevens of years. “Seventy weeks are determined upon thy people and upon thy holy city, to finish the transgression, and to make an end of sins, and to make reconciliation for iniquity, and to bring in everlasting righteousness.” Jerusalem will be rebuilt. Then “shall Messiah be cut off, but not for himself.” Later, the city and temple will be destroyed. Christians have often understood this as a prophecy of Christ's death and the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, though its exact meaning has been debated for centuries.

**Daniel Is Strengthened for His Final Vision (Daniel 10:1–11:35)** — Daniel mourns and fasts before receiving a vision near the Tigris River. A heavenly messenger strengthens him and describes conflict involving Persia, Greece, and later rulers. The vision includes a contemptible king who acts deceitfully, profanes the sanctuary, and persecutes those who know their God.

In the third year of Cyrus king of Persia, Daniel spends three weeks mourning and fasting. Then, standing by the Tigris River, he looks up and sees a man dressed in linen with a gold belt. His body shines like a jewel, his face like lightning, his eyes like flaming torches, and his voice is like the roar of a crowd. Daniel's companions run and hide in terror. Daniel's strength leaves him, and he falls to the ground.

A hand touches him and lifts him up. The messenger says, “O Daniel, a man greatly beloved... Fear not, Daniel: for from the first day that thou didst set thine heart to understand... thy words were heard.” He explains that he was delayed for twenty-one days because “the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me,” until Michael, one of the chief angels, came to help him. This shows that there are spiritual battles behind the events of history.

Then the messenger describes the future in great detail. More kings will rule Persia. Then a mighty king will rise in Greece, whose kingdom will be divided into four. Long wars will follow between the king of the north, Syria, and the king of the south, Egypt. Finally a “vile person” will take the throne by flattery and trickery. His forces will pollute the temple and “place the abomination that maketh desolate.” Jesus referred to this abomination in Matthew 24:15. But “the people that do know their God shall be strong, and do exploits.” Some faithful teachers will suffer and die, but their suffering will refine and purify God's people.

**The Final Conflict and the Promise of Resurrection (Daniel 11:36–12:13)** — The final vision describes a proud ruler and a time of unmatched trouble. Daniel is told that many who sleep in the dust will awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame, and that the wise will shine. The book ends with Daniel instructed to seal the words until the appointed time and promised rest and a final inheritance.

The vision continues. A king will arise who “shall exalt himself, and magnify himself above every god, and shall speak marvellous things against the God of gods.” He will succeed for a while, but in the end, “he shall come to his end, and none shall help him.”

Then comes the last great event. “And at that time shall Michael stand up, the great prince which standeth for the children of thy people: and there shall be a time of trouble, such as never was since there was a nation even to that same time.” But God's people will be rescued, everyone whose name is written in the book. Then comes one of the clearest promises of resurrection in the Old Testament: “And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt. And they that be wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament; and they that turn many to righteousness as the stars for ever and ever.”

Daniel is told to “shut up the words, and seal the book, even to the time of the end.” He asks what the end will be, but he is told, “Go thy way, Daniel: for the words are closed up and sealed till the time of the end.” The book ends with a personal promise to the old prophet, who has served God faithfully in a foreign land for about seventy years: “for thou shalt rest, and stand in thy lot at the end of the days.”
