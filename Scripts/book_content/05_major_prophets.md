# Isaiah
When it happened: Isaiah prophesied "in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah" (Isaiah 1:1), roughly 740–700 BC. Chapters 40–66 speak to the people in Babylonian exile and name Cyrus of Persia (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1).
When it was written: Many scholars think chapters 40–66 come from later prophets in Isaiah's tradition during and after the exile; others hold that the whole book comes from Isaiah himself.
Where: Jerusalem and Judah, with prophecies concerning Assyria, Babylon, Egypt, Moab, Tyre, and other nations.
The world at the time: The Assyrian Empire was expanding westward. Syria and the northern kingdom of Israel attacked Judah under King Ahaz (Isaiah 7), Assyria destroyed Samaria in 722 BC, and in 701 BC Sennacherib invaded Judah and surrounded Jerusalem.

**God Calls Judah to Repent and Return (Isaiah 1:1–5:30)** — Isaiah warns Judah that religious ceremonies cannot replace obedience. God calls His people to reject injustice, defend the vulnerable, and return to Him, while promising forgiveness and warning that pride, idolatry, oppression, and corrupt leadership will bring judgment.

Isaiah opens with God's lament that “The ox knoweth his owner,” but Israel does not know Him. Their sacrifices and feasts have become a burden to God while their hands are full of blood, so He calls them to “learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.” Then comes the invitation: “Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow.” Isaiah sees a day when nations will stream to the Lord's house and “they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” In the song of the vineyard, God's carefully tended vine yields only wild grapes, and six woes follow, including “Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil.”

**Isaiah Sees God's Holiness and Receives His Calling (Isaiah 6:1–13)** — Isaiah sees the Lord enthroned in holiness, confesses his uncleanness, receives cleansing from the altar, and accepts a difficult mission to a people who will largely refuse to understand. Judgment will leave the land desolate, but a holy remnant will remain.

“In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up,” Isaiah writes. Seraphim cried to one another, “Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory,” and the house filled with smoke. Isaiah cried, “Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips.” A seraph touched his mouth with a live coal from the altar, and his iniquity was taken away. When the Lord asked, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” Isaiah answered, “Here am I; send me.” He was warned that the people would hear without understanding until the land was desolate, yet a holy seed would remain like the stump of a felled tree.

**God Promises a Sign During Judah's Crisis (Isaiah 7:1–9:7)** — During threats from Syria and northern Israel, Isaiah calls Ahaz to trust God. He announces Immanuel as a sign, warns of Assyria, and points to a future child and righteous ruler whose reign will bring light, justice, and peace.

When Syria and Israel marched against Jerusalem, the heart of King Ahaz shook like trees in the wind. Isaiah met him with his son Shearjashub, whose name means the remnant shall return, and warned, “If ye will not believe, surely ye shall not be established.” When Ahaz refused to ask for a sign, God gave one: “Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel,” meaning God with us, a verse Matthew applies to the birth of Jesus. Isaiah named his own son Mahershalalhashbaz as a sign that Assyria would soon plunder Damascus and Samaria. Beyond the coming darkness, “The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light,” for “unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.”

**Israel's Pride and Assyria's Arrogance Face Judgment (Isaiah 9:8–10:34)** — God condemns northern Israel's pride, injustice, and persistent rebellion. Assyria will be used as an instrument of judgment, but its king's cruelty and arrogance will also be judged; a remnant will return to the Lord.

Four times a refrain rings out over the northern kingdom: “For all this his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still.” Israel's pride, corrupt leaders, and unjust laws that rob the poor bring one blow after another. Then God turns to Assyria: “O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger.” Assyria was only a tool in God's hand, yet its king boasted of his own strength, and Isaiah asks, “Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith?” Assyria's forest of soldiers will be cut down, and “The remnant shall return” to the mighty God.

**A Righteous King Brings Justice and Peace (Isaiah 11:1–12:6)** — A shoot from Jesse's family will rule through the Spirit of the Lord, judging with righteousness and defending the oppressed. Isaiah pictures peace, the gathering of scattered Israel and Judah, and joyful praise for God's salvation.

“And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots.” The Spirit of the Lord will rest on Him, the spirit of wisdom, counsel, might, knowledge, and the fear of the Lord, and He will judge the poor with righteousness. In His kingdom “The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid,” and “a little child shall lead them,” “for the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea.” The Lord will gather His scattered people a second time, and they will sing, “with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation.”

**Babylon and Other Nations Receive Warnings of Judgment (Isaiah 13:1–17:14)** — Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, and northern Israel receive warnings that pride, cruelty, and reliance on earthly power cannot escape God's judgment. Even so, a remnant will turn toward its Maker.

Isaiah announces that Babylon, the glory of kingdoms, will be overthrown like Sodom and Gomorrah and never be inhabited again. In a taunt against the king of Babylon, the dead greet him in the grave, and Isaiah cries, “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” mocking a ruler who wanted to ascend above the stars of God. The Lord swears that Assyria will be broken in His land. Philistia, Moab, and Damascus hear their judgments as well; Isaiah weeps over Moab's ruin, and Damascus becomes a ruinous heap.

**God's Authority Reaches Every Nation (Isaiah 18:1–23:18)** — Messages to Ethiopia, Egypt, Babylon, Arabia, Jerusalem, and Tyre show that wealth, trade, military power, and national pride remain under God's authority. Egypt and Assyria are ultimately pictured sharing God's blessing with Israel.

Egypt's idols tremble and its wise counselors become foolish, and God told Isaiah to walk naked and barefoot for three years as a sign that Assyria would lead Egypt and Ethiopia away captive. Yet Isaiah foresees an altar to the Lord in Egypt and a highway joining Egypt and Assyria in worship, when God will say, “Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel mine inheritance.” A watchman cries that Babylon is fallen. In Jerusalem's valley of vision, the people feast instead of repenting, saying, “let us eat and drink; for to morrow we shall die.” Tyre, the great merchant city, will be laid waste for seventy years.

**The Whole Earth Faces Judgment and Restoration (Isaiah 24:1–27:13)** — Isaiah expands the vision to worldwide judgment for human unfaithfulness, while also promising praise, salvation, victory over death, peace for those who trust God, and the gathering of scattered Israelites to worship in Jerusalem.

Because the earth's inhabitants have broken the everlasting covenant, the earth reels like a drunkard under God's judgment. Yet on Mount Zion the Lord will make a feast for all peoples: “He will swallow up death in victory; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from off all faces,” words Paul and Revelation take up. Judah will sing, “Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee,” and even declares, “Thy dead men shall live.” In that day the Lord will punish leviathan, the sea monster, and gather His people from Assyria and Egypt to worship in Jerusalem.

**Judah Is Warned Against False Security (Isaiah 28:1–31:9)** — Isaiah rebukes drunken leaders, hypocritical worship, and Judah's trust in alliances with Egypt. God offers Zion a sure foundation and calls His people to return, rest, and trust Him rather than horses, chariots, and political schemes.

Drunken priests and prophets mock Isaiah's teaching as baby talk, “precept upon precept; line upon line,” so God will speak to them through foreign tongues. Yet He promises, “Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation,” a verse Peter and Paul apply to Christ. God says of Judah, “this people draw near me with their mouth, and with their lips do honour me, but have removed their heart far from me,” words Jesus quoted. Isaiah warns, “Woe to them that go down to Egypt for help,” for God had said, “In returning and rest shall ye be saved; in quietness and in confidence shall be your strength,” and promised that His people would hear a word behind them saying, “This is the way, walk ye in it.”

**God Promises Righteous Rule and Renewal (Isaiah 32:1–35:10)** — A righteous king and just leaders will bring protection and clarity. After warnings to the complacent and judgment on hostile powers, Isaiah promises the Spirit's renewal, healing, a highway of holiness, and joyful return for the redeemed.

“Behold, a king shall reign in righteousness,” Isaiah declares, though the land will lie desolate until the Spirit is poured out from on high and the wilderness becomes a fruitful field. After woes on the complacent women of Jerusalem and on the destroyer, and judgment on Edom, Isaiah paints the return of the redeemed: “the desert shall rejoice, and blossom as the rose.” “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped,” and a highway will run through the desert: “it shall be called The way of holiness.” “The ransomed of the LORD shall return, and come to Zion with songs,” and sorrow and sighing shall flee away.

**Hezekiah Trusts God During Assyria's Invasion (Isaiah 36:1–37:38)** — When Sennacherib threatens Jerusalem, Hezekiah seeks the Lord and asks Isaiah to pray. God promises that Assyria will not capture the city; the angel of the Lord strikes the invading camp, and Sennacherib returns to Nineveh.

These chapters closely parallel 2 Kings 18–19. The Rabshakeh stood by the conduit of the upper pool and mocked Hezekiah's trust in God, telling the people that no god had saved any nation from Assyria. Hezekiah tore his clothes, sent to Isaiah, and spread Sennacherib's letter before the Lord, praying, “thou art the God, even thou alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth.” God answered through Isaiah, “For I will defend this city to save it for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake.” That night the angel of the Lord struck 185,000 Assyrians, and Sennacherib went home to Nineveh, where his sons killed him.

**Hezekiah Receives Healing and a Warning (Isaiah 38:1–39:8)** — God extends Hezekiah's life by fifteen years after his prayer and gives a confirming sign. After Hezekiah shows Babylonian envoys his treasures, Isaiah warns that Judah's wealth and some of its descendants will one day be taken to Babylon.

When Hezekiah was sick to death, he turned his face to the wall and prayed, and God added fifteen years to his life, moving the shadow back ten degrees on the dial of Ahaz as a sign. Isaiah records a psalm Hezekiah wrote after his recovery, which praises God: “thou hast cast all my sins behind thy back.” When envoys came from Merodachbaladan of Babylon, Hezekiah showed them all his treasures, and Isaiah foretold that everything would one day be carried to Babylon. Hezekiah replied, “There shall be peace and truth in my days.”

**God Comforts His People and Reveals His Greatness (Isaiah 40:1–42:25)** — God announces comfort after captivity, reveals His unmatched power, and promises to shepherd His people. He introduces a servant who will establish justice and be a light to the nations, while confronting Israel's blindness and disobedience.

The second half of Isaiah begins, “Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God,” for Jerusalem's warfare is accomplished and her iniquity pardoned. “The voice of him that crieth in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the LORD,” a verse all four Gospels apply to John the Baptist. All flesh is grass, but “the word of our God shall stand for ever.” God measures the waters in His hand, calls the stars by name, and “shall feed his flock like a shepherd,” and “they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint.” Then God introduces His servant: “Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth... A bruised reed shall he not break.”

**God Promises Redemption and the End of Captivity (Isaiah 43:1–45:25)** — God promises to redeem Israel, make a way through the wilderness, and blot out sin. He exposes idols as powerless and names Cyrus as the ruler appointed to restore Jerusalem and the temple, declaring that He alone is God and Savior.

God tells Israel, “When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee,” and, “Behold, I will do a new thing,” making a road in the wilderness. Though Israel had wearied Him with sins, He declares, “I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake.” Isaiah mocks the idol maker who burns half a tree to cook his meal and bows down to the other half as his god. Then God names the coming deliverer, Cyrus, long before his rise: “He is my shepherd,” and calls him “his anointed,” the one who will say to Jerusalem, “Thou shalt be built.” God invites, “Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth: for I am God, and there is none else,” and swears, “unto me every knee shall bow.”

**Babylon's Idols Fall Before God's Power (Isaiah 46:1–48:22)** — Babylon's gods are helpless burdens, unlike the Lord who carries Israel. Babylon's pride will be humbled, and God calls His stubborn people to listen, leave captivity, remember His redemption, and recognize that the wicked have no true peace.

“Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth,” as Babylon's gods are loaded on weary animals to be carried away. Unlike idols that must be carried, the Lord carries His people: “even to your old age I am he; and even to hoar hairs will I carry you.” The virgin daughter of Babylon, who said she would be a lady forever, must sit in the dust. God tells stubborn Israel, “I have chosen thee in the furnace of affliction,” and commands, “Go ye forth of Babylon,” but warns, “There is no peace, saith the LORD, unto the wicked.”

**God's Servant Brings Salvation Beyond Israel (Isaiah 49:1–50:11)** — God's servant is called to restore Israel and become a light so salvation reaches the ends of the earth. Zion is assured that God has not forgotten her, and the obedient servant trusts God through rejection and suffering.

The servant, called from the womb, hears God say that restoring Israel is too small a task: “I will also give thee for a light to the Gentiles, that thou mayest be my salvation unto the end of the earth.” When Zion complains that the Lord has forgotten her, God asks, “Can a woman forget her sucking child... yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands.” The servant speaks of his own obedience through suffering: “I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair: I hid not my face from shame and spitting.”

**God Promises Everlasting Salvation to Zion (Isaiah 51:1–52:12)** — God calls His people to remember Abraham, trust His enduring salvation, and awaken from judgment. Zion is promised comfort, freedom, restoration, and messengers declaring peace as the people depart Babylon under God's protection.

God calls those who seek Him to “look unto the rock whence ye are hewn,” to Abraham and Sarah, whom He blessed and increased. His salvation will outlast the heavens and the earth. Jerusalem, which has drunk the cup of God's fury, is told to awake, put on her strength, and shake herself from the dust. Watchmen lift up their voices together as the Lord returns to Zion, for “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace,” a verse Paul quotes about those who preach the gospel.

**The Suffering Servant Bears the Sins of Many (Isaiah 52:13–53:12)** — The servant is rejected, suffers innocently, and bears the sins of others like a lamb led to slaughter. God vindicates and exalts him, declaring that he makes intercession for transgressors and brings justification to many.

The servant's appearance is marred more than any man's, and kings shut their mouths at him. “He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief.” “But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.” Silent before his accusers, “he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter,” and he is buried with the rich though he had done no violence. Yet he will see his offspring and be satisfied, for “he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.” In Acts 8, Philip explains this passage to an Ethiopian official as speaking of Jesus.

**God Offers Mercy and an Everlasting Covenant (Isaiah 54:1–55:13)** — God promises Zion restoration, lasting compassion, protection, and an enduring covenant. He invites the thirsty to receive His provision freely, calls the wicked to return for mercy, and declares that His word will accomplish His purpose.

The barren woman is told to sing, for her children will be many, and God promises, “the mountains shall depart, and the hills be removed; but my kindness shall not depart from thee,” and, “No weapon that is formed against thee shall prosper.” Then comes the great invitation: “Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters,” to buy wine and milk without money. “Seek ye the LORD while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near,” for “my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD.” God's word, like rain that waters the earth, “shall not return unto me void.”

**True Worship Welcomes the Faithful and Demands Justice (Isaiah 56:1–58:14)** — God welcomes faithful outsiders and calls His house a house of prayer for all peoples. He condemns selfish leaders and fasting without mercy, declaring that true worship requires justice, care for the oppressed, generosity, and honoring the Sabbath.

Foreigners and eunuchs who hold fast to God's covenant are welcomed, “for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people,” words Jesus quoted when He cleansed the temple. God condemns blind watchmen, greedy shepherds, and idolatry, yet says, “I dwell in the high and holy place, with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit.” When the people complain that God ignores their fasting, He answers that they fast while quarreling and oppressing their workers: “Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the bands of wickedness,” to share bread with the hungry and shelter the poor. Those who “call the sabbath a delight” will ride on the high places of the earth.

**Sin Separates the People, but God Promises Redemption (Isaiah 59:1–21)** — Isaiah declares that injustice, violence, and lies have separated the people from God. When no human deliverer is found, the Lord Himself acts in righteousness, promises judgment on evil, and sends a Redeemer to those who turn from sin.

“Behold, the LORD'S hand is not shortened, that it cannot save; neither his ear heavy, that it cannot hear: But your iniquities have separated between you and your God.” Isaiah describes hands defiled with blood, lips speaking lies, and feet running to evil, until justice stands far off and truth is fallen in the street. Seeing no one to intervene, the Lord acts Himself: “he put on righteousness as a breastplate, and an helmet of salvation upon his head,” imagery Paul uses for the armor of God. “And the Redeemer shall come to Zion,” to those who turn from transgression, and God's Spirit and words will not depart from them.

**Zion's Light Draws the Nations (Isaiah 60:1–62:12)** — God promises that Zion will rise from darkness as nations bring their people and wealth to her. The anointed messenger brings good news, healing, liberty, and comfort; Jerusalem will be known for salvation, righteousness, and God's delight.

“Arise, shine; for thy light is come, and the glory of the LORD is risen upon thee.” Nations come to Zion's light, bringing her sons and daughters and their wealth, and the Lord Himself will be her everlasting light. Then the anointed one speaks: “The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek; he hath sent me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives,” the passage Jesus read in the synagogue at Nazareth, saying it was fulfilled that day. He gives mourners “beauty for ashes,” and Jerusalem, once called Forsaken, will be called Hephzibah and Beulah, for the Lord delights in her.

**God Judges Evil and Remembers His Mercy (Isaiah 63:1–64:12)** — Isaiah presents God's judgment against hostile evil, then remembers His kindness to Israel and pleads for mercy. The people confess sin, call God their Father and potter, and mourn Jerusalem and the burned temple.

A watchman asks, “Who is this that cometh from Edom, with dyed garments from Bozrah?” and the Lord answers that He has trodden the winepress of His wrath alone. Isaiah then recalls God's lovingkindness at the exodus: “In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them,” though they rebelled and grieved His Holy Spirit. The people pray for God to rend the heavens and come down, confessing, “we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags,” and pleading, “But now, O LORD, thou art our father; we are the clay, and thou our potter.” They mourn that the holy cities are a wilderness and the beautiful temple has been burned.

**God Promises New Heavens and a New Earth (Isaiah 65:1–25)** — God judges persistent rebellion but preserves servants who seek Him. He promises new heavens and a new earth marked by joy, long life, secure work, answered prayer, and peace throughout creation.

God says He was found by those who did not seek Him, while He spread out His hands all day to a rebellious people. He will judge the idolaters but preserve His servants, as new wine is kept in a cluster for the blessing in it. Then He announces, “For, behold, I create new heavens and a new earth: and the former shall not be remembered.” Jerusalem will be a joy, weeping will no more be heard, people will live in the houses they build and eat from the vineyards they plant, God will answer before they call, and “The wolf and the lamb shall feed together.”

**God's Glory Is Revealed Among All Nations (Isaiah 66:1–24)** — God rejects empty worship and honors the humble who tremble at His word. He promises Jerusalem's comfort and restoration, gathers nations to see His glory, and closes with the enduring contrast between universal worship and judgment for rebellion.

“The heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool,” says the Lord, so no house can contain Him; instead, “to this man will I look, even to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word.” Zion gives birth to a nation in a day, and God promises, “As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you.” He will gather all nations to see His glory, send messengers to distant coastlands, and make the new heavens and earth endure. The book closes with all flesh coming to worship before Him, and with a sober image of the rebels' bodies, of whom “their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched,” words Jesus repeated in warning about hell.

# Jeremiah
When it happened: From the thirteenth year of King Josiah (627 BC) through the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, and afterward among the Jews who fled to Egypt (Jeremiah 1:2–3; 43–44).
When it was written: Jeremiah dictated his messages to his scribe Baruch, and when King Jehoiakim burned the scroll, Jeremiah dictated it again with more added (Jeremiah 36).
Where: Anathoth, Jerusalem, and later Tahpanhes in Egypt.
The world at the time: Assyria collapsed, and Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar defeated Egypt at Carchemish in 605 BC (Jeremiah 46:2). Judah's last kings wavered between Egypt and Babylon until Babylon destroyed Jerusalem.

**God Calls Jeremiah to Warn Judah (Jeremiah 1:1–19)** — God appoints Jeremiah before his birth to warn nations of judgment. Though Jeremiah feels too young, God places His words in the prophet's mouth, reveals disaster from the north, and promises to strengthen and deliver him against opposition.

Jeremiah, a priest's son from Anathoth, heard God say, “Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee, and I ordained thee a prophet unto the nations.” He protested, “Ah, Lord GOD! behold, I cannot speak: for I am a child,” but the Lord touched his mouth and said, “Behold, I have put my words in thy mouth,” setting him over nations “to root out, and to pull down, and to destroy, and to throw down, to build, and to plant.” God showed him an almond branch, for “I will hasten my word to perform it,” and a boiling pot tipped from the north, picturing invasion. God promised to make Jeremiah a fortified city and an iron pillar against Judah's kings, princes, and priests.

**Judah Abandons the God Who Delivered Her (Jeremiah 2:1–4:4)** — God accuses Judah of abandoning the living water of His covenant for useless idols. Although Judah ignored northern Israel's example of judgment, God repeatedly calls the people to acknowledge sin, practice justice, remove idols, and return.

God remembers Israel's early devotion in the wilderness, when she followed Him like a bride, and asks what wrong her fathers found in Him that they went after worthless idols. “For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water.” Judah has seen northern Israel go into exile for unfaithfulness yet has become worse than her sister. Still God pleads, “Return, ye backsliding children, and I will heal your backslidings,” and calls the men of Judah to “Break up your fallow ground,” sowing not among thorns but circumcising their hearts to the Lord.

**Judgment Approaches a Nation Refusing Correction (Jeremiah 4:5–6:30)** — Jeremiah warns that an invading army from the north will devastate Judah because leaders and people reject truth, justice, and correction. False prophets promise peace, but the nation refuses the old paths of righteousness.

Jeremiah sees the lion of the north coming and the land returning to formless emptiness, and cries, “My bowels, my bowels! I am pained at my very heart.” God challenges, “Run ye to and fro through the streets of Jerusalem,” to find even one person who does justice and seeks truth, that He might pardon the city. Prophets and priests deal falsely, “saying, Peace, peace; when there is no peace.” God urges, “Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls. But they said, We will not walk therein.”

**The Temple Cannot Protect an Unfaithful People (Jeremiah 7:1–10:25)** — Jeremiah confronts false confidence in the temple, declaring that worship cannot protect people who steal, oppress, commit violence, and serve idols. He mourns Judah's refusal to repent and contrasts handmade idols with the living Creator.

Standing at the temple gate, Jeremiah warned, “Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these.” They stole, murdered, committed adultery, and burned incense to Baal, then came to stand in God's house: “Is this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes?” Jesus quoted these words. God told them to remember Shiloh, where His tabernacle once stood. Jeremiah grieves, “The harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved,” asks, “Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there?” and longs, “Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears.” God declares, “Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom... But let him that glorieth glory in this, that he understandeth and knoweth me.”

**Judah Breaks the Covenant and Rejects Jeremiah (Jeremiah 11:1–12:17)** — God recalls Judah's broken covenant and reveals a plot by Jeremiah's hometown against him. The prophet asks why wicked people prosper, while God warns of harder trials, announces judgment, and promises eventual compassion and restoration.

God reminds Judah of the covenant He made when He brought their fathers out of Egypt, which they had broken by serving other gods, and tells Jeremiah not to pray for them. Then God revealed that the men of Jeremiah's own town, Anathoth, were plotting to kill him; he had been “like a lamb or an ox that is brought to the slaughter.” Jeremiah asked, “Wherefore doth the way of the wicked prosper?” God answered with a harder question: “If thou hast run with the footmen, and they have wearied thee, then how canst thou contend with horses?” Yet God also promised that after uprooting the nations, He would have compassion and bring them back.

**Jeremiah's Actions Show Judah's Coming Ruin (Jeremiah 13:1–15:21)** — A ruined linen girdle and jars of wine portray Judah's pride and coming captivity. Drought, false promises of peace, and Jeremiah's grief reveal that judgment will not be removed, though God promises to sustain His rejected prophet.

God had Jeremiah buy a linen girdle, wear it, then hide it in a hole of the rock by the Euphrates. When he dug it up, it was ruined and “profitable for nothing,” a picture of how God would mar Judah's pride. Jars filled with wine pictured the drunkenness of judgment. Jeremiah asked, “Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots?” During a drought, God told him not to pray for the people: “Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my mind could not be toward this people.” Jeremiah cried, “Woe is me, my mother, that thou hast borne me,” yet also confessed, “Thy words were found, and I did eat them; and thy word was unto me the joy and rejoicing of mine heart.”

**Jeremiah Warns of Exile and Calls for Trust (Jeremiah 16:1–17:27)** — Jeremiah's restricted personal life becomes a sign of coming disaster. God contrasts confidence in human strength with trust in Him, exposes the deceitfulness of the heart, and calls Judah to honor the Sabbath or face judgment.

God told Jeremiah, “Thou shalt not take thee a wife,” nor have children, nor join in mourning or feasting, because death and silence were coming to the land. Yet God also promised a new exodus from the north country. “Cursed be the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm,” but “Blessed is the man that trusteth in the LORD... For he shall be as a tree planted by the waters.” God exposes the root problem: “The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?” Jeremiah prays, “Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed,” and he is sent to the city gates to call Judah to keep the Sabbath holy.

**The Potter Reveals God's Authority Over Nations (Jeremiah 18:1–20:18)** — At the potter's house, God shows His authority to reshape nations in response to repentance or rebellion. Judah refuses to turn, Jeremiah breaks a bottle to symbolize coming destruction, and persecution drives him to lament while God's word still burns within him.

At the potter's house, Jeremiah watched a marred vessel being reshaped into another, and God said, “as the clay is in the potter's hand, so are ye in mine hand, O house of Israel.” If a nation turns from evil, God will relent; if it does evil, He will turn from the good He intended. Jeremiah then broke a clay jar before the elders in the valley of Hinnom, declaring that God would break the city the same way. The priest Pashur had him beaten and put in the stocks. Jeremiah complained, “O LORD, thou hast deceived me, and I was deceived,” yet when he tried to stop speaking, “his word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones.” He ended, “Cursed be the day wherein I was born.”

**Judah's Kings Hear the Warning of Destruction (Jeremiah 21:1–23:40)** — Jeremiah tells Judah's kings that Jerusalem will fall and condemns rulers who exploit rather than protect. God promises to gather a remnant and raise a righteous Branch from David's line, while false prophets who promise peace face judgment.

When Zedekiah asked whether God would deliver Jerusalem from Nebuchadnezzar, Jeremiah answered that God Himself would fight against the city, and told the people, “I set before you the way of life, and the way of death”: surrender to the Chaldeans and live. He confronted the kings: “Woe unto him that buildeth his house by unrighteousness,” he said of Jehoiakim, contrasting him with his father Josiah, who judged the cause of the poor and needy, which God called truly knowing Him. Jehoiachin, also called Coniah, would be written childless as a king, with no descendant on David's throne. God condemned the shepherds who scattered His sheep, promised, “I will raise unto David a righteous Branch,” named “THE LORD OUR RIGHTEOUSNESS,” and asked of the false prophets, “Is not my word like as a fire? saith the LORD; and like a hammer that breaketh the rock in pieces?”

**Two Baskets of Figs Reveal Different Futures (Jeremiah 24:1–10)** — Good figs represent Judeans already exiled to Babylon, whom God will watch over and restore. Bad figs represent Zedekiah and those remaining in Jerusalem or fleeing to Egypt, who will face further disaster for continued unfaithfulness.

After Nebuchadnezzar carried Jeconiah and the princes and craftsmen to Babylon, God showed Jeremiah two baskets of figs set before the temple, one very good and one too bad to eat. The good figs were the captives, whom God would bring back, promising, “I will give them an heart to know me,” and they would return to Him with their whole heart. The bad figs were Zedekiah, his princes, and those who stayed in the land or went to Egypt, who would become a reproach among the nations.

**Seventy Years of Captivity Are Announced (Jeremiah 25:1–38)** — After years of ignored warnings, Jeremiah announces seventy years of Babylonian domination for Judah and surrounding nations. Babylon itself will later be judged as the cup of God's wrath reaches rulers and peoples across the earth.

In the fourth year of Jehoiakim, the first year of Nebuchadnezzar, Jeremiah reminded the people that he had spoken to them for twenty-three years, rising early, but they had not listened. Now God would bring Nebuchadnezzar, whom He called His servant, against the land, and “these nations shall serve the king of Babylon seventy years.” After that, Babylon itself would be punished. God told Jeremiah, “Take the wine cup of this fury at my hand,” and make all the nations drink it, beginning with Jerusalem and reaching to the kings of the earth.

**Jeremiah Is Threatened for Warning the Temple (Jeremiah 26:1–24)** — Jeremiah's warning that the temple could become like Shiloh leads priests and prophets to demand his death. Officials and elders defend his right to speak, recalling Micah, while Ahikam protects Jeremiah from Jehoiakim's violence.

At the start of Jehoiakim's reign, Jeremiah stood in the temple court and warned that if the people did not listen, God would “make this house like Shiloh.” The priests, prophets, and people seized him and said he must die. Jeremiah answered, “As for me, behold, I am in your hand,” but warned that killing him would bring innocent blood upon them. Some elders recalled that Micah had prophesied, “Zion shall be plowed like a field,” and King Hezekiah had repented instead of killing him. Another prophet, Urijah, who spoke the same message, fled to Egypt but was brought back and killed by Jehoiakim. Ahikam the son of Shaphan protected Jeremiah.

**God Commands the Nations to Submit to Babylon (Jeremiah 27:1–29:32)** — Jeremiah wears a yoke to command submission to Babylon and exposes false predictions of quick deliverance. He tells exiles to build homes, raise families, and seek Babylon's peace while waiting for God's promised restoration after seventy years.

Jeremiah made bonds and yokes, wore one on his neck, and sent word to the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon that God had given their lands to Nebuchadnezzar. The prophet Hananiah broke the yoke from Jeremiah's neck and promised that the captivity would end within two years. God answered through Jeremiah, “Thou hast broken the yokes of wood; but thou shalt make for them yokes of iron,” and Hananiah died that same year, as Jeremiah foretold. Jeremiah wrote to the exiles in Babylon: “Build ye houses, and dwell in them... And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it.” After seventy years God would bring them home: “For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, saith the LORD, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you an expected end... And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart.”

**God Promises Israel's Restoration and a New Covenant (Jeremiah 30:1–31:40)** — God promises to heal, gather, and rebuild Israel and Judah after captivity. He announces a new covenant in which His law will be written on their hearts, sins will be forgiven, and His commitment to His people will endure.

God told Jeremiah to write in a book that He would bring His people back from captivity. Though it would be “the time of Jacob's trouble,” Jacob would be saved out of it. God declares, “Yea, I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee.” A voice is heard in Ramah, “Rahel weeping for her children refused to be comforted,” words Matthew connects to Herod's slaughter at Bethlehem, but God promises that her children will return. No longer will people say, “The fathers have eaten a sour grape, and the children's teeth are set on edge.” Then comes the promise Hebrews quotes in full: “Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah... I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people... for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more.”

**Jeremiah Buys a Field as a Sign of Hope (Jeremiah 32:1–33:26)** — While Jerusalem is under siege and Jeremiah is confined, he buys a field in Anathoth as a sign that homes, fields, and vineyards will be owned again. God confirms both coming judgment and His everlasting covenant of restoration.

In the tenth year of Zedekiah, with Babylon's army besieging Jerusalem and Jeremiah shut up in the court of the prison, his cousin Hanameel came to sell him a field in Anathoth. Jeremiah paid seventeen shekels of silver and had the deeds sealed in an earthen jar, for God said, “Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land.” He prayed, “Ah Lord GOD! behold, thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee,” and God answered, “Behold, I am the LORD, the God of all flesh: is there any thing too hard for me?” While Jeremiah was still confined, God told him, “Call unto me, and I will answer thee, and shew thee great and mighty things, which thou knowest not,” promising that the Branch of David would come and that His covenant was as sure as day and night.

**Judah Breaks Its Promise to Free Servants (Jeremiah 34:1–22)** — During the siege, Judah's leaders covenant to free Hebrew servants but then force them back into slavery. God condemns the betrayal and declares that Babylon will return, capture Jerusalem, and bring consequences upon the covenant breakers.

During the siege, Zedekiah and the people made a covenant to release their Hebrew servants, cutting a calf in two and passing between its parts, as the law of Moses required freedom after six years. Then they took their servants back. God declared, “I proclaim a liberty for you, saith the LORD, to the sword, to the pestilence, and to the famine.” Babylon's army, which had withdrawn for a time, would return, take the city, and burn it.

**The Rechabites Demonstrate Faithful Obedience (Jeremiah 35:1–19)** — The Rechabites refuse wine because they have obeyed their ancestor's command for generations. God uses their faithfulness to expose Judah's refusal to hear His repeated warnings, while promising Jonadab's descendants an enduring place before Him.

In Jehoiakim's days, God told Jeremiah to bring the family of the Rechabites into a temple chamber and set wine before them. They refused, saying, “We will drink no wine,” because their forefather Jonadab had commanded them never to drink wine, build houses, or plant vineyards, and they had obeyed for generations. God contrasted their faithfulness to a human father with Judah's refusal to obey Him though He had spoken to them again and again. He promised, “Jonadab the son of Rechab shall not want a man to stand before me for ever.”

**Jehoiakim Burns Jeremiah's Written Warnings (Jeremiah 36:1–32)** — Jeremiah dictates God's warnings to Baruch, who reads them publicly. Though officials fear the message, Jehoiakim cuts the scroll apart and burns it; God preserves Jeremiah and Baruch and commands a new scroll containing the same judgment.

In Jehoiakim's fourth year, God told Jeremiah to write all His words in a scroll, and Jeremiah dictated them to Baruch. Since Jeremiah could not go to the temple, Baruch read the scroll there on a fast day. The princes were alarmed and told Baruch and Jeremiah to hide. As the scroll was read to the king beside a fire in his winter house, Jehoiakim cut off each few columns with a penknife and threw them into the fire until it was gone. “Yet they were not afraid, nor rent their garments,” though some officials begged him not to burn it. The king ordered Baruch and Jeremiah arrested, “but the LORD hid them.” Jeremiah dictated a new scroll, and “there were added besides unto them many like words.”

**Jeremiah Is Imprisoned During Jerusalem's Final Siege (Jeremiah 37:1–38:28)** — Jeremiah continues declaring that Egypt will not save Jerusalem and that surrender to Babylon will preserve life. He is beaten, imprisoned, and lowered into a muddy cistern until Ebedmelech rescues him, but Zedekiah still refuses God's warning.

When Egypt's army approached and the Babylonians withdrew for a time, Jeremiah set out for his land in Benjamin. He was arrested at the gate as a deserter, beaten, and put in a dungeon. Zedekiah secretly sent for him and asked, “Is there any word from the LORD?” Jeremiah answered that the king would be delivered into Babylon's hand. The princes later persuaded the king to let them throw Jeremiah into a cistern with no water, and he sank in the mire. Ebedmelech the Ethiopian, a palace official, pleaded for him and drew him out with ropes and old rags. In a final secret meeting, Jeremiah urged Zedekiah to surrender to save the city, but the king was afraid.

**Jerusalem Falls and Jeremiah Is Released (Jeremiah 39:1–18)** — Babylon captures Jerusalem, blinds and carries away Zedekiah, burns the city, and breaks its walls. Jeremiah is protected and placed with Gedaliah, while Ebedmelech receives God's promise of survival because he trusted the Lord.

After an eighteen-month siege, the wall was broken in Zedekiah's eleventh year. Zedekiah fled but was captured near Jericho; at Riblah his sons were killed before his eyes, his eyes were put out, and he was bound for Babylon. The city and its walls were burned and broken down. Nebuchadnezzar commanded concerning Jeremiah, “Take him, and look well to him, and do him no harm,” and he was entrusted to Gedaliah. God had promised Ebedmelech that he would not die by the sword “because thou hast put thy trust in me.”

**The Survivors Struggle After Jerusalem's Destruction (Jeremiah 40:1–41:18)** — Gedaliah governs the remaining people in Judah and urges peace under Babylon, but Ishmael murders him and takes captives. Johanan rescues the captives, and the survivors fear Babylonian retaliation for the murder of its appointed governor.

Nebuzaradan, captain of the guard, freed Jeremiah from his chains at Ramah and let him choose where to go, and Jeremiah stayed with Gedaliah, the governor at Mizpah. Scattered Jews gathered to Gedaliah and harvested wine and summer fruit. Johanan warned Gedaliah that Ishmael was plotting to kill him, but Gedaliah did not believe it. Ishmael, of the royal family, murdered Gedaliah at a meal along with Jews and Babylonian soldiers, then killed pilgrims and threw their bodies in a pit. Johanan pursued him and rescued the captives, but Ishmael escaped to Ammon, and the survivors feared Babylon's reprisal.

**The Survivors Reject God's Command to Remain in Judah (Jeremiah 42:1–44:30)** — The survivors ask Jeremiah for God's direction, promise obedience, then reject His command to remain in Judah. They flee to Egypt, continue idolatry, and receive warnings that the disasters they fear will follow them there.

The survivors begged Jeremiah to pray for guidance and swore they would obey whatever God said. After ten days the answer came: “If ye will still abide in this land, then will I build you,” but if they went to Egypt, the sword and famine they feared would overtake them there. The leaders replied, “Thou speakest falsely,” and took everyone, including Jeremiah and Baruch, to Tahpanhes in Egypt. There Jeremiah hid stones at Pharaoh's palace and foretold that Nebuchadnezzar would set his throne on them. When he rebuked the Jews in Egypt for idolatry, the women insisted they would keep burning incense to the queen of heaven, and God declared they would know “whose words shall stand, mine, or theirs.”

**God Gives Baruch a Personal Promise (Jeremiah 45:1–5)** — God answers Baruch's distress by warning him not to seek greatness in a time of coming national disaster. Though judgment is near, God promises to preserve Baruch's life wherever he goes.

Back in the fourth year of Jehoiakim, when Baruch wrote Jeremiah's words, he cried, “Woe is me now! for the LORD hath added grief to my sorrow.” God answered that He was breaking down what He had built, and asked, “And seekest thou great things for thyself? seek them not.” Yet He promised, “thy life will I give unto thee for a prey in all places whither thou goest.”

**Egypt and Philistia Face God's Judgment (Jeremiah 46:1–47:7)** — Jeremiah foretells Egypt's defeat by Babylon and warns that its gods and military confidence cannot save it, though Egypt will later be inhabited again. Philistia also faces an invading northern force appointed by God for judgment.

Jeremiah's messages to the nations begin with Egypt, whose army Nebuchadnezzar defeated at Carchemish by the Euphrates. Egypt rises like the Nile in flood, but God mocks her: “Go up into Gilead, and take balm, O virgin, the daughter of Egypt: in vain shalt thou use many medicines.” Egypt would fall to Babylon, though afterward it would be inhabited again, and God told Israel, “fear not thou, O my servant Jacob.” Philistia is overwhelmed by waters from the north, and Jeremiah cries, “O thou sword of the LORD, how long will it be ere thou be quiet?”

**Moab and Ammon Receive Warnings of Destruction (Jeremiah 48:1–49:6)** — Moab is judged for pride, trust in wealth, and confidence in Chemosh; Ammon is judged for occupying Gad's territory and trusting its resources. Both nations receive a later promise of restored fortunes after judgment.

“Moab hath been at ease from his youth, and he hath settled on his lees,” like wine never poured from vessel to vessel, so its proud taste remained. God would empty Moab's vessels, and Moab would be ashamed of Chemosh as Israel had been ashamed of Bethel. Jeremiah grieves for Moab's ruin even as he announces it. Ammon is asked, “Hath Israel no sons? hath he no heir?” for its king had taken Gad's land. Yet God promised to restore the captivity of both Moab and Ammon in the latter days.

**God Judges Edom, Damascus, Kedar, and Elam (Jeremiah 49:7–39)** — Edom's wisdom and mountain strongholds, Damascus's defenses, Kedar's desert distance, and Elam's military strength cannot prevent God's judgment. Elam, like other nations, is also promised future restoration.

God asks of Edom, “Is wisdom no more in Teman?” and warns, “though thou shouldest make thy nest as high as the eagle,” He would bring it down, words closely echoed in the book of Obadiah. Damascus grows feeble, the tents of Kedar and the kingdoms of Hazor are plundered by Nebuchadnezzar, and God will break the bow of Elam and scatter its people to the four winds. Yet the chapter ends with the promise that God will bring again the captivity of Elam in the latter days.

**Babylon's Fall and Israel's Return Are Foretold (Jeremiah 50:1–51:64)** — Babylon, which destroyed Jerusalem and carried Judah captive, will itself fall for pride and cruelty. Israel and Judah will return to seek the Lord, and a scroll sunk in the Euphrates symbolizes Babylon's final downfall.

The longest of Jeremiah's messages to the nations is against Babylon. God says, “My people hath been lost sheep: their shepherds have caused them to go astray,” but they will return weeping to seek the Lord and join themselves to Him in an everlasting covenant. A nation from the north will destroy Babylon, for “Their Redeemer is strong; the LORD of hosts is his name.” “Babylon hath been a golden cup in the LORD'S hand,” but now “Babylon is suddenly fallen and destroyed,” words echoed in Revelation. In Zedekiah's fourth year, Jeremiah gave the prophecy to Seraiah to read in Babylon, tie to a stone, and throw into the Euphrates, saying, “Thus shall Babylon sink, and shall not rise.” The section closes, “Thus far are the words of Jeremiah.”

**Jerusalem Falls and Jehoiachin Receives Mercy (Jeremiah 52:1–34)** — The book closes with Jerusalem's siege, the blinding and captivity of Zedekiah, the destruction of the temple, and the exile of Judah. Years later, Babylon's new king releases Jehoiachin and grants him honor and daily provision.

The final chapter closely parallels 2 Kings 24–25. It recounts Zedekiah's rebellion, the long siege and famine, the breaching of the wall, Zedekiah's capture and blinding, and the burning of the temple, palace, and great houses. The Babylonians broke the bronze pillars and carried away the temple vessels, and executed the chief priest and leaders at Riblah. The chapter adds the numbers carried captive in three deportations, four thousand six hundred in all. It ends in the thirty-seventh year of Jehoiachin's captivity, when Evilmerodach of Babylon released him from prison, spoke kindly to him, and gave him a seat at the king's table for the rest of his life.

# Lamentations
When it happened: Just after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and the temple in 586 BC.
When it was written: The first four poems are acrostics, each verse or group of verses beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Their vivid detail suggests an eyewitness writing soon after the city fell. Jeremiah is known to have written laments (2 Chronicles 35:25).
Where: The ruins of Jerusalem.
The world at the time: The Babylonians had burned the temple and the city, many people had been killed or carried into exile, and those left behind faced famine. Neighboring Edom rejoiced at Jerusalem's fall (Lamentations 4:21).

**Jerusalem Mourns Its Destruction (Lamentations 1:1–22)** — Jerusalem, once full of worshipers and life, lies empty after Babylon's conquest. The city acknowledges that the Lord is righteous and that Judah rebelled, yet it mourns captivity, hunger, loss, and the cruelty of enemies who offer no comfort.

The book opens with a cry of grief: “How doth the city sit solitary, that was full of people! how is she become as a widow!” Jerusalem weeps through the night, and “among all her lovers she hath none to comfort her,” for the allies she trusted have become her enemies. Judah has gone into captivity, the roads to Zion mourn because no one comes to the feasts, and the city's young people have been carried away. Speaking for herself, Jerusalem calls to those who pass by, “Is it nothing to you, all ye that pass by? behold, and see if there be any sorrow like unto my sorrow.” Yet she confesses, “The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against his commandment.” Like the next three poems, this chapter is an acrostic of twenty-two verses, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet.

**God's Judgment Falls Upon His People (Lamentations 2:1–22)** — The destruction of Jerusalem and its temple is presented as God's judgment on Judah's sin. Leaders, priests, and prophets are brought low; children faint from hunger; false prophets are exposed; and the people are urged to pour out their desperate hearts before God.

The poet describes the Lord as an enemy who has swallowed up Israel's palaces, thrown down the strongholds of Judah, and cast off His own altar and sanctuary. “The LORD hath done that which he had devised,” fulfilling the word He had commanded long before. The poet says, “Mine eyes do fail with tears,” as infants faint in the streets and ask their mothers for bread. “Thy prophets have seen vain and foolish things for thee: and they have not discovered thine iniquity,” and passersby mock, “Is this the city that men call The perfection of beauty, The joy of the whole earth?” The people are urged to “pour out thine heart like water before the face of the Lord,” and the chapter ends with the horror of the siege, when women ate their own children and priests and prophets were slain in the sanctuary.

**Hope Appears in the Middle of Suffering (Lamentations 3:1–39)** — A suffering voice describes affliction and despair, then recalls that the Lord's compassion and faithfulness do not end. God is the speaker's portion and reason for hope; He disciplines but does not cast people away forever and does not approve injustice.

The central poem is the longest, sixty-six verses with three verses for each letter of the alphabet. One man speaks: “I am the man that hath seen affliction by the rod of his wrath.” He feels walled in and driven into darkness, and “when I cry and shout, he shutteth out my prayer.” Then, “Remembering mine affliction and my misery, the wormwood and the gall,” he turns: “This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope. It is of the LORD'S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.” He declares, “The LORD is my portion, saith my soul; therefore will I hope in him,” and, “It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD,” for “the Lord will not cast off for ever” and “he doth not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men.”

**A Broken People Cry Out to God (Lamentations 3:40–66)** — The people are called to examine their ways, confess rebellion, and return to God. Amid tears and persecution, the speaker calls on the Lord, remembers that He heard and defended him, and asks God to see and judge the cruelty of enemies.

The speaker calls the people, “Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD. Let us lift up our heart with our hands unto God in the heavens,” confessing that they have transgressed and rebelled. His eyes run with streams of water for the destruction of his people. Enemies hunted him and shut him in a pit, but he says, “I called upon thy name, O LORD, out of the low dungeon,” and God drew near: “thou saidst, Fear not,” and “thou hast redeemed my life.” He closes by asking God to see the wrongs done to him and repay his persecutors.

**The Terrible Cost of Jerusalem's Fall (Lamentations 4:1–22)** — Jerusalem's former honor is replaced by famine, shame, and unbearable suffering. Priests and prophets share responsibility for corruption, false hope in foreign aid fails, and the captured king cannot protect the people; yet Zion's punishment is not promised to last forever.

“How is the gold become dim! how is the most fine gold changed!” The precious sons of Zion are treated like clay pots, nursing infants thirst, and those once raised in luxury lie on ash heaps. The famine was so severe that “The hands of the pitiful women have sodden their own children.” This came “For the sins of her prophets, and the iniquities of her priests,” who had shed the blood of the just. The people confess, “we have watched for a nation that could not save us,” and mourn that “The breath of our nostrils, the anointed of the LORD, was taken in their pits,” meaning King Zedekiah. Edom is warned that the cup will pass to her too, while Zion is told, “The punishment of thine iniquity is accomplished, O daughter of Zion; he will no more carry thee away into captivity.”

**Remembering the Ruins and Pleading for Restoration (Lamentations 5:1–22)** — Judah brings its disgrace, hunger, oppression, and ruined Jerusalem before God. Though joy has become mourning and Mount Zion is desolate, the people remember that the Lord reigns forever and plead for restoration while ending with unresolved sorrow.

The final poem is a prayer: “Remember, O LORD, what is come upon us: consider, and behold our reproach.” Their inheritance has gone to strangers, they pay for their own water and wood, servants rule over them, women are ravished, and princes are hanged. “Our fathers have sinned, and are not; and we have borne their iniquities.” “The joy of our heart is ceased; our dance is turned into mourning. The crown is fallen from our head: woe unto us, that we have sinned!” Foxes walk on desolate Mount Zion. Still they confess, “Thou, O LORD, remainest for ever; thy throne from generation to generation,” and pray, “Turn thou us unto thee, O LORD, and we shall be turned; renew our days as of old.” The book ends without resolution: “But thou hast utterly rejected us; thou art very wroth against us.” This chapter also has twenty-two verses but, unlike the first four, is not an acrostic.

# Ezekiel
When it happened: From the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's captivity (593 BC; Ezekiel 1:2) to the twenty-seventh year (571 BC; Ezekiel 29:17). Many of the prophecies are dated to the day.
When it was written: Most of the book is Ezekiel's first-person record of his visions and messages (Ezekiel 1:1), likely written down during the exile in Babylon.
Where: Among the exiles by the river Chebar in Babylonia, at Tel-abib (Ezekiel 1:1–3; 3:15). In visions he was carried to Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Ezekiel was taken to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC. For his first six years of prophesying, Jerusalem still stood under King Zedekiah; then in 586 BC the exiles received news that the city had fallen (Ezekiel 33:21).

**Ezekiel Sees God's Glory and Is Called (Ezekiel 1:1–3:27)** — Among Judean exiles by the river Chebar in Babylon, Ezekiel sees a vision of God's glory: living creatures, wheels, and a throne above them. God calls the priest Ezekiel to be a watchman for Israel, gives him a scroll of lamentation to eat, and commands him to speak whether the people listen or refuse.

**Signs Announce Jerusalem's Coming Siege (Ezekiel 4:1–7:27)** — God directs Ezekiel to act out Jerusalem's siege, lie on his side for Israel and Judah's years of sin, and eat restricted food as a sign of famine. Shaving his head and dividing the hair portrays death, scattering, and a small remnant, while God declares that idolatry and violence have brought the end near.

**God's Glory Reveals Jerusalem's Idolatry (Ezekiel 8:1–11:25)** — In visions of Jerusalem, Ezekiel sees hidden idol worship in the temple and leaders leading the people astray. God marks those who grieve over the city's sins, brings judgment on the rest, and His glory departs from the temple, while promising that He will later gather and give His people a new heart and spirit.

**Judgment Reaches Kings and People (Ezekiel 12:1–19:14)** — Ezekiel acts out exile and announces that Zedekiah will be captured. God rejects false prophets, teaches that each person is accountable for sin, and declares that even righteous people cannot save a corrupt land. Parables of a useless vine, an unfaithful wife, two eagles, and lions expose Jerusalem's rebellion and the fall of its rulers.

**Israel's Long Rebellion Brings the End (Ezekiel 20:1–24:27)** — God recounts Israel's repeated rebellion from Egypt through the wilderness and the land, yet preserves His name among the nations. Ezekiel warns that Babylon's sword is coming, exposes Jerusalem's bloodshed and idolatry, and announces the siege through the sign of a boiling pot and the death of his wife.

**God Judges Israel's Neighboring Nations (Ezekiel 25:1–28:26)** — Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, and Sidon are judged for pride, violence, and delight in Jerusalem's ruin. Tyre's wealth and trade cannot save it, and its ruler's exalted claims are brought down. God promises that Israel will dwell securely after judgment on the nations around it.

**Egypt and Pharaoh Are Brought Low (Ezekiel 29:1–32:32)** — Egypt is compared to a great river creature that boasts of the Nile, and Pharaoh is pictured as a fallen cedar and a lion caught in a net. Babylon will humble Egypt and its allies, showing that military strength and wealth cannot stand against God's judgment.

**Ezekiel Becomes Watchman Again (Ezekiel 33:1–33)** — After news arrives that Jerusalem has fallen, God renews Ezekiel's duty as a watchman. He teaches personal responsibility, calls the wicked to turn and live, rebukes people who hear the prophet's words without obeying them, and exposes leaders who exploit the land.

**God Promises a True Shepherd and Restored Land (Ezekiel 34:1–36:38)** — God condemns Israel's shepherds, its leaders, for feeding themselves instead of caring for the flock. He promises to seek His sheep and set one shepherd, His servant David, over them. Edom is judged for hostility, while Israel is promised cleansing, a new heart, a new spirit, restored land, and renewed fruitfulness.

**Dry Bones Live and Israel Is Reunited (Ezekiel 37:1–28)** — In a valley of dry bones, God shows Ezekiel that He can restore the whole house of Israel from hopeless exile. Breath enters the bones, and they become a great army. Two joined sticks portray Judah and Israel becoming one nation under one king from David's line, with God's sanctuary among them.

**Gog Attacks but God Defends His People (Ezekiel 38:1–39:29)** — Ezekiel prophesies against Gog of Magog, who will come against a restored people living securely. God Himself defeats the invading forces, displays His holiness among the nations, and promises that Israel will know Him after He gathers and restores them.

**Ezekiel Is Shown a Future Temple (Ezekiel 40:1–43:27)** — In the twenty-fifth year of exile, Ezekiel is brought in a vision to Israel and shown a carefully measured temple, its courts, gates, rooms, altar, and sacred boundaries. The glory of the Lord returns from the east and fills the temple, and God gives instructions for the altar and offerings.

**Worship Is Ordered for a Holy Community (Ezekiel 44:1–46:24)** — God sets apart the eastern gate and gives duties for priests, Levites, the prince, and the people. Worship must reject former idolatry and honor God's holiness. Instructions govern offerings, Sabbaths, feasts, the prince's responsibilities, land, and sacred spaces.

**The River Brings Life and the Land Is Divided (Ezekiel 47:1–48:29)** — Water flowing from the temple grows into a deep river that brings life wherever it goes, including healing for the Dead Sea. Ezekiel then describes the renewed land's boundaries and portions for the tribes, the sanctuary, priests, Levites, and prince.

**The Restored City Is Called The Lord Is There (Ezekiel 48:30–35)** — The final vision names Jerusalem's gates after Israel's tribes and gives the restored city its closing name: THE LORD IS THERE. The book ends with God's presence, holiness, judgment, restoration, and His faithfulness to His scattered people held together.

# Daniel
When it happened: From the third year of King Jehoiakim (605 BC; Daniel 1:1) to the third year of Cyrus king of Persia (about 536 BC; Daniel 10:1).
When it was written: Chapters 7–12 record Daniel's visions in his own voice. Many scholars date the book's final form to the 160s BC, during the persecution under the Greek king Antiochus IV. Daniel 2:4–7:28 is written in Aramaic rather than Hebrew.
Where: Babylon, the palace at Shushan (Daniel 8:2), and the bank of the river Hiddekel, the Tigris (Daniel 10:4).
The world at the time: The book spans the Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar and its conquest by the Medes and Persians under Darius the Median and Cyrus.

**Daniel and His Friends Remain Faithful in Babylon (Daniel 1:1–21)** — Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon carries young people from Judah into exile, including Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. They are trained for the king's court and given Babylonian names, but Daniel asks not to defile himself with the royal food. God gives the four men knowledge and skill, and Daniel receives understanding in visions and dreams.

**God Reveals the King's Dream to Daniel (Daniel 2:1–49)** — Nebuchadnezzar demands that his wise men tell both his dream and its meaning, a task they cannot perform. Daniel and his friends pray, and God reveals the mystery: a great image represents a sequence of kingdoms, while a stone made without hands becomes a kingdom that will never be destroyed. The king honors Daniel's God and promotes Daniel and his friends.

**Three Hebrews Are Delivered from the Furnace (Daniel 3:1–30)** — Nebuchadnezzar sets up a golden image and commands worship. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse, trusting God even if He does not deliver them. They are thrown into a blazing furnace, where a fourth figure appears with them; they emerge unharmed, and the king acknowledges the power of their God.

**Nebuchadnezzar Learns Who Rules the Kingdoms (Daniel 4:1–37)** — Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a great tree cut down until he learns that the Most High rules human kingdoms. Daniel warns him to turn from sin, but the king later boasts in his power and is driven from people until he recognizes God's authority. His sanity and kingdom are restored when he praises the King of heaven.

**Belshazzar Is Judged at His Feast (Daniel 5:1–31)** — Belshazzar uses vessels taken from Jerusalem's temple while praising idols. A hand writes a message on the wall, and Daniel explains that God has numbered the kingdom, weighed the king, and given Babylon to the Medes and Persians. Belshazzar is killed that night.

**Daniel Is Delivered from the Lions' Den (Daniel 6:1–28)** — Under Darius, Daniel's enemies use his faithfulness in prayer to trap him under a royal decree. Daniel continues praying to God and is thrown into a den of lions, but God shuts the lions' mouths. Darius recognizes God's power, while Daniel prospers during the reigns of Darius and Cyrus.

**Daniel Sees Four Kingdoms and the Son of Man (Daniel 7:1–28)** — Daniel sees four beasts rising from the sea, followed by a heavenly court where the Ancient of days judges the arrogant power. One like the Son of man receives everlasting dominion. The vision explains that earthly kingdoms will pass away, while the saints of the Most High will receive the kingdom.

**The Ram, the Goat, and the Coming Conflict (Daniel 8:1–27)** — Daniel sees a ram defeated by a swift goat whose great horn is broken and replaced. A later arrogant ruler opposes the holy people and interrupts the sanctuary service for a time. Gabriel explains that the vision concerns future kingdoms and a period of severe trouble.

**Daniel Prays and Receives a Promise of Restoration (Daniel 9:1–27)** — While reading Jeremiah's prophecy of seventy years, Daniel confesses Israel's sin and asks God to restore Jerusalem and its sanctuary. Gabriel brings a message concerning seventy weeks, speaking of Jerusalem's rebuilding, an anointed one, coming desolation, and an appointed end; the passage's details have received differing interpretations.

**Daniel Is Strengthened for His Final Vision (Daniel 10:1–11:35)** — Daniel mourns and fasts before receiving a vision near the Tigris River. A heavenly messenger strengthens him and describes conflict involving Persia, Greece, and later rulers. The vision includes a contemptible king who acts deceitfully, profanes the sanctuary, and persecutes those who know their God.

**The Final Conflict and the Promise of Resurrection (Daniel 11:36–12:13)** — The final vision describes a proud ruler and a time of unmatched trouble. Daniel is told that many who sleep in the dust will awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame, and that the wise will shine. The book ends with Daniel instructed to seal the words until the appointed time and promised rest and a final inheritance.
