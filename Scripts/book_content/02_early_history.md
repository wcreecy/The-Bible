# Joshua
When it happened: From the death of Moses to the death of Joshua at 110 (Joshua 24:29). Depending on the date of the exodus, this is placed either around 1400 BC or in the 1200s BC.
When it was written: The book records that "Joshua wrote these words in the book of the law of God" (Joshua 24:26). Repeated notes that things remain "unto this day" show that at least parts were written some time after the events.
Where: The Jordan River, Gilgal, Jericho, Ai, Gibeon, Mount Ebal, Shiloh, and Shechem in the land of Canaan.
The world at the time: Canaan was a patchwork of walled city-states, each with its own king—Joshua 12 lists thirty-one of them. No single empire controlled the land at this point in the story.

**God Appoints Joshua to Lead Israel (Joshua 1:1–18)** — After Moses dies, God commands Joshua to lead Israel into the promised land and encourages him to remain strong, courageous, and obedient.

The Israelites were camped east of the Jordan River, preparing to enter Canaan. God spoke to Joshua, Moses' longtime assistant and appointed successor, and commanded him to lead the people across the Jordan. God promised to remain with Joshua as He had been with Moses. Joshua was to obey the law, prepare provisions, and lead the eastern tribes' fighting men alongside the other tribes. The people pledged obedience and encouraged him to lead with courage.

**Rahab Protects Israel's Spies (Joshua 2:1–24)** — Joshua sends two spies into Jericho, where Rahab protects them and receives a promise that her family will be spared.

Joshua secretly sent two men to examine Jericho. Rahab hid them from the king's messengers and explained that Jericho had heard of God's acts for Israel. She acknowledged that Israel's God ruled over heaven and earth and asked that her family be spared. The spies agreed if she kept their mission secret and displayed a scarlet cord in her window.

**God Leads Israel Across the Jordan (Joshua 3:1–4:24)** — God stops the Jordan River so Israel can cross on dry ground, confirming Joshua's leadership and establishing a memorial for future generations.

When the priests carrying the ark stepped into the overflowing Jordan, its waters stopped upstream and Israel crossed on dry ground. Twelve men carried stones from the riverbed to Gilgal as a memorial, and Joshua also set up stones where the priests had stood. The memorial would remind future generations that God dried up the Jordan as He had parted the Red Sea.

**Israel Renews Its Commitment Before Jericho (Joshua 5:1–15)** — Before entering battle, Israel renews covenant practices, celebrates Passover, and Joshua encounters the commander of the Lord's army.

Joshua circumcised the Israelite males born during the wilderness journey, and Israel celebrated Passover at Gilgal. The manna then ceased as the people ate the produce of Canaan. Near Jericho, Joshua encountered the captain of the Lord's host and was told to remove his shoes because the ground was holy.

**The Walls of Jericho Fall (Joshua 6:1–27)** — God gives Israel an unusual plan for capturing Jericho, and the city's walls collapse after the people follow His instructions.

For six days Israel marched once around Jericho with priests, trumpets, and the ark. On the seventh day they marched seven times; at Joshua's command the people shouted, and the wall fell. Israel destroyed the city, but Rahab and her household were spared because she had protected the spies. Designated metals went into the Lord's treasury, and Joshua pronounced a curse on rebuilding Jericho.

**Achan's Sin Brings Defeat at Ai (Joshua 7:1–8:35)** — Israel suffers defeat because of hidden disobedience, but after the wrongdoing is judged, God gives Joshua victory over Ai.

Israel was defeated at Ai when Achan secretly took items from Jericho devoted to destruction. After his confession, judgment was carried out in the valley of Achor. God then directed Joshua to attack Ai again. Israel used an ambush, captured the city, and afterward Joshua built an altar on Mount Ebal and read God's law, blessings, and curses.

**The Gibeonites Deceive Israel (Joshua 9:1–27)** — The people of Gibeon trick Israel into making a peace agreement, revealing the consequences of acting without seeking God's guidance.

The Gibeonites disguised themselves as distant travelers and persuaded Israel's leaders to make a covenant with them. Israel did not ask counsel from the Lord. When the deception was discovered, the oath was kept; Joshua spared the Gibeonites and assigned them as woodcutters and water carriers for the congregation and the house of God.

**God Gives Israel Victory Over Southern Kings (Joshua 10:1–43)** — Joshua leads Israel against an alliance of southern Canaanite kings, and God grants victory through battle and extraordinary signs.

Joshua came to Gibeon's aid against five kings. The Lord sent great hailstones upon the fleeing armies, and Scripture records that the sun stood still while Israel fought. The five kings were captured and executed. Joshua then campaigned against southern cities including Lachish, Eglon, Hebron, and Debir before returning to Gilgal.

**Israel Defeats the Northern Kings (Joshua 11:1–12:24)** — A powerful alliance of northern kings gathers against Israel, but Joshua defeats their armies and secures victories throughout much of Canaan.

Jabin of Hazor gathered numerous armies with horses and chariots. God told Joshua not to fear, and Israel defeated the alliance near the waters of Merom. Hazor was captured and burned. The account lists the territories taken and thirty-one kings defeated west of the Jordan, while noting that land remained to be possessed.

**God Prepares Israel to Divide the Land (Joshua 13:1–14:15)** — As Joshua grows old, God commands the remaining land to be assigned among Israel's tribes, while Caleb receives his promised inheritance.

God told Joshua that much territory remained and instructed him to distribute the land. Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh already had land east of the Jordan; the Levites received no separate territory. Caleb, faithful when Israel first explored Canaan, asked for Hebron at age eighty-five. Joshua blessed him and granted it.

**Israel Receives Its Tribal Inheritances (Joshua 15:1–19:51)** — Joshua and Israel's leaders assign territories to the tribes, establishing the boundaries and towns each family group will inherit.

Judah received a large southern territory, and Caleb received Hebron. Ephraim and Manasseh received central lands. The record gives boundaries and cities, including places where Canaanites remained. The tabernacle was set up at Shiloh, and seven remaining tribes received their portions by lot. Joshua received Timnathserah.

**God Establishes Cities of Refuge and Levitical Cities (Joshua 20:1–21:45)** — God provides cities of refuge for accidental deaths and assigns towns to the Levites throughout Israel's territories.

Six cities of refuge gave protection to those responsible for accidental deaths while cases were examined. The Levites received forty-eight cities and surrounding pasturelands throughout Israel, including these cities. The account declares that God gave Israel the land and rest He had promised, and that none of His good promises had failed.

**The Eastern Tribes Return Home (Joshua 22:1–34)** — The tribes east of the Jordan build an altar that nearly causes conflict, but an explanation prevents fighting among the Israelites.

Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh returned east after helping the other tribes. They built a large altar near the Jordan, alarming the western tribes. Phinehas and representatives confronted them. The eastern tribes explained that it was a witness, not an altar for sacrifices, that both sides shared the same God. The conflict ended.

**Joshua Calls Israel to Remain Faithful (Joshua 23:1–16)** — Near the end of his life, Joshua gathers Israel's leaders and warns them not to abandon the God who has given them victory.

Joshua reminded Israel's leaders that God had fought for them and fulfilled His promises. He urged obedience to Moses' law and warned against the gods and relationships of the nations remaining in the land. If Israel turned to idolatry, those nations would bring trouble and Israel would lose the land.

**Joshua Renews the Covenant Before His Death (Joshua 24:1–33)** — Joshua gathers Israel at Shechem, recounts God's faithfulness, and challenges the people to choose whom they will serve before his death.

Joshua recalled God's faithfulness from Abraham through Egypt, the wilderness, and Canaan. He urged Israel to put away foreign gods and declared that he and his household would serve the Lord. Israel affirmed its commitment, and Joshua established a covenant at Shechem. Joshua died at 110; Joseph's bones were buried at Shechem, Eleazar died, and Israel served the Lord throughout Joshua's life and the elders' lives.

# Judges
When it happened: Between the death of Joshua and the rise of Israel's first king—roughly the 1300s to the 1000s BC. The judges' years add up to several centuries, but some of them may have ruled at the same time in different regions.
When it was written: The repeated phrase "In those days there was no king in Israel" suggests it was written after Israel had kings.
Where: Throughout the tribal lands of Israel—the hill country of Ephraim, the valley of Jezreel, Gilead east of the Jordan, the far northern city of Dan, and the Philistine cities such as Gaza.
The world at the time: The tribes had no central government and were attacked again and again by neighbors: a king of Mesopotamia, Moab, the Canaanite king Jabin of Hazor, Midian, Ammon, and the Philistines.

**Israel Struggles to Remain Faithful (Judges 1:1–3:6)** — After Joshua's death, Israel fails to drive out many inhabitants of Canaan and begins turning away from God's commandments.

The tribes gained some victories but left many Canaanites in the land. An angel warned that the nations and their gods would become a snare because Israel had broken the covenant. A generation arose that did not know the Lord or His works. Israel served Baal and Ashtaroth; when enemies oppressed them and they cried out, God raised judges to deliver them. After each judge died, the people returned to disobedience.

**God Raises Up Israel's First Deliverers (Judges 3:7–31)** — When Israel turns toward false gods, God allows foreign rulers to oppress the people before raising up Othniel, Ehud, and Shamgar.

God raised Othniel against Cushanrishathaim, and the land rested forty years. Later Ehud, a left-handed Benjaminite, killed Eglon king of Moab and led Israel to victory, bringing eighty years of rest. Shamgar also delivered Israel by killing six hundred Philistines with an ox goad.

**Deborah and Barak Defeat the Canaanites (Judges 4:1–5:31)** — God delivers Israel through Deborah, Barak, and Jael, overcoming a powerful Canaanite army and bringing forty years of peace.

Deborah, a prophetess and judge, summoned Barak to fight Sisera. God threw Sisera's army into confusion. Sisera fled to Jael's tent, where Jael killed him. Deborah and Barak sang of the victory, and the land rested forty years.

**God Calls Gideon to Deliver Israel (Judges 6:1–8:35)** — God chooses Gideon to rescue Israel from Midian, demonstrating His power through a small army before Gideon's later actions bring new trouble.

Midian oppressed Israel for seven years. God called Gideon, who destroyed Baal's altar and sought assurance through signs with a fleece. God reduced his army to three hundred men, who defeated Midian with trumpets, pitchers, lamps, and a shout. Gideon refused kingship, but his golden ephod became a snare. After his death Israel returned to Baal.

**Abimelech Seizes Power Through Violence (Judges 9:1–57)** — Gideon's son Abimelech murders his brothers and becomes ruler at Shechem, but his violent reign ends in destruction and judgment.

With Shechem's support, Abimelech murdered seventy brothers; only Jotham escaped and warned of judgment. After three years Abimelech destroyed Shechem. At Thebez a woman dropped a millstone on his head, and he ordered his armor-bearer to kill him. Scripture describes this as God's judgment.

**Jephthah Delivers Israel from Ammon (Judges 10:1–12:15)** — After further rebellion and oppression, God raises up Jephthah, whose victory over Ammon is followed by a tragic vow and conflict within Israel.

Israel again served foreign gods and was oppressed. Jephthah, driven away by his brothers, became Gilead's leader. God's Spirit came upon him, and he defeated Ammon after making a vow. His daughter mourned her virginity, and the account says Jephthah fulfilled his vow without explicitly describing how. Later conflict with Ephraim brought many deaths; Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon followed as judges.

**God Announces the Birth of Samson (Judges 13:1–25)** — During Philistine oppression, God announces that Samson will be born and set apart from birth to begin delivering Israel.

God allowed the Philistines to rule for forty years. An angel announced to Manoah's wife that she would bear a Nazirite son whose hair was not to be cut. The angel ascended in the altar flame. Samson was born, grew, and the Spirit of the Lord began moving him.

**Samson Battles the Philistines (Judges 14:1–15:20)** — Samson's marriage arrangements and conflicts with the Philistines lead to escalating violence as God uses him against Israel's oppressors.

Samson killed a lion through God's Spirit and later made a riddle from finding honey in its carcass. When Philistines pressured his bride to reveal the answer, conflict followed. Samson killed thirty men, burned Philistine crops with foxes, broke bonds when Judah handed him over, and killed one thousand men with a donkey's jawbone. God gave him water, and Samson judged Israel twenty years.

**Samson's Strength and Final Victory (Judges 16:1–31)** — Samson's relationship with Delilah leads to his capture, but God grants him strength once more to strike the Philistines before his death.

Delilah learned that Samson's uncut hair marked his dedication to God. After his hair was shaved, the Philistines captured, blinded, and imprisoned him. At Dagon's temple Samson prayed for strength and pushed down its pillars, dying with thousands of Philistines. His family buried him.

**Micah Establishes False Worship (Judges 17:1–18:31)** — A man named Micah creates a private shrine, and members of the tribe of Dan later take its idols and priest for themselves.

Micah made images and a private shrine, then hired a Levite as priest. Danites seeking land took the images and priest, destroyed Laish, rebuilt it as Dan, and established the stolen image as worship. The account displays Israel's religious disorder.

**Violence in Gibeah Leads to Civil War (Judges 19:1–20:48)** — A Levite's concubine suffers horrific abuse in Gibeah, leading the tribes of Israel into a devastating war against Benjamin.

At Gibeah wicked men abused a Levite's concubine through the night. The Levite sent pieces of her body throughout Israel, and the tribes demanded Benjamin surrender the guilty men. Benjamin refused. Israel eventually defeated Benjamin after severe losses, leaving only six hundred Benjaminite men.

**Israel Faces the Consequences of Its Own Actions (Judges 21:1–25)** — After nearly destroying Benjamin, the Israelites struggle to preserve the tribe, resorting to troubling decisions as the book closes without national unity.

Israel mourned Benjamin's near destruction after swearing not to give daughters to Benjaminite men. They brought women from Jabeshgilead and later directed remaining men to take women at Shiloh. Benjamin rebuilt its towns. Judges closes: Israel had no king, and everyone did what seemed right in their own eyes.

# Ruth
When it happened: "In the days when the judges ruled" (Ruth 1:1)—the period between Joshua and the first king, Saul, often placed around the 1100s BC. Exact dates are uncertain.
When it was written: The book ends with David's genealogy (Ruth 4:17–22), so it was written down no earlier than David's time; scholars propose dates from the early monarchy to after the exile.
Where: Bethlehem in Judah, and the country of Moab across the Dead Sea to the east.
The world at the time: Israel had no king, and the tribes lived in the cycles of unfaithfulness and rescue described in Judges. Moab was a neighboring people that had at times oppressed Israel (Judges 3:12–30), which makes a Moabite woman's place in David's family line all the more striking.

**Naomi Loses Her Family in Moab (Ruth 1:1–22)** — A famine leads an Israelite family to Moab, where death leaves Naomi and her daughters-in-law widowed before Ruth chooses to remain with her.

During the time when judges led Israel, a famine struck the land. Elimelech left Bethlehem in Judah with Naomi and their sons Mahlon and Chilion for Moab. Elimelech died there. The sons married Orpah and Ruth, but after about ten years both sons also died.

When Naomi heard that God had provided food in Judah, she returned to Bethlehem and urged her daughters-in-law to remain in Moab. Orpah returned to her people, but Ruth refused to leave Naomi. Ruth committed herself to Naomi's people and to the God of Israel.

They arrived in Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest. Naomi asked to be called Mara, meaning bitter, because she believed the Lord had dealt bitterly with her. Yet Ruth faithfully accompanied her into an uncertain future.

**Ruth Finds Favor in the Field of Boaz (Ruth 2:1–23)** — Ruth gathers leftover grain to provide for Naomi and herself, where she meets Boaz, a wealthy relative who shows her kindness and protection.

Naomi had a relative named Boaz, a wealthy man from Elimelech's family. Ruth gathered leftover grain in his field, as Israel's law allowed poor people and foreigners to do. Boaz learned that she was the Moabite woman who had returned with Naomi.

He told Ruth to stay in his fields, protected her from trouble, and let her drink from the workers' water. Boaz had heard of her faithful care for Naomi and prayed that the Lord would reward her. He invited her to eat and instructed his workers to leave extra grain for her.

Naomi recognized that Boaz was a close relative and a possible kinsman-redeemer, a relative who could recover family property and help preserve a deceased relative's family line. Ruth continued in Boaz's fields through the harvests.

**Ruth Seeks Protection Through Boaz (Ruth 3:1–18)** — Naomi encourages Ruth to approach Boaz as a potential family redeemer, and he promises to address her request according to Israel's customs.

Naomi sent Ruth to Boaz at the threshing floor, where grain was separated from husks. Ruth uncovered Boaz's feet and lay down there as Naomi instructed. When Boaz awoke, Ruth requested that he spread his garment over her because he was a near kinsman.

Boaz praised Ruth as a virtuous woman and was willing to act, but another relative had a closer claim. He promised to settle the matter properly. Before Ruth left in the morning, Boaz gave her six measures of barley. Naomi told Ruth to wait, confident that Boaz would resolve the matter that day.

**Boaz Redeems Ruth and Naomi's Family (Ruth 4:1–22)** — Boaz fulfills the responsibilities of a family redeemer, marries Ruth, and their son becomes an ancestor of Israel's future king, David.

At the city gate, Boaz called the nearer relative and ten elders to witness the legal matter. The relative first agreed to redeem Elimelech's land, but declined when Boaz explained that Ruth was also involved so Mahlon's name could be preserved. He gave his shoe to Boaz, surrendering his right.

Boaz publicly acquired the family property and took Ruth as his wife. The Lord enabled Ruth to conceive, and she bore a son. The women of Bethlehem praised God for providing Naomi a family redeemer and named the child Obed.

Obed became the father of Jesse, and Jesse the father of David. Ruth closes with the line from Pharez to David: famine and loss gave way to marriage, a child, and a lasting place in Israel's history.

# 1 Samuel
When it happened: From the end of the judges to the death of King Saul, roughly 1100–1010 BC.
When it was written: 1 and 2 Samuel were originally one book. It continues past Samuel's death (1 Samuel 25:1), and 1 Chronicles 29:29 mentions records kept by Samuel, Nathan, and Gad. Many scholars think it was compiled from earlier records during the monarchy.
Where: Shiloh, Ramah, Mizpeh, Gibeah, Bethlehem, the valley of Elah, the wilderness of Judah around En-gedi, the Philistine cities of Gath and Ziklag, and Mount Gilboa.
The world at the time: The Philistines held the coastal plain and controlled iron-working, so that Israel had few swords (1 Samuel 13:19–22). Pressed by enemies, Israel asked for a king "like all the nations" (1 Samuel 8:5).

**Hannah Prays for a Son (1 Samuel 1:1–2:11)** — A woman named Hannah asks God for a child and promises to dedicate him to the Lord, beginning the life of Samuel.

Hannah prayed at Shiloh and promised to dedicate a son to the Lord. Eli blessed her, and she bore Samuel. When he was weaned, Hannah brought him to Shiloh to serve under Eli. Her prayer praised God's holiness, power, and justice.

**God Rejects Eli's Unfaithful Household (1 Samuel 2:12–3:21)** — While Samuel grows in service to God, Eli's sons misuse their priestly positions, and God announces judgment against their family.

Hophni and Phinehas despised God's offerings and committed wrongdoing. A man of God warned Eli, and God later called Samuel by name. Samuel received the message that judgment would come on Eli's family. As Samuel grew, all Israel recognized him as a prophet.

**Israel Loses the Ark of the Covenant (1 Samuel 4:1–7:2)** — Israel suffers defeat against the Philistines, and the captured ark brings judgment upon their cities before being returned to Israel.

Israel took the ark into battle but was defeated; Hophni and Phinehas died and the Philistines captured it. Eli died on hearing the news. Dagon fell before the ark and Philistine cities suffered affliction, so the Philistines returned it. It came to Kirjathjearim, where it remained for many years.

**Samuel Leads Israel Back to God (1 Samuel 7:3–17)** — Samuel calls Israel to abandon false gods, and God delivers the nation from the Philistines after the people turn toward Him.

Israel put away Baal and Ashtaroth at Samuel's call. At Mizpeh Samuel prayed and offered a sacrifice when Philistines approached; God thundered against them and Israel won. Samuel named a stone Ebenezer to remember God's help and continued judging Israel.

**Israel Demands a King (1 Samuel 8:1–22)** — Israel's elders demand a human king to lead them like neighboring nations, despite Samuel's warning about the burdens of monarchy.

Samuel's sons took bribes, so the elders asked for a king. God said they had rejected His rule, but told Samuel to listen after warning them that a king would take their children, property, and produce. Israel insisted on being like other nations.

**God Chooses Saul as Israel's First King (1 Samuel 9:1–10:27)** — God directs Saul to Samuel, who anoints him as king before Saul is publicly chosen to lead the nation.

Saul searched for his father's donkeys and came to Samuel. God had told Samuel to anoint a Benjaminite ruler. Samuel anointed Saul privately and gave confirming signs. At Mizpeh Saul was chosen publicly, though he hid among the baggage.

**Saul Wins His First Great Victory (1 Samuel 11:1–12:25)** — Saul delivers Jabeshgilead from an Ammonite threat, and Samuel reminds Israel that both king and people remain accountable to God.

Saul gathered Israel and defeated Nahash of Ammon. At Gilgal the kingdom was renewed. Samuel reviewed God's deliverances and warned that king and people must fear and obey God. God sent thunder and rain during harvest as a sign.

**Saul Disobeys God and Loses His Favor (1 Samuel 13:1–15:35)** — Saul repeatedly fails to follow God's instructions, and Samuel announces that the kingdom will not remain with Saul's family.

Saul offered sacrifice without Samuel, and Samuel said his dynasty would not continue. Jonathan's faith helped defeat Philistines, though Saul's rash oath endangered him. Saul later spared Agag and valuable Amalekite livestock against God's command. Samuel declared that obedience was better than sacrifice and that God had rejected Saul.

**God Chooses David to Replace Saul (1 Samuel 16:1–23)** — God sends Samuel to anoint David, the youngest son of Jesse, while Saul begins experiencing distress after God's Spirit departs from him.

God chose David, Jesse's youngest son, rather than judging by outward appearance. Samuel anointed him, and the Spirit came upon David. A troubling spirit afflicted Saul, so David was brought to play the harp and became Saul's armor-bearer.

**David Defeats Goliath (1 Samuel 17:1–58)** — A young shepherd named David confronts the Philistine champion Goliath, trusting God's power rather than weapons or physical strength.

Goliath challenged Israel for forty days. David trusted God, declined Saul's armor, and went with a sling and five stones. He struck Goliath, killed him with the giant's sword, and Israel pursued the fleeing Philistines.

**Saul Becomes Jealous of David (1 Samuel 18:1–20:42)** — David's growing success makes Saul increasingly hostile, while Saul's son Jonathan forms a loyal friendship with David and protects his life.

Jonathan made a covenant with David. Saul became jealous of David's success, tried to kill him, and gave him Michal after David defeated Philistines. Michal helped David escape, and Jonathan confirmed Saul intended to kill him. The friends renewed their covenant and parted.

**David Flees from Saul's Pursuit (1 Samuel 21:1–23:29)** — David becomes a fugitive, finding temporary refuge and protection while Saul continues searching for opportunities to kill him.

David received consecrated bread and Goliath's sword at Nob, then gathered followers at Adullam. Saul had Doeg kill the priests of Nob, but Abiathar escaped to David. David rescued Keilah, fled Saul, and Jonathan encouraged him. A Philistine attack stopped Saul's near capture of David.

**David Spares Saul's Life (1 Samuel 24:1–22)** — David has an opportunity to kill Saul but refuses to harm the king whom God had appointed over Israel.

In a cave near Engedi David cut Saul's robe rather than kill him. He showed Saul the cloth and entrusted judgment to God. Saul admitted David's righteousness and asked him not to destroy his descendants; David promised.

**Abigail Prevents David from Taking Revenge (1 Samuel 25:1–44)** — After Samuel's death, Abigail wisely intervenes when her husband insults David, preventing David from carrying out a violent act of revenge.

Nabal refused David's request for provisions despite David's protection of his shepherds. Abigail brought supplies and persuaded David not to take revenge. Nabal later died after the Lord struck him, and Abigail became David's wife.

**David Spares Saul Again and Leaves Israel (1 Samuel 26:1–27:12)** — David again refuses to kill Saul, but continued danger leads him to seek refuge among the Philistines.

David entered Saul's camp, took his spear and water container, and refused to kill the Lord's anointed. Saul again admitted wrong. David then sought refuge with Achish at Gath, who gave him Ziklag.

**Saul Seeks Guidance Before His Final Battle (1 Samuel 28:1–25)** — As the Philistines prepare for war, Saul receives no answer from God and turns to a forbidden medium who announces his approaching death.

Saul received no answer from God and sought a medium at Endor. Samuel's message declared that the kingdom would go to David and that Saul, his sons, and Israel would fall to the Philistines.

**David Recovers His Captured Families (1 Samuel 29:1–30:31)** — Philistine commanders reject David's participation in their campaign, and he returns to discover that Amalekites have attacked Ziklag.

Philistine commanders rejected David. Amalekites burned Ziklag and took captives, but David sought God, pursued them, and recovered everyone and everything. He established that those guarding supplies shared equally and sent gifts to elders in Judah.

**Saul and Jonathan Die in Battle (1 Samuel 31:1–13)** — The Philistines defeat Israel at Mount Gilboa, killing Jonathan and Saul's other sons before Saul takes his own life.

Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua died at Mount Gilboa. Saul, badly wounded, fell on his sword. Philistines displayed the bodies, but men of Jabeshgilead recovered them and fasted. Saul's reign ended in defeat, while David remained alive but had not yet taken the throne.

# 2 Samuel
When it happened: The forty-year reign of King David, roughly 1010–970 BC—seven and a half years in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:4–5).
When it was written: It continues 1 Samuel, with which it was originally one book. It likely draws on records kept by prophets of David's court such as Nathan and Gad.
Where: Hebron, Jerusalem (the stronghold of Zion), Mahanaim east of the Jordan, and the surrounding lands of the Philistines, Moab, Ammon, Edom, and Syria.
The world at the time: Egypt and Assyria were relatively weak in this era, which allowed a smaller kingdom to grow. David subdued Israel's neighbors and made Jerusalem the political and religious capital.

**David Mourns the Death of Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel 1:1–27)** — David learns that Saul and Jonathan have died in battle and responds with grief rather than celebration.

At Ziklag, David received news of Saul and Jonathan's deaths. He executed the Amalekite who claimed to have killed Saul, mourned with his men, and composed a lament honoring Saul and Jonathan.

**David Becomes King Over Judah (2 Samuel 2:1–3:39)** — David is anointed king in Hebron, but conflict develops between his supporters and those loyal to Saul's surviving son.

Judah anointed David king at Hebron, while Abner made Ishbosheth king over much of Israel. Conflict followed. Abner later offered to bring Israel to David, but Joab killed him in revenge for Asahel. David publicly mourned and condemned the killing.

**David Becomes King Over All Israel (2 Samuel 4:1–5:25)** — Following Ishbosheth's death, Israel's tribes unite under David, who captures Jerusalem and defeats Philistine attacks.

David executed Ishbosheth's murderers. Israel's elders made him king over all Israel. He captured Jerusalem, made Zion his city, and defeated Philistines after seeking God's guidance.

**David Brings the Ark to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6:1–23)** — David brings the ark of the covenant into Jerusalem, and Uzzah's death shows that God's holiness must be respected.

When Uzzah touched the ark, God struck him. After the ark blessed Obededom's household, David brought it to Jerusalem with sacrifices and celebration. Michal despised David's dancing before the Lord.

**God Establishes a Covenant with David (2 Samuel 7:1–29)** — God promises to establish David's royal household and kingdom, declaring that David's son will succeed him and build a house for God's name.

God told Nathan that David would not build Him a house; instead God would establish David's house, an enduring royal line. David's son would build for God's name. David responded with humility and thanksgiving.

**David's Kingdom Grows in Strength (2 Samuel 8:1–10:19)** — David wins victories, establishes justice, shows kindness to Jonathan's son, and defeats an Ammonite alliance.

David defeated neighboring nations and dedicated captured wealth to the Lord. He restored Saul's land to Mephibosheth and gave him a place at the king's table. Israel defeated Ammonite and Syrian forces after Hanun humiliated David's messengers.

**David Sins Against Bathsheba and Uriah (2 Samuel 11:1–27)** — David commits adultery with Bathsheba and arranges her husband's death.

David slept with Bathsheba, Uriah's wife, and she became pregnant. His efforts to cover the sin failed, so he arranged for Uriah to die in battle. David married Bathsheba, but what he had done displeased the Lord.

**Nathan Confronts David's Sin (2 Samuel 12:1–31)** — Nathan exposes David's wrongdoing, and although David confesses, painful consequences follow.

Nathan's parable exposed David's sin. David confessed, but Nathan declared that violence would arise in his household and the child would die. The child died; later Bathsheba bore Solomon, also called Jedidiah.

**Violence Tears David's Family Apart (2 Samuel 13:1–39)** — Amnon assaults Tamar, leading Absalom to arrange Amnon's death and flee.

Amnon forced Tamar and then rejected her. Absalom sheltered Tamar and, two years later, arranged Amnon's murder. Absalom fled to Geshur, while David mourned.

**Absalom Returns and Turns Against David (2 Samuel 14:1–15:37)** — Absalom returns but wins public support and declares himself king.

David allowed Absalom to return, eventually receiving him. Absalom then gained support at the city gate and proclaimed himself king at Hebron. David fled Jerusalem, sent the ark back, and left Hushai to counter Absalom's plans.

**David Faces Betrayal During His Escape (2 Samuel 16:1–17:29)** — As David flees, he encounters loyalty and betrayal while Absalom receives competing advice.

Ziba accused Mephibosheth, and Shimei cursed David. Absalom accepted Hushai's slower plan rather than Ahithophel's immediate attack, giving David time to escape across the Jordan to Mahanaim.

**Absalom Dies and David Mourns (2 Samuel 18:1–19:8)** — David's forces defeat Absalom's rebellion, but Joab kills Absalom against the king's wishes.

David ordered his commanders to deal gently with Absalom. Absalom became caught in an oak, and Joab killed him. David mourned deeply, until Joab warned that his grief dishonored the soldiers who had saved him.

**David Returns to Jerusalem Amid Division (2 Samuel 19:9–20:26)** — David regains his throne, forgives opponents, and faces another rebellion.

David returned, forgave Shimei, and heard Mephibosheth's account of Ziba's deception. Disagreement among the tribes led Sheba to rebel. Joab killed Amasa and pursued Sheba until a wise woman negotiated his death.

**David Addresses an Unresolved Wrong Against Gibeon (2 Samuel 21:1–22)** — A famine leads David to address Saul's violence against the Gibeonites.

God revealed that famine followed Saul's bloodguilt against Gibeon. David handed over seven descendants, while sparing Jonathan's son Mephibosheth. After burial of Saul's household, God answered prayer for the land. Israel also fought Philistine giants.

**David Praises God for Deliverance (2 Samuel 22:1–23:7)** — David celebrates God's protection and reflects on righteous leadership and God's covenant.

David called God his rock, fortress, shield, and deliverer. His final words honored just rule in the fear of God and the everlasting covenant God had made with his house.

**David's Mighty Warriors Are Remembered (2 Samuel 23:8–39)** — The account honors courageous men who served David in Israel's military struggles.

The record names David's mighty men, including Joshebbasshebeth, Eleazar, Shammah, Abishai, and Benaiah. Three warriors brought David water from Bethlehem, which he poured out before the Lord. The list includes Uriah the Hittite.

**David's Census Brings Judgment Upon Israel (2 Samuel 24:1–25)** — David orders a census that brings judgment, but God's mercy stops the destruction after David builds an altar.

David ordered a census despite Joab's warning and then confessed sin. A pestilence killed seventy thousand until God stopped the angel near Jerusalem. David bought Araunah's threshing floor, built an altar, and offered sacrifices; the plague ended.
