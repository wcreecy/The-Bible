# Joshua
When it happened: From the death of Moses to the death of Joshua at 110 (Joshua 24:29). Depending on the date of the exodus, this is placed either around 1400 BC or in the 1200s BC.
When it was written: The author is unnamed; Jewish tradition credits Joshua himself, and the book records that "Joshua wrote these words in the book of the law of God" (Joshua 24:26). Repeated notes that things remain "unto this day" show that at least parts were written some time after the events.
Where: The Jordan River, Gilgal, Jericho, Ai, Gibeon, Mount Ebal, Shiloh, and Shechem in the land of Canaan.
The world at the time: Canaan was a patchwork of walled city-states, each with its own king—Joshua 12 lists thirty-one of them. No single empire controlled the land at this point in the story.

**Joshua 1-2** — After Moses died, the LORD told Joshua to lead Israel across the Jordan: "Be strong and of a good courage... for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest." Joshua sent two spies to Jericho, where Rahab hid them on her roof and let them down through her window by a cord, because she had heard what the LORD had done for Israel. They promised to spare her family if she bound a line of scarlet thread in the window.

**Joshua 3-5** — When the priests carrying the ark stepped into the Jordan at flood season, the waters upstream rose up in a heap, and the people crossed on dry ground. They set up twelve stones from the river at Gilgal as a memorial. The men born in the wilderness were circumcised there, Israel kept the Passover, and the manna stopped. Near Jericho, Joshua met a man with a drawn sword who was the captain of the host of the LORD.

**Joshua 6-8** — For six days Israel marched around Jericho once a day, and on the seventh day seven times. Then the priests blew the trumpets, the people shouted, and the wall fell down flat. Only Rahab and her household were saved. But Achan secretly took silver, gold, and a Babylonish garment from the spoil devoted to the LORD, and Israel was defeated at Ai. When Achan's sin was exposed and judged, Israel took Ai by an ambush. Joshua built an altar on Mount Ebal and read all the words of the law to the people.

**Joshua 9-12** — The people of Gibeon tricked Israel into a treaty by pretending to come from a far country, wearing old clothes and carrying dry, mouldy bread. When five Amorite kings attacked Gibeon, Joshua came to its defence, and "the sun stood still in the midst of heaven" until Israel had won. Joshua led campaigns through the south and then the north, defeating the kings of the land—thirty-one in all.

**Joshua 13-21** — Much land still remained to be taken, but Joshua, now old, divided the land among the tribes by lot. Caleb, at eighty-five, asked for Hebron, the hill country promised to him forty-five years earlier. The tabernacle was set up at Shiloh, cities of refuge were appointed, and the Levites received forty-eight cities. "There failed not ought of any good thing which the LORD had spoken unto the house of Israel; all came to pass."

**Joshua 22-24** — The tribes east of the Jordan returned home and built an altar by the river, which nearly started a war until they explained it was a witness, not a place for sacrifice. In his old age Joshua gathered all Israel at Shechem, retold their history from Abraham onward, and challenged them: "choose you this day whom ye will serve... but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD." The people renewed the covenant. Joshua died at 110, and the bones of Joseph, brought up from Egypt, were buried at Shechem.

# Judges
When it happened: Between the death of Joshua and the rise of Israel's first king—roughly the 1300s to the 1000s BC. The judges' years add up to several centuries, but some of them may have ruled at the same time in different regions.
When it was written: The author is unnamed; Jewish tradition credits Samuel. The repeated phrase "In those days there was no king in Israel" suggests it was written after Israel had kings.
Where: Throughout the tribal lands of Israel—the hill country of Ephraim, the valley of Jezreel, Gilead east of the Jordan, the far northern city of Dan, and the Philistine cities such as Gaza.
The world at the time: The tribes had no central government and were attacked again and again by neighbors: a king of Mesopotamia, Moab, the Canaanite king Jabin of Hazor, Midian, Ammon, and the Philistines.

**Judges 1-2** — After Joshua died, the tribes failed to drive out the Canaanites, and a new generation grew up "which knew not the LORD." The book describes a repeating cycle: Israel served the Baals, the LORD gave them into the hands of their enemies, they cried out, and the LORD raised up judges to deliver them. But whenever a judge died, they turned back and became even worse.

**Judges 3** — Othniel, Caleb's nephew, delivered Israel from a king of Mesopotamia. The left-handed Ehud brought a present to the very fat King Eglon of Moab and killed him with a hidden dagger. Shamgar killed six hundred Philistines with an ox goad.

**Judges 4-5** — When Jabin, a Canaanite king with nine hundred chariots of iron, oppressed Israel, the prophetess Deborah, who judged Israel under a palm tree, summoned Barak to battle. The LORD routed the army of Jabin's captain Sisera by the river Kishon, and Sisera fled on foot to the tent of Jael, who drove a tent nail through his temple as he slept. Deborah and Barak sang of the victory.

**Judges 6-8** — The Midianites swarmed the land "as grasshoppers for multitude." The angel of the LORD found Gideon threshing wheat in a winepress to hide it and called him a "mighty man of valour." Gideon tore down his father's altar to Baal and asked God for signs with a fleece. The LORD cut his army from 32,000 to 300 so that Israel could not boast. At night the three hundred blew trumpets, broke their pitchers, held up their lamps, and cried, "The sword of the LORD, and of Gideon," and the Midianites turned on one another. Gideon refused to be king, but the gold he collected became an ephod that Israel worshipped.

**Judges 9-12** — Gideon's son Abimelech killed seventy of his brothers to make himself king at Shechem, and died when a woman dropped a piece of a millstone on his head. More judges followed. Jephthah, driven out by his half-brothers, was called back to fight Ammon. He made a rash vow to offer whatever came out of his house first if he returned victorious, and it was his only daughter. Later the men of Gilead identified fleeing Ephraimites because they could not say "Shibboleth."

**Judges 13-16** — The angel of the LORD announced to a barren woman that she would bear a son who would be a Nazarite from the womb and "begin to deliver Israel out of the hand of the Philistines." Samson tore a lion apart with his bare hands, killed a thousand Philistines with the jawbone of an ass, and carried off the gates of Gaza. But Delilah, paid by the Philistine lords, coaxed out the secret of his strength. They shaved his head, put out his eyes, and set him grinding in prison. At a feast for their god Dagon, Samson prayed for strength one last time and pulled down the pillars of the house, killing more at his death than in his life.

**Judges 17-21** — The book ends with two dark stories from a time when "there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes." A man named Micah set up an idol and hired a Levite as his priest, and the tribe of Dan took both when it moved to the far north. A Levite's concubine was abused and killed at Gibeah in Benjamin, and the outrage led the other tribes into a war that nearly wiped out the tribe of Benjamin.

# Ruth
When it happened: "In the days when the judges ruled" (Ruth 1:1)—the period between Joshua and the first king, Saul, often placed around the 1100s BC. Exact dates are uncertain.
When it was written: The author is unnamed; Jewish tradition credits Samuel. The book ends with David's genealogy (Ruth 4:17–22), so it was written down no earlier than David's time; scholars propose dates from the early monarchy to after the exile.
Where: Bethlehem in Judah, and the country of Moab across the Dead Sea to the east.
The world at the time: Israel had no king, and the tribes lived in the cycles of unfaithfulness and rescue described in Judges. Moab was a neighboring people that had at times oppressed Israel (Judges 3:12–30), which makes a Moabite woman's place in David's family line all the more striking.

**Ruth 1** — In the days when the judges ruled, there was a famine in the land. A man of Bethlehem named Elimelech took his wife Naomi and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, to live in the country of Moab. There Elimelech died. The sons married Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and after about ten years both sons died as well, leaving Naomi without her husband or her children.

When Naomi heard that the LORD had visited his people by giving them bread, she set out for home and urged her daughters-in-law to go back to their mothers' houses. Orpah kissed her goodbye, but Ruth clung to her: "whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God." The two women reached Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest. Naomi told the women of the town, "Call me not Naomi, call me Mara: for the Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me."

**Ruth 2** — Ruth went out to glean the leftover grain and happened to come to the field of Boaz, a wealthy relative of Elimelech. Boaz had heard how she had cared for Naomi. He let her glean among the sheaves, told his young men not to touch her, shared his meal with her, and had extra grain dropped for her to gather. When Naomi learned whose field it was, she told Ruth that Boaz was one of their near kinsmen. Ruth gleaned in his fields until the end of the barley and wheat harvests.

**Ruth 3** — Naomi then sent Ruth to the threshing floor at night where Boaz was winnowing barley. As Naomi had instructed, Ruth uncovered his feet and lay down. At midnight Boaz woke, and Ruth asked him to spread his skirt over her, "for thou art a near kinsman." Boaz blessed her for this kindness and promised to do all she asked—but there was a kinsman nearer than he. Before dawn he sent her home with six measures of barley.

**Ruth 4** — At the city gate, before ten of the elders, Boaz met the nearer kinsman. The man was willing to redeem Elimelech's land until he learned that whoever bought it must also take Ruth, to raise up the name of the dead. He gave up his right and drew off his shoe, as was the custom in Israel. Boaz bought the land and took Ruth as his wife, and the LORD gave her a son. The women of Bethlehem told Naomi the child would be "a restorer of thy life," and Naomi became his nurse. They named him Obed. Obed was the father of Jesse, and Jesse was the father of David.

# 1 Samuel
When it happened: From the end of the judges to the death of King Saul, roughly 1100–1010 BC.
When it was written: The author is unnamed; 1 and 2 Samuel were originally one book. Jewish tradition holds that Samuel wrote the early part and the prophets Nathan and Gad completed it (compare 1 Chronicles 29:29). Many scholars think it was compiled from earlier records during the monarchy.
Where: Shiloh, Ramah, Mizpeh, Gibeah, Bethlehem, the valley of Elah, the wilderness of Judah around En-gedi, the Philistine cities of Gath and Ziklag, and Mount Gilboa.
The world at the time: The Philistines held the coastal plain and controlled iron-working, so that Israel had few swords (1 Samuel 13:19–22). Pressed by enemies, Israel asked for a king "like all the nations" (1 Samuel 8:5).

**1 Samuel 1-3** — Hannah, childless and provoked by her rival, prayed at the tabernacle in Shiloh so earnestly that the priest Eli thought she was drunk. She vowed that if the LORD gave her a son, she would give him back to the LORD. She bore Samuel, and once he was weaned she brought him to serve under Eli. Eli's own sons were wicked priests. One night the LORD called the boy Samuel by name. Samuel answered, "Speak; for thy servant heareth," and the LORD told him of the judgment coming on Eli's house. All Israel came to know that Samuel was a prophet of the LORD.

**1 Samuel 4-7** — The Philistines defeated Israel and captured the ark of God. Eli's sons were killed, and when Eli heard the news he fell backward and died. In Philistia, the image of Dagon fell face down before the ark and the people were struck with plagues, so they sent the ark back on a cart drawn by two cows. Years later Samuel called Israel to put away their foreign gods, and the LORD thundered against the Philistines at Mizpeh. Samuel set up a stone and called it Ebenezer, saying, "Hitherto hath the LORD helped us."

**1 Samuel 8-12** — When Samuel grew old and his sons proved corrupt, the elders asked for a king "to judge us like all the nations." The LORD told Samuel, "they have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me," yet allowed their request. While searching for his father's lost asses, Saul, a tall young man of Benjamin, came to Samuel, who anointed him. Saul was chosen by lot at Mizpeh, rescued the city of Jabesh-gilead from the Ammonites, and was confirmed as king at Gilgal.

**1 Samuel 13-15** — Saul's reign soon went wrong. He offered a sacrifice himself instead of waiting for Samuel, and later spared King Agag and the best of the Amalekites' livestock when God had commanded their destruction. Samuel told him "to obey is better than sacrifice" and that the LORD had rejected him from being king. Meanwhile Saul's son Jonathan and his armourbearer climbed up to a Philistine garrison and began a great victory.

**1 Samuel 16-17** — The LORD sent Samuel to Bethlehem to anoint one of Jesse's sons, saying, "man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart." He chose the youngest, David, who was keeping the sheep. David came to Saul's court to play the harp when an evil spirit troubled the king. When the giant Goliath of Gath defied the armies of Israel, David went out against him with a sling and five smooth stones, declaring that "the battle is the LORD's," and brought him down with a single stone.

**1 Samuel 18-26** — Jonathan "loved him as his own soul," and the women sang that Saul had slain his thousands and David his ten thousands. Saul's jealousy turned murderous: he threw a javelin at David, and David fled. With Jonathan's help David escaped and lived as an outlaw, gathering a band of men around him. When Saul learned that the priests of Nob had helped David, he had eighty-five of them killed. Twice David had the chance to kill Saul—in a cave at En-gedi and in Saul's camp at night—but he would not lift his hand against "the LORD's anointed." Abigail's wisdom kept David from taking revenge on her foolish husband Nabal, and after Nabal died she became David's wife.

**1 Samuel 27-31** — David took refuge among the Philistines, who gave him the town of Ziklag. As the Philistines gathered for war, Saul, receiving no answer from God, consulted a woman with a familiar spirit at En-dor, and Samuel appeared and told him that he and his sons would die the next day. Meanwhile David pursued the Amalekites who had raided Ziklag and recovered everyone they had taken. On Mount Gilboa the Philistines defeated Israel. Jonathan and his brothers were killed, and the wounded Saul fell on his own sword. The men of Jabesh-gilead recovered the bodies of Saul and his sons and buried them.

# 2 Samuel
When it happened: The forty-year reign of King David, roughly 1010–970 BC—seven and a half years in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:4–5).
When it was written: The author is unnamed; it continues 1 Samuel, with which it was originally one book. It likely draws on records kept by prophets of David's court such as Nathan and Gad.
Where: Hebron, Jerusalem (the stronghold of Zion), Mahanaim east of the Jordan, and the surrounding lands of the Philistines, Moab, Ammon, Edom, and Syria.
The world at the time: Egypt and Assyria were relatively weak in this era, which allowed a smaller kingdom to grow. David subdued Israel's neighbors and made Jerusalem the political and religious capital.

**2 Samuel 1-4** — David learned of the deaths of Saul and Jonathan and lamented, "how are the mighty fallen!" The tribe of Judah anointed David king at Hebron, while Saul's commander Abner made Saul's son Ish-bosheth king over the rest of Israel. A long war followed between the house of Saul and the house of David. Abner eventually came over to David but was murdered by David's commander Joab, and Ish-bosheth was killed by two of his own captains.

**2 Samuel 5-7** — All the tribes of Israel then anointed David king. He captured the stronghold of Zion from the Jebusites, made Jerusalem his city, and defeated the Philistines. As the ark was being brought to Jerusalem, Uzzah touched it and died. Three months later David brought it in with sacrifices and danced before the LORD "with all his might." David wanted to build a house for God, but through the prophet Nathan the LORD promised instead to build a lasting house for David: "thy throne shall be established for ever."

**2 Samuel 8-10** — David defeated the Philistines, Moab, the Syrians, Edom, and the Ammonites. For Jonathan's sake he showed kindness to Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth, who ate at the king's table for the rest of his life.

**2 Samuel 11-12** — At the time when kings went out to battle, David stayed in Jerusalem. He saw Bath-sheba bathing, sent for her, and she conceived. To hide his sin, he arranged for her husband Uriah to be killed in battle and then married her. The prophet Nathan told David a story about a rich man who took a poor man's only lamb. When David burned with anger, Nathan said, "Thou art the man." David confessed, "I have sinned against the LORD." He was forgiven, but the child died. Later Bath-sheba bore another son, Solomon.

**2 Samuel 13-18** — Trouble arose within David's family, as Nathan had warned. David's son Amnon raped his half-sister Tamar, and her brother Absalom killed Amnon and fled. After returning, Absalom won the hearts of the people and proclaimed himself king in Hebron. David fled Jerusalem weeping and crossed the Jordan. In the battle in the wood of Ephraim, Absalom's head caught in an oak, and Joab killed him. David mourned, "O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee."

**2 Samuel 19-24** — David returned to Jerusalem and put down another revolt led by Sheba. The book closes with an appendix: a famine and the Gibeonites' revenge on Saul's house, David's song of deliverance ("The LORD is my rock, and my fortress, and my deliverer"), his last words, a list of his mighty men, and his sinful census of the people. When a plague followed, David bought the threshing floor of Araunah and built an altar there, and the plague was stopped.
