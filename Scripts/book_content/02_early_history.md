# Joshua
When it happened: From the death of Moses to the death of Joshua at 110 (Joshua 24:29). Depending on the date of the exodus, this is placed either around 1400 BC or in the 1200s BC.
When it was written: The book records that "Joshua wrote these words in the book of the law of God" (Joshua 24:26). Repeated notes that things remain "unto this day" show that at least parts were written some time after the events.
Where: The Jordan River, Gilgal, Jericho, Ai, Gibeon, Mount Ebal, Shiloh, and Shechem in the land of Canaan.
The world at the time: Canaan was a patchwork of walled city-states, each with its own king—Joshua 12 lists thirty-one of them. No single empire controlled the land at this point in the story.

**God Appoints Joshua to Lead Israel (Joshua 1:1–18)** — After Moses dies, God commands Joshua to lead Israel into the promised land and encourages him to remain strong, courageous, and obedient.

Moses is dead, and the people are camped across the Jordan from the promised land. God speaks to Joshua, Moses' longtime assistant: “Moses my servant is dead; now therefore arise, go over this Jordan, thou, and all this people, unto the land which I do give to them.” He promises, “Every place that the sole of your foot shall tread upon, that have I given unto you.” “As I was with Moses, so I will be with thee: I will not fail thee, nor forsake thee.”

Three times God tells him, “Be strong and of a good courage.” The key to success is God's word: “This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night.” Then comes one of the most encouraging verses in the Bible: “Have not I commanded thee? Be strong and of a good courage; be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed: for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest.”

Joshua tells the officers to get the people ready, because in three days they will cross the Jordan. He reminds the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh, who already have land on the east side, of their promise to fight alongside their brothers. They answer, “All that thou commandest us we will do, and whithersoever thou sendest us, we will go.”

**Rahab Protects Israel's Spies (Joshua 2:1–24)** — Joshua sends two spies into Jericho, where Rahab protects them and receives a promise that her family will be spared.

Joshua secretly sends two spies across the river to look at the land, especially the walled city of Jericho. They come to the house of a prostitute named Rahab, whose home is built into the city wall. Someone tells the king of Jericho, and he sends men to Rahab's door to arrest the spies. But she has hidden them on her roof under stalks of flax spread out to dry. She tells the king's men that the spies were there but left at dusk, and if they hurry they can catch them. The soldiers rush off toward the river.

Then Rahab goes up to the roof and tells the spies something amazing. “I know that the LORD hath given you the land, and that your terror is fallen upon us.” Everyone in Jericho has heard how God dried up the Red Sea forty years earlier and how Israel defeated King Sihon and King Og. “And as soon as we had heard these things, our hearts did melt.” Then she confesses her faith: “for the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath.” She asks them to promise that when Israel takes the city, they will spare her and her family.

The spies swear to protect her if she keeps their mission secret. She must tie a scarlet cord in her window and bring her whole family into her house. Anyone outside will not be protected. Rahab lets them down by a rope through her window and tells them to hide in the hills for three days. She ties the scarlet cord in the window. The spies return to Joshua and report, “Truly the LORD hath delivered into our hands all the land; for even all the inhabitants of the country do faint because of us.” Rahab later appears in Jesus' family tree (Matthew 1:5).

**God Leads Israel Across the Jordan (Joshua 3:1–4:24)** — God stops the Jordan River so Israel can cross on dry ground, confirming Joshua's leadership and establishing a memorial for future generations.

Israel moves to the edge of the Jordan River. It is harvest time, and the river is flooding over its banks. Joshua tells the people, “Sanctify yourselves: for to morrow the LORD will do wonders among you.” The priests carrying the ark of the covenant will go first, and the people must follow at a distance of about half a mile.

As soon as the priests' feet touch the edge of the water, the river stops flowing. The water piles up in a heap far upstream at a town called Adam, and the riverbed below runs dry. The priests stand in the middle of the Jordan holding the ark, while the whole nation crosses on dry ground, just as their parents crossed the Red Sea.

God tells Joshua to have one man from each tribe pick up a stone from the middle of the riverbed, where the priests are standing. When everyone has crossed, the priests come up out of the river, and the moment their feet reach dry land, the Jordan floods its banks again. The twelve stones are set up at Gilgal, their first camp in the promised land. Joshua tells the people that when their children ask, “What mean these stones?” they should tell them how Israel crossed the Jordan on dry ground. God did it, he says, “That all the people of the earth might know the hand of the LORD, that it is mighty.”

**Israel Renews Its Commitment Before Jericho (Joshua 5:1–15)** — Before entering battle, Israel renews covenant practices, celebrates Passover, and Joshua encounters the commander of the Lord's army.

When the kings of Canaan hear that God dried up the Jordan, their hearts melt with fear. Meanwhile, at Gilgal, God tells Joshua to circumcise all the men born in the wilderness, because this sign of the covenant had not been done during the forty years of wandering. God says, “This day have I rolled away the reproach of Egypt from off you.” Then Israel celebrates the Passover in the promised land for the first time. The next day they eat food grown in Canaan, “And the manna ceased on the morrow after they had eaten of the old corn of the land.” After forty years, the bread from heaven stops.

Near Jericho, Joshua looks up and sees a man standing in front of him with a drawn sword. Joshua walks up to him and asks, “Art thou for us, or for our adversaries?” The man answers, “Nay; but as captain of the host of the LORD am I now come.” Joshua falls on his face and worships. The commander tells him, “Loose thy shoe from off thy foot; for the place whereon thou standest is holy,” the same words God spoke to Moses at the burning bush. The battle ahead belongs to God.

**The Walls of Jericho Fall (Joshua 6:1–27)** — God gives Israel an unusual plan for capturing Jericho, and the city's walls collapse after the people follow His instructions.

Jericho is locked up tight. No one goes in or out. God gives Joshua an unusual battle plan. For six days, the army is to march around the city once a day, with seven priests blowing trumpets made from rams' horns in front of the ark. The people must not shout or even speak. On the seventh day, they must march around the city seven times. Then the priests will give a long blast, and all the people must shout.

They follow the plan exactly. On the seventh day, after the seventh time around, Joshua commands, “Shout; for the LORD hath given you the city.” The people shout, the trumpets blast, and “the wall fell down flat.” The army charges straight in and takes the city.

Everything in Jericho is devoted to God for destruction. The silver, gold, bronze, and iron go into the LORD's treasury. But Joshua keeps the spies' promise. They bring Rahab and her whole family out safely, and she lives among Israel from then on. Then Joshua pronounces a curse: “Cursed be the man before the LORD, that riseth up and buildeth this city Jericho.” Centuries later, a man who rebuilt Jericho lost his oldest and youngest sons, just as Joshua said (1 Kings 16:34). “So the LORD was with Joshua; and his fame was noised throughout all the country.”

**Achan's Sin Brings Defeat at Ai (Joshua 7:1–8:35)** — Israel suffers defeat because of hidden disobedience, but after the wrongdoing is judged, God gives Joshua victory over Ai.

But there is a problem no one knows about. A man named Achan, from the tribe of Judah, secretly took some of the things devoted to God from Jericho. Next, Joshua sends men to spy out the small town of Ai. They report that it will be easy, so only about three thousand soldiers attack. But the men of Ai chase them away and kill thirty-six of them. “The hearts of the people melted, and became as water.” Joshua tears his clothes and falls on his face before the ark until evening.

God tells him, “Get thee up; wherefore liest thou thus upon thy face? Israel hath sinned.” Someone has stolen what belongs to God. The next morning, Joshua brings the tribes forward one by one. The lot falls on Judah, then on Achan's clan, then his family, then Achan himself. Joshua says, “My son, give, I pray thee, glory to the LORD God of Israel.” Achan confesses: “I saw among the spoils a goodly Babylonish garment, and two hundred shekels of silver, and a wedge of gold of fifty shekels weight, then I coveted them, and took them.” They are buried in the ground under his tent. Messengers run and find them there. Then all Israel stones Achan and his family in the Valley of Achor and piles a heap of stones over them.

Now God tells Joshua not to be afraid and to attack Ai again. This time Joshua sets an ambush behind the city. The main army pretends to run away, and the men of Ai chase them out of the city. Then the hidden soldiers rush in and set it on fire. When the men of Ai look back and see the smoke, they are trapped between the two Israelite forces. Afterward, Joshua builds an altar on Mount Ebal, as Moses had commanded, and writes a copy of the law on stones. Half the people stand in front of Mount Gerizim and half in front of Mount Ebal while Joshua reads the blessings and curses. “There was not a word of all that Moses commanded, which Joshua read not before all the congregation of Israel.”

**The Gibeonites Deceive Israel (Joshua 9:1–27)** — The people of Gibeon trick Israel into making a peace agreement, revealing the consequences of acting without seeking God's guidance.

The kings of Canaan join forces to fight Israel. But the people of Gibeon, a large city nearby, come up with a trick. They dress in worn-out clothes and patched sandals, load their donkeys with old sacks and cracked wineskins, and carry dry, moldy bread. They come to Joshua's camp and say, “We be come from a far country: now therefore make ye a league with us.” They show their stale bread as proof of their long journey.

The Israelite leaders look at the evidence, “and asked not counsel at the mouth of the LORD.” Joshua makes a peace treaty with them, and the leaders swear an oath to let them live. Three days later, Israel learns the truth: the Gibeonites live just a short distance away. The people are angry, but the leaders say, “We have sworn unto them by the LORD God of Israel: now therefore we may not touch them.” So Joshua lets them live, but makes them servants, “hewers of wood and drawers of water” for the house of God.

**God Gives Israel Victory Over Southern Kings (Joshua 10:1–43)** — Joshua leads Israel against an alliance of southern Canaanite kings, and God grants victory through battle and extraordinary signs.

When Adonizedek, the king of Jerusalem, hears that Gibeon has made peace with Israel, he joins with four other Amorite kings to attack Gibeon. The Gibeonites send an urgent message to Joshua: “Come up to us quickly, and save us.” Joshua marches all night with his army and takes the enemy by surprise. God throws them into a panic, and as they flee, He sends huge hailstones from the sky. More of them die from the hail than from the sword.

Then Joshua prays in front of all Israel: “Sun, stand thou still upon Gibeon; and thou, Moon, in the valley of Ajalon. And the sun stood still, and the moon stayed, until the people had avenged themselves upon their enemies.” The sun stops in the middle of the sky for about a whole day. “And there was no day like that before it or after it, that the LORD hearkened unto the voice of a man: for the LORD fought for Israel.”

The five kings hide in a cave at Makkedah. Joshua has the cave sealed with large stones while the army finishes the battle. Afterward, he brings the kings out and tells his commanders to put their feet on the kings' necks, a sign of complete victory. He tells his men, “Fear not, nor be dismayed, be strong and of good courage: for thus shall the LORD do to all your enemies.” Then the kings are executed. Joshua's army sweeps through the southern part of Canaan, taking city after city, “because the LORD God of Israel fought for Israel.”

**Israel Defeats the Northern Kings (Joshua 11:1–12:24)** — A powerful alliance of northern kings gathers against Israel, but Joshua defeats their armies and secures victories throughout much of Canaan.

Now the kings of the north gather under Jabin, king of Hazor, the most powerful city in the region. They bring a huge army, “even as the sand that is upon the sea shore in multitude,” with many horses and chariots, and camp by the waters of Merom. God tells Joshua, “Be not afraid because of them: for to morrow about this time will I deliver them up all slain before Israel.” Joshua attacks suddenly and defeats them. As God commanded, he cuts the hamstrings of their horses and burns their chariots, so Israel will trust in God rather than in weapons. Then he captures and burns Hazor.

The wars go on for a long time, but in the end, “Joshua took the whole land, according to all that the LORD said unto Moses.” He even defeats the Anakim, the giants who had frightened the spies forty years before. Joshua gives the land to Israel as an inheritance, tribe by tribe. “And the land rested from war.” Chapter 12 lists the kings Israel defeated, two east of the Jordan under Moses and thirty-one west of the Jordan under Joshua.

**God Prepares Israel to Divide the Land (Joshua 13:1–14:15)** — As Joshua grows old, God commands the remaining land to be assigned among Israel's tribes, while Caleb receives his promised inheritance.

Many years pass, and Joshua is now old. God tells him, “Thou art old and stricken in years, and there remaineth yet very much land to be possessed.” Some areas, like the Philistine cities along the coast, still have not been taken. But it is time to divide the land among the tribes. The book describes the land Moses had already given to Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh east of the Jordan. The tribe of Levi receives no land of its own, because “the LORD God of Israel was their inheritance.”

Before the dividing begins, Caleb comes to Joshua. Forty-five years earlier, he and Joshua were the only spies who trusted God. Now he is eighty-five years old. He says, “As yet I am as strong this day as I was in the day that Moses sent me.” He asks for the very hill country where the giants lived: “Now therefore give me this mountain.” Joshua blesses him and gives him Hebron. It becomes Caleb's inheritance, “because that he wholly followed the LORD God of Israel.”

**Israel Receives Its Tribal Inheritances (Joshua 15:1–19:51)** — Joshua and Israel's leaders assign territories to the tribes, establishing the boundaries and towns each family group will inherit.

These chapters describe the borders and cities of each tribe's land, assigned by casting lots before the LORD. Judah receives a large territory in the south. Caleb drives the giants out of Hebron and offers his daughter Achsah in marriage to whoever captures the nearby city of Kirjathsepher. His nephew Othniel takes it and marries her. Achsah asks her father for a blessing: “give me also springs of water.” So he gives her “the upper springs, and the nether springs.” But Judah cannot drive the Jebusites out of Jerusalem, and they remain there for centuries.

The descendants of Joseph, Ephraim and Manasseh, receive land in the center of the country. They complain that their share is too small. Joshua tells them, “If thou be a great people, then get thee up to the wood country, and cut down for thyself there,” clearing the forests. They worry about the Canaanites' iron chariots, but Joshua says they will drive them out.

The whole community gathers at Shiloh and sets up the tabernacle there. Seven tribes still have not received their land. Joshua asks them, “How long are ye slack to go to possess the land, which the LORD God of your fathers hath given you?” He sends three men from each tribe to survey the remaining land and divide it into seven parts. Then he casts lots for Benjamin, Simeon, Zebulun, Issachar, Asher, Naphtali, and Dan. When everything is divided, the people give Joshua his own town, Timnath-serah, in the hill country of Ephraim.

**God Establishes Cities of Refuge and Levitical Cities (Joshua 20:1–21:45)** — God provides cities of refuge for accidental deaths and assigns towns to the Levites throughout Israel's territories.

As Moses commanded, Israel sets apart six cities of refuge, three on each side of the Jordan, including Kedesh, Shechem, and Hebron. Anyone who kills a person by accident can flee there and be safe from relatives seeking revenge, until he has a fair trial and until the death of the high priest.

The Levites come to Eleazar the priest and Joshua and ask for the towns Moses promised them. They receive forty-eight cities with pastureland scattered throughout the land, so the priests and Levites can live among all the tribes and teach them God's law.

The section ends with a summary of God's faithfulness. God gave Israel all the land He had sworn to their ancestors, gave them rest on every side, and gave their enemies into their hands. “There failed not ought of any good thing which the LORD had spoken unto the house of Israel; all came to pass.”

**The Eastern Tribes Return Home (Joshua 22:1–34)** — The tribes east of the Jordan build an altar that nearly causes conflict, but an explanation prevents fighting among the Israelites.

The war is over, so Joshua calls the soldiers of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh. They have kept their promise for many years. He praises them, blesses them, and sends them home across the Jordan with great wealth from the war. He urges them “to love the LORD your God, and to walk in all his ways.”

On their way home, by the Jordan River, these tribes build a huge altar. When the other tribes hear about it, they think the eastern tribes are turning away from God by building a rival altar, and they gather at Shiloh to go to war against them. First they send Phinehas the priest and ten leaders to confront them. They remind them how God punished all Israel for the sin at Peor and for Achan's sin.

The eastern tribes explain that they never meant to offer sacrifices there. They were afraid that one day the western tribes' children would say the Jordan separated them from Israel and from the LORD. So they built a copy of God's altar as a witness that they too belong to Him. Phinehas and the leaders are pleased, and the war is called off. The eastern tribes name the altar Ed, meaning witness, saying, “it shall be a witness between us that the LORD is God.”

**Joshua Calls Israel to Remain Faithful (Joshua 23:1–16)** — Near the end of his life, Joshua gathers Israel's leaders and warns them not to abandon the God who has given them victory.

A long time later, Joshua is very old. He calls together Israel's leaders and reminds them of all God has done: “for the LORD your God is he that hath fought for you.” God will drive out the nations that remain, but Israel must obey everything written in the law of Moses. “One man of you shall chase a thousand: for the LORD your God, he it is that fighteth for you.” “Take good heed therefore unto yourselves, that ye love the LORD your God.”

But Joshua also warns them. If they marry the people of the nations that remain and worship their gods, those nations will become “snares and traps unto you, and scourges in your sides, and thorns in your eyes,” until Israel is driven out of the land. Then he says, “behold, this day I am going the way of all the earth.” He reminds them that “not one thing hath failed of all the good things which the LORD your God spake concerning you.” And just as God has kept every good promise, He will also keep His warnings if they break His covenant.

**Joshua Renews the Covenant Before His Death (Joshua 24:1–33)** — Joshua gathers Israel at Shechem, recounts God's faithfulness, and challenges the people to choose whom they will serve before his death.

Joshua gathers all the tribes at Shechem, where Abraham first received God's promise in Canaan, centuries before. He retells their history as God's own words. Their ancestors, including Abraham's father, Terah, worshiped other gods beyond the Euphrates River. God called Abraham, gave him Isaac, and gave Isaac Jacob and Esau. He sent Moses and Aaron, struck Egypt, parted the sea, defeated the kings, and turned Balaam's curses into blessings. “And I have given you a land for which ye did not labour, and cities which ye built not.”

Then Joshua puts the choice before them: “And if it seem evil unto you to serve the LORD, choose you this day whom ye will serve... but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD.” The people answer, “God forbid that we should forsake the LORD, to serve other gods.” But Joshua says, “Ye cannot serve the LORD: for he is an holy God; he is a jealous God.” He wants them to understand how serious their promise is. They insist, “Nay; but we will serve the LORD.” Joshua says, “Ye are witnesses against yourselves,” and they answer, “We are witnesses.”

Joshua writes their promise in the book of the law and sets up a large stone under an oak tree as a witness. Then he sends everyone home. Joshua dies at 110 and is buried in his town of Timnath-serah. “And Israel served the LORD all the days of Joshua, and all the days of the elders that overlived Joshua.” The Israelites bury Joseph's bones at Shechem, keeping the promise made more than four hundred years earlier. Eleazar the priest also dies. The book of Joshua ends with God's promises kept and the people's promise still to be tested.

# Judges
When it happened: Between the death of Joshua and the rise of Israel's first king—roughly the 1300s to the 1000s BC. The judges' years add up to several centuries, but some of them may have ruled at the same time in different regions.
When it was written: The repeated phrase "In those days there was no king in Israel" suggests it was written after Israel had kings.
Where: Throughout the tribal lands of Israel—the hill country of Ephraim, the valley of Jezreel, Gilead east of the Jordan, the far northern city of Dan, and the Philistine cities such as Gaza.
The world at the time: The tribes had no central government and were attacked again and again by neighbors: a king of Mesopotamia, Moab, the Canaanite king Jabin of Hazor, Midian, Ammon, and the Philistines.

**Israel Struggles to Remain Faithful (Judges 1:1–3:6)** — After Joshua's death, Israel fails to drive out many inhabitants of Canaan and begins turning away from God's commandments.

After Joshua dies, the tribes continue fighting for their land. Judah goes first and wins several battles. They capture a cruel king named Adonibezek and cut off his thumbs and big toes. He admits, “Threescore and ten kings, having their thumbs and their great toes cut off, gathered their meat under my table: as I have done, so God hath requited me.” Caleb's nephew Othniel captures a city and marries Caleb's daughter.

But then the book gives a long, sad list. Tribe after tribe fails to drive out the Canaanites. Benjamin does not drive out the Jebusites from Jerusalem. Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, and Naphtali all let Canaanites live among them, often making them forced laborers instead. The tribe of Dan is pushed back into the hills.

The angel of the LORD comes to Bochim and tells the people that because they made treaties with the Canaanites and did not tear down their altars, “I will not drive them out from before you; but they shall be as thorns in your sides, and their gods shall be a snare unto you.” The people weep loudly, so the place is named Bochim, meaning weepers.

Then the book explains the pattern that will repeat again and again. Joshua's generation dies, “and there arose another generation after them, which knew not the LORD.” The Israelites do evil and worship Baal and other gods. So God hands them over to enemies who oppress them. When they cry out in their misery, God raises up judges, leaders and deliverers who rescue them. But when each judge dies, the people turn back and act even worse than before. God leaves some nations in the land to test Israel, and the Israelites marry them and serve their gods.

**God Raises Up Israel's First Deliverers (Judges 3:7–31)** — When Israel turns toward false gods, God allows foreign rulers to oppress the people before raising up Othniel, Ehud, and Shamgar.

The cycle begins. Israel forgets God and worships Baal, so God lets a king from Mesopotamia oppress them for eight years. When they cry out, God raises up Othniel, Caleb's nephew. “The Spirit of the LORD came upon him,” and he defeats the king. The land has peace for forty years.

When Othniel dies, Israel does evil again. God strengthens Eglon, king of Moab, who takes over Jericho and rules Israel for eighteen years. Eglon is “a very fat man.” When Israel cries out, God raises up Ehud, a left-handed man from the tribe of Benjamin. Ehud makes a short double-edged sword, about a foot and a half long, and straps it to his right thigh under his clothes, where guards would not expect it. He brings Israel's tribute money to Eglon. Then he says, “I have a secret errand unto thee, O king,” and the king sends everyone out of the room. Ehud says, “I have a message from God unto thee.” As Eglon rises from his seat, Ehud draws the sword with his left hand and plunges it into the king's belly. The fat closes over the blade, and Ehud leaves it there. He locks the doors and escapes.

The servants find the doors locked and think the king is using the toilet, so they wait and wait until they are embarrassed. Finally they unlock the door and find their king dead. By then Ehud has escaped and blown a trumpet in the hills of Ephraim. He leads the Israelites down to seize the river crossings, and they kill about ten thousand Moabite soldiers. The land has peace for eighty years. After Ehud comes Shamgar, who kills six hundred Philistines with an ox goad, a long stick used to drive cattle.

**Deborah and Barak Defeat the Canaanites (Judges 4:1–5:31)** — God delivers Israel through Deborah, Barak, and Jael, overcoming a powerful Canaanite army and bringing forty years of peace.

After Ehud dies, Israel does evil again. God lets Jabin, a Canaanite king, rule over them for twenty years. His army commander, Sisera, has nine hundred iron chariots and cruelly oppresses Israel. At this time, the judge of Israel is a woman, Deborah, a prophetess. She sits under a palm tree in the hills, and the Israelites come to her to settle their disputes.

Deborah calls for a man named Barak and gives him God's command to gather ten thousand men at Mount Tabor. God will draw Sisera and his chariots to the Kishon River and give them into Barak's hand. But Barak says, “If thou wilt go with me, then I will go: but if thou wilt not go with me, then I will not go.” Deborah agrees, but warns that the honor will not be his, “for the LORD shall sell Sisera into the hand of a woman.”

Sisera brings his chariots, and Deborah tells Barak, “Up; for this is the day.” God throws the enemy army into confusion. A storm seems to have turned the plain to mud, and the river sweeps chariots away. Barak's men chase them, and not a single soldier survives. But Sisera jumps from his chariot and escapes on foot. He runs to the tent of Jael, the wife of Heber, whose clan is at peace with King Jabin. She comes out and says, “Turn in, my lord, turn in to me; fear not.” She covers him with a blanket. When he asks for water, she gives him milk instead. He tells her to stand at the door and say no one is there. As he sleeps, worn out, Jael takes a tent peg and a hammer, creeps up to him, and drives the peg through his temple into the ground, “so he died.” When Barak arrives, chasing Sisera, Jael shows him the body.

Chapter 5 is the Song of Deborah, one of the oldest poems in the Bible. “Awake, awake, Deborah: awake, awake, utter a song.” It praises the tribes that came to fight and scolds those that stayed home. “The stars in their courses fought against Sisera. The river of Kishon swept them away.” “Blessed above women shall Jael the wife of Heber the Kenite be.” Then the song pictures Sisera's mother looking out her window, wondering, “Why is his chariot so long in coming?” Her ladies assure her that he is just dividing the spoils. But he will never come home. The song ends, “So let all thine enemies perish, O LORD: but let them that love him be as the sun when he goeth forth in his might.” The land has peace for forty years.

**God Calls Gideon to Deliver Israel (Judges 6:1–8:35)** — God chooses Gideon to rescue Israel from Midian, demonstrating His power through a small army before Gideon's later actions bring new trouble.

Again Israel does evil, and God gives them into the hands of the Midianites for seven years. Every harvest, the Midianites and other desert tribes swarm in “as grasshoppers for multitude,” with countless camels, and destroy everything. The Israelites hide in caves and mountains. When they cry out, God sends a prophet to remind them that they have disobeyed Him.

Then the angel of the LORD sits under an oak tree where a young man named Gideon is secretly threshing wheat in a winepress, hiding from the Midianites. The angel says, “The LORD is with thee, thou mighty man of valour.” Gideon answers, “Oh my Lord, if the LORD be with us, why then is all this befallen us?” God tells him to go and save Israel. Gideon objects, “my family is poor in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house.” God says, “Surely I will be with thee.” Gideon brings an offering, and when the angel touches it with his staff, fire bursts from the rock and burns it up. That night, at God's command, Gideon tears down his father's altar to Baal and the wooden pole beside it. In the morning, the townspeople want to kill him, but his father Joash says, “Will ye plead for Baal?” If Baal is a god, let him defend himself. So Gideon is nicknamed Jerubbaal, meaning let Baal contend.

The Midianites gather for another raid, and the Spirit of the LORD comes on Gideon. He calls the tribes together, but he still wants proof. He lays a wool fleece on the ground and asks God to make the fleece wet with dew while the ground stays dry. In the morning he wrings out a bowl of water. Then he asks for the opposite, and the next morning the fleece is dry and the ground is wet.

Gideon has 32,000 men, but God says that is too many, or Israel will boast that they saved themselves. Anyone who is afraid may go home, and 22,000 leave. Still too many. God tells Gideon to take the men down to the water. Most kneel down to drink, but three hundred scoop water in their hands and lap it like dogs. God chooses those three hundred. That night God sends Gideon to sneak into the enemy camp, where he overhears a soldier describing a dream of a barley loaf rolling into the camp and knocking down a tent. His friend says it means God has given Midian into Gideon's hand.

Gideon gives each of his three hundred men a trumpet and an empty jar with a torch hidden inside. They surround the camp in the dark. At Gideon's signal, they blow their trumpets, smash their jars, hold up their torches, and shout, “The sword of the LORD, and of Gideon.” The Midianites panic and in the confusion attack one another, then flee. Gideon chases the Midianite kings across the Jordan and captures and executes them. He punishes the towns of Succoth and Penuel for refusing to feed his exhausted men.

The Israelites ask Gideon to become their king and start a royal family. He refuses: “I will not rule over you, neither shall my son rule over you: the LORD shall rule over you.” But he asks for the gold earrings from the spoil and makes a golden ephod, a priestly garment, which he sets up in his town. “And all Israel went thither a whoring after it: which thing became a snare unto Gideon.” Gideon has seventy sons by many wives, and one more son, Abimelech, by a concubine in Shechem. The land has peace for forty years. But as soon as Gideon dies, Israel goes back to worshiping Baal.

**Abimelech Seizes Power Through Violence (Judges 9:1–57)** — Gideon's son Abimelech murders his brothers and becomes ruler at Shechem, but his violent reign ends in destruction and judgment.

Gideon had refused to be king, but his son Abimelech wants the crown. He convinces the people of Shechem, his mother's town, that it is better to be ruled by one man than by Gideon's seventy sons. They give him money from the temple of Baal, and he hires reckless, worthless men. He goes to his father's house and murders his seventy brothers on a single stone. Only the youngest, Jotham, escapes by hiding. Then the people of Shechem make Abimelech king.

Jotham climbs Mount Gerizim and shouts a parable to the people below. The trees once went looking for a king. They asked the olive tree, the fig tree, and the grapevine, but each refused to give up its useful work. Finally they asked the bramble, a thorny bush that gives no shade, and it said that if they truly wanted it as king, they could take shelter in its shade, or else “let fire come out of the bramble, and devour the cedars of Lebanon.” Jotham warns that if they have not acted honorably, fire will come from Abimelech and destroy Shechem, and fire will come from Shechem and destroy Abimelech. Then he runs away.

Three years later, God sends an evil spirit between Abimelech and the people of Shechem, and they turn against him. Abimelech destroys the city and scatters salt over it. About a thousand people hide in the stronghold of their temple, and Abimelech piles branches against it and burns them all alive. Then he attacks the town of Thebez. The people flee to a strong tower. As Abimelech comes close to set the door on fire, a woman on the roof drops a millstone on his head and cracks his skull. He calls to his armor-bearer, “Draw thy sword, and slay me, that men say not of me, A woman slew him.” The young man runs him through, and he dies. So God repaid Abimelech for murdering his brothers, and Jotham's curse came true.

**Jephthah Delivers Israel from Ammon (Judges 10:1–12:15)** — After further rebellion and oppression, God raises up Jephthah, whose victory over Ammon is followed by a tragic vow and conflict within Israel.

Two judges, Tola and Jair, lead Israel quietly for many years. Jair has thirty sons who ride thirty donkeys. Then Israel turns to worship the gods of every nation around them, and God gives them into the hands of the Philistines and the Ammonites for eighteen years. When they cry out, God answers, “Go and cry unto the gods which ye have chosen; let them deliver you.” But the people confess their sin, get rid of their foreign gods, and serve the LORD, “and his soul was grieved for the misery of Israel.”

In Gilead lives Jephthah, “a mighty man of valour, and he was the son of an harlot.” His half-brothers drove him away so he would not share their inheritance, and he became the leader of a band of outlaws. Now that the Ammonites are attacking, the elders of Gilead come and beg him to lead them. Jephthah agrees on the condition that he will be their head. First he sends messages to the Ammonite king, explaining Israel's history and claim to the land. The king ignores him.

The Spirit of the LORD comes on Jephthah. But before the battle, he makes a rash vow: “whatsoever cometh forth of the doors of my house to meet me, when I return in peace from the children of Ammon, shall surely be the LORD'S, and I will offer it up for a burnt offering.” God gives him a great victory. When Jephthah comes home, his only child, his daughter, comes out dancing with tambourines to greet him. He tears his clothes and cries, “Alas, my daughter! thou hast brought me very low.” “I have opened my mouth unto the LORD, and I cannot go back.” She tells him to keep his vow, but asks for two months to go into the hills with her friends and mourn that she will never marry. When she returns, he “did with her according to his vow.” Some readers believe he sacrificed her, which the law of Moses strictly forbade. Others believe he dedicated her to God's service for life, so that she never married. Every year afterward, the young women of Israel spent four days remembering her.

Then the men of Ephraim angrily accuse Jephthah of not calling them to the battle and threaten to burn his house down. A civil war breaks out. The men of Gilead capture the river crossings. When fleeing Ephraimites try to cross, they are asked to say “Shibboleth.” People from Ephraim cannot pronounce the sh sound and say “Sibboleth” instead, so they are caught and killed, 42,000 in all. Jephthah judges Israel six years. After him come three more judges: Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon.

**God Announces the Birth of Samson (Judges 13:1–25)** — During Philistine oppression, God announces that Samson will be born and set apart from birth to begin delivering Israel.

Again Israel does evil, and God lets the Philistines rule over them for forty years. A man named Manoah from the tribe of Dan has a wife who cannot have children. The angel of the LORD appears to her and says she will have a son. She must not drink wine or eat anything unclean, because the boy will be a Nazarite from birth. “No razor shall come on his head,” and “he shall begin to deliver Israel out of the hand of the Philistines.”

She runs to tell her husband. Manoah prays for the man of God to come back and teach them how to raise the boy, and the angel appears again. Manoah offers to cook him a young goat and asks his name. The angel answers, “Why askest thou thus after my name, seeing it is secret?” Manoah offers the goat on a rock as a sacrifice, and as the flame rises toward heaven, the angel goes up in the flame. Manoah and his wife fall on their faces. Manoah says, “We shall surely die, because we have seen God.” But his wife sensibly replies that if God meant to kill them, He would not have accepted their offering or told them all this. The woman gives birth to a son and names him Samson. The boy grows, the LORD blesses him, “And the Spirit of the LORD began to move him.”

**Samson Battles the Philistines (Judges 14:1–15:20)** — Samson's marriage arrangements and conflicts with the Philistines lead to escalating violence as God uses him against Israel's oppressors.

When Samson grows up, he sees a Philistine woman in the town of Timnath and tells his parents, “Get her for me; for she pleaseth me well.” His parents object, but the book explains, “it was of the LORD, that he sought an occasion against the Philistines.” On the way to Timnath, a young lion roars at Samson. “The Spirit of the LORD came mightily upon him,” and he tears the lion apart with his bare hands. Later, he finds a swarm of bees and honey in the lion's carcass. He scoops some out and eats it, even though a Nazarite was not supposed to touch a dead body.

At his wedding feast, Samson gives the thirty Philistine guests a riddle. If they solve it in seven days, he will give them thirty sets of clothes. If not, they must give him thirty sets. The riddle is: “Out of the eater came forth meat, and out of the strong came forth sweetness.” They cannot solve it, so they threaten his bride, saying they will burn her and her father's house unless she gets the answer. She cries and nags Samson all week until he tells her. On the seventh day the men say, “What is sweeter than honey? and what is stronger than a lion?” Samson answers, “If ye had not plowed with my heifer, ye had not found out my riddle.” Furious, he goes to the city of Ashkelon, kills thirty Philistines, and gives their clothes to the guests. Then he goes home, and his bride is given to his best man.

Later, Samson returns to visit his wife, but her father will not let him in. In revenge, Samson catches three hundred foxes, ties them tail to tail in pairs with a burning torch between each pair, and lets them loose in the Philistines' grain fields, vineyards, and olive groves. The Philistines burn his wife and her father to death. Samson attacks them in a great slaughter and then hides in a cave.

The Philistines march into Judah to capture him. Three thousand men of Judah come to Samson and say, “Knowest thou not that the Philistines are rulers over us?” They tie him with two new ropes and hand him over. But when the Philistines rush at him shouting, the Spirit comes on him, and the ropes melt like burned flax. He grabs the fresh jawbone of a donkey and kills a thousand men with it. He sings, “With the jawbone of an ass, heaps upon heaps, with the jaw of an ass have I slain a thousand men.” Then he is so thirsty he thinks he will die. He cries out to God, and God splits open a hollow place, and water flows out. Samson judges Israel for twenty years.

**Samson's Strength and Final Victory (Judges 16:1–31)** — Samson's relationship with Delilah leads to his capture, but God grants him strength once more to strike the Philistines before his death.

Samson goes to the Philistine city of Gaza and spends the night with a prostitute. The men of the city wait at the city gate to kill him at dawn. But at midnight Samson gets up, pulls up the city gates, posts and all, puts them on his shoulders, and carries them to the top of a hill near Hebron, nearly forty miles away.

Later he falls in love with a woman named Delilah in the valley of Sorek. The Philistine lords each offer her eleven hundred pieces of silver to find out the secret of his strength. Delilah asks him, and he lies: tie him with seven fresh bowstrings, and he will be weak. She does it while he sleeps, then cries, “The Philistines be upon thee, Samson.” He snaps the bowstrings like thread. Next he says new ropes, and he breaks them too. Then he tells her to weave his hair into a loom, and he pulls it all loose. Delilah complains, “How canst thou say, I love thee, when thine heart is not with me?” She nags him every day until “his soul was vexed unto death.” Finally he tells her the truth: “if I be shaven, then my strength will go from me.”

Delilah sends for the Philistine lords, and they bring the money. She lulls Samson to sleep on her knees and calls a man to shave off his seven braids. Then she cries, “The Philistines be upon thee, Samson.” He wakes up, thinking he will shake himself free as before, but “he wist not that the LORD was departed from him.” The Philistines seize him, gouge out his eyes, and take him to Gaza. They bind him with bronze chains, and he grinds grain in the prison like an animal. But his hair begins to grow again.

The Philistine lords gather for a great feast to thank their god Dagon for giving them Samson. Drunk and happy, they bring him out to entertain them. About three thousand men and women are watching from the roof of the temple. Samson asks the boy leading him to let him feel the two main pillars holding up the building. Then he prays, “O Lord GOD, remember me, I pray thee, and strengthen me, I pray thee, only this once.” He braces his hands against the two pillars and says, “Let me die with the Philistines.” He pushes with all his might, and the temple collapses on the lords and everyone in it. “So the dead which he slew at his death were more than they which he slew in his life.” His family buries him in his father's tomb. He had judged Israel twenty years.

**Micah Establishes False Worship (Judges 17:1–18:31)** — A man named Micah creates a private shrine, and members of the tribe of Dan later take its idols and priest for themselves.

The last chapters of Judges show how bad things became. A man named Micah, from the hill country of Ephraim, steals eleven hundred pieces of silver from his mother. When he hears her curse the thief, he confesses and gives it back. She is so pleased that she has part of it made into an idol. Micah sets up a shrine in his house, makes an ephod and household idols, and makes one of his sons his priest. The book explains, “In those days there was no king in Israel, but every man did that which was right in his own eyes.”

One day a young Levite from Bethlehem passes through, looking for a place to live. Micah hires him as his personal priest for ten shekels a year, plus clothes and food. Micah says, “Now know I that the LORD will do me good, seeing I have a Levite to my priest.” He thinks having the right kind of priest will make God bless his idol worship.

Meanwhile, the tribe of Dan still has not conquered the land given to them, so they send five spies to find a new home. At Micah's house, they ask the Levite to inquire of God, and he tells them their journey will succeed. They find the city of Laish, far to the north, where the people live quietly and peacefully, with no allies nearby. Six hundred armed men from Dan set out to attack. On the way, they stop at Micah's house and steal his idol, ephod, and household gods. When the Levite protests, they ask, “Is it better for thee to be a priest unto the house of one man, or that thou be a priest unto a tribe and a family in Israel?” He gladly goes with them. Micah chases after them and cries, “Ye have taken away my gods which I made, and the priest, and ye are gone away: and what have I more?” But they are too strong, and he goes home.

The Danites attack Laish, kill its peaceful people, burn the city, rebuild it, and rename it Dan. There they set up Micah's idol and make the Levite and his sons their priests. The book reveals that this Levite was Jonathan, a grandson of Gershom, the son of Moses. Many ancient copies changed the name to Manasseh to hide the shame. His family served as priests to the idol until Israel went into exile.

**Violence in Gibeah Leads to Civil War (Judges 19:1–20:48)** — A Levite's concubine suffers horrific abuse in Gibeah, leading the tribes of Israel into a devastating war against Benjamin.

Now comes one of the darkest stories in the Bible. “In those days, when there was no king in Israel,” a Levite from the hill country of Ephraim takes a concubine, a secondary wife, from Bethlehem. She is unfaithful to him and goes back to her father's house. After four months, the Levite goes to bring her home. Her father welcomes him and keeps him there for several days of eating and drinking. Finally, late in the afternoon, the Levite leaves with his concubine and his servant.

As night falls, they pass the city of Jebus, later called Jerusalem. The servant suggests stopping there, but the Levite refuses to stay in a city of foreigners. They push on to Gibeah, a town of the tribe of Benjamin, expecting fellow Israelites to welcome them. But no one offers them a place to stay, until an old man coming in from the fields takes them home. While they are eating, wicked men of the city, “certain sons of Belial,” surround the house and pound on the door, demanding that the old man bring out his guest so they can rape him. It is like the story of Sodom. The old man begs them not to do this terrible thing and even offers his own virgin daughter and the concubine instead. When the men will not listen, the Levite grabs his concubine and pushes her out the door. They rape and abuse her all night. At dawn she staggers back and collapses at the door, with her hands on the threshold.

In the morning, the Levite opens the door to leave and finds her lying there. He says, “Up, and let us be going. But none answered.” She is dead. He puts her body on his donkey and goes home. Then he takes a knife, cuts her body into twelve pieces, and sends them throughout Israel. Everyone who sees it says, “There was no such deed done nor seen from the day that the children of Israel came up out of the land of Egypt unto this day.”

Four hundred thousand soldiers gather at Mizpeh. They demand that the tribe of Benjamin hand over the guilty men of Gibeah. But Benjamin refuses and prepares for war, with 26,000 soldiers, including seven hundred left-handed slingers who “could sling stones at an hair breadth, and not miss.” On the first day, Benjamin kills 22,000 Israelites. On the second day, 18,000 more. The people weep and fast before God, and God promises victory. On the third day, Israel sets an ambush, just as Joshua did at Ai. Gibeah is burned, and 25,000 Benjamite soldiers are killed. Only six hundred escape to the rock of Rimmon. Then the Israelites destroy the towns of Benjamin, killing people and animals alike.

**Israel Faces the Consequences of Its Own Actions (Judges 21:1–25)** — After nearly destroying Benjamin, the Israelites struggle to preserve the tribe, resorting to troubling decisions as the book closes without national unity.

When the war is over, Israel realizes that the tribe of Benjamin is nearly wiped out. To make things worse, at Mizpeh they had sworn an oath that none of them would ever let their daughters marry a Benjamite. They weep before God: “why is this come to pass in Israel, that there should be to day one tribe lacking in Israel?”

They find a way around their oath. The town of Jabeshgilead had not sent anyone to the gathering at Mizpeh. So the Israelites send twelve thousand soldiers to destroy the town, killing everyone except four hundred young virgins, whom they give to the Benjamites as wives. But two hundred men still have no wives. So the elders come up with another plan. There is a yearly festival to the LORD at Shiloh, where the young women come out to dance in the vineyards. They tell the Benjamites to hide in the vineyards, and each man is to grab a girl and carry her off as his wife. If the girls' fathers complain, the elders will tell them that, since the girls were taken rather than given, they have not broken their oath. The Benjamites do this, return to their land, and rebuild their towns.

The book ends with the same words that have echoed through its final chapters: “In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes.” Judges shows a nation falling apart without faithful leadership. It prepares the way for the story of Israel's kings.

# Ruth
When it happened: "In the days when the judges ruled" (Ruth 1:1)—the period between Joshua and the first king, Saul, often placed around the 1100s BC. Exact dates are uncertain.
When it was written: The book ends with David's genealogy (Ruth 4:17–22), so it was written down no earlier than David's time; scholars propose dates from the early monarchy to after the exile.
Where: Bethlehem in Judah, and the country of Moab across the Dead Sea to the east.
The world at the time: Israel had no king, and the tribes lived in the cycles of unfaithfulness and rescue described in Judges. Moab was a neighboring people that had at times oppressed Israel (Judges 3:12–30), which makes a Moabite woman's place in David's family line all the more striking.

**Naomi Loses Her Family in Moab (Ruth 1:1–22)** — A famine leads an Israelite family to Moab, where death leaves Naomi and her daughters-in-law widowed before Ruth chooses to remain with her.

“Now it came to pass in the days when the judges ruled, that there was a famine in the land.” A man named Elimelech leaves Bethlehem in Judah with his wife Naomi and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, and moves to the country of Moab. Bethlehem means house of bread, but there is no bread there now. In Moab, Elimelech dies. The two sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. About ten years later, both sons die too. Naomi is left alone in a foreign land with no husband and no sons.

Then Naomi hears that “the LORD had visited his people in giving them bread,” so she sets out for home with her two daughters-in-law. On the road, she stops and urges them to go back: “Go, return each to her mother's house: the LORD deal kindly with you, as ye have dealt with the dead, and with me.” She kisses them, and they all weep. Naomi tells them she has no more sons for them to marry, and she says, “it grieveth me much for your sakes that the hand of the LORD is gone out against me.” Orpah kisses her mother-in-law goodbye and goes home. “But Ruth clave unto her.”

Naomi urges her to follow her sister-in-law. But Ruth answers with some of the most loyal words ever spoken: “Intreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee: for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God: Where thou diest, will I die, and there will I be buried: the LORD do so to me, and more also, if ought but death part thee and me.” When Naomi sees that Ruth is determined, she stops arguing.

When the two women arrive in Bethlehem, the whole town is stirred. The women ask, “Is this Naomi?” Naomi's name means pleasant. She says, “Call me not Naomi, call me Mara: for the Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and the LORD hath brought me home again empty.” Mara means bitter. Yet she is not really empty, because Ruth is with her. And they arrive “in the beginning of barley harvest.”

**Ruth Finds Favor in the Field of Boaz (Ruth 2:1–23)** — Ruth gathers leftover grain to provide for Naomi and herself, where she meets Boaz, a wealthy relative who shows her kindness and protection.

Naomi has a relative on her husband's side named Boaz, “a mighty man of wealth.” Ruth says to Naomi, “Let me now go to the field, and glean ears of corn after him in whose sight I shall find grace.” God's law allowed the poor, widows, and foreigners to follow the harvesters and gather the leftover grain. “And her hap was to light on a part of the field belonging unto Boaz.” It looks like chance, but the story hints that God is guiding her.

Just then Boaz arrives from Bethlehem and greets his workers, “The LORD be with you. And they answered him, The LORD bless thee.” He notices Ruth and asks, “Whose damsel is this?” His foreman tells him she is the Moabite woman who came back with Naomi, and that she has worked hard all morning with only a short rest. Boaz goes to Ruth and tells her not to glean in any other field, but to stay close to his young women. He has warned the young men not to bother her, and she may drink from their water jars whenever she is thirsty.

Ruth bows to the ground and asks, “Why have I found grace in thine eyes, that thou shouldest take knowledge of me, seeing I am a stranger?” Boaz answers, “It hath fully been shewed me, all that thou hast done unto thy mother in law since the death of thine husband.” He knows she left her parents and her homeland. Then he blesses her: “The LORD recompense thy work, and a full reward be given thee of the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings thou art come to trust.” At mealtime, he invites her to eat with the workers and hands her roasted grain until she is full, with some left over. When she goes back to work, he secretly tells the young men to let her glean among the sheaves and even to “let fall also some of the handfuls of purpose for her.”

By evening, Ruth has gathered about an ephah of barley, more than half a bushel. She brings it home to Naomi, along with the leftover food from lunch. Naomi is amazed and asks where she worked. When Ruth says the man's name is Boaz, Naomi exclaims, “Blessed be he of the LORD, who hath not left off his kindness to the living and to the dead.” Then she explains, “The man is near of kin unto us, one of our next kinsmen.” He is a kinsman redeemer, a close relative who has the right to buy back a family's land and to care for a widow in the family. Ruth keeps gleaning in Boaz's fields through the whole barley and wheat harvest.

**Ruth Seeks Protection Through Boaz (Ruth 3:1–18)** — Naomi encourages Ruth to approach Boaz as a potential family redeemer, and he promises to address her request according to Israel's customs.

At the end of the harvest, Naomi decides it is time to act. She says, “My daughter, shall I not seek rest for thee, that it may be well with thee?” She means a home and a husband. Tonight, Boaz will be at the threshing floor, where the grain is separated from the straw. Naomi tells Ruth to wash, put on perfume and her best clothes, and go there without letting him see her. When Boaz has finished eating and drinking and lies down to sleep, Ruth is to “uncover his feet, and lay thee down,” and he will tell her what to do. Ruth answers, “All that thou sayest unto me I will do.”

Boaz eats, drinks, and is in good spirits, and he lies down beside a pile of grain. Ruth comes quietly, uncovers his feet, and lies down. At midnight Boaz wakes up with a start and finds a woman lying at his feet. “Who art thou?” She answers, “I am Ruth thine handmaid: spread therefore thy skirt over thine handmaid; for thou art a near kinsman.” Spreading the edge of his cloak over her was a way of asking him to marry her and protect her.

Boaz is deeply moved. “Blessed be thou of the LORD, my daughter: for thou hast shewed more kindness in the latter end than at the beginning, inasmuch as thou followedst not young men, whether poor or rich.” She could have looked for a younger husband, but she has chosen to care for Naomi's family. He promises to do what she asks, “for all the city of my people doth know that thou art a virtuous woman.” But there is one problem. Another relative is more closely related than Boaz and has the first right to redeem. If he will not do it, Boaz promises, “as the LORD liveth,” he will.

Ruth lies at his feet until morning and gets up before anyone can recognize her, so there will be no gossip. Boaz pours six measures of barley into her shawl and sends her home. When Naomi hears everything, she says, “Sit still, my daughter, until thou know how the matter will fall: for the man will not be in rest, until he have finished the thing this day.”

**Boaz Redeems Ruth and Naomi's Family (Ruth 4:1–22)** — Boaz fulfills the responsibilities of a family redeemer, marries Ruth, and their son becomes an ancestor of Israel's future king, David.

Boaz goes straight to the town gate, where legal business is done, and sits down. When the closer relative walks by, Boaz calls, “Ho, such a one! turn aside, sit down here.” He gathers ten elders of the city as witnesses. Then he explains that Naomi is selling the land that belonged to Elimelech, and the relative has the first right to buy it. The man says, “I will redeem it.”

But Boaz adds one more detail. Whoever buys the land must also marry Ruth the Moabite widow, so that her first son can carry on the family name of her dead husband and inherit the land. The relative quickly changes his mind: “I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I mar mine own inheritance.” He gives up his right. In those days, a man sealed a deal like this by taking off his sandal and handing it to the other person. So the relative takes off his sandal and gives it to Boaz.

Boaz announces to the elders and all the people, “Ye are witnesses this day,” that he has bought everything that belonged to Elimelech and his sons, and that he is taking Ruth as his wife. The people answer, “We are witnesses.” They bless him: “The LORD make the woman that is come into thine house like Rachel and like Leah, which two did build the house of Israel.”

So Boaz marries Ruth, “the LORD gave her conception, and she bare a son.” The women of Bethlehem tell Naomi, “Blessed be the LORD, which hath not left thee this day without a kinsman.” They say the child will renew her life and care for her in old age, for he is the son of “thy daughter in law, which loveth thee, which is better to thee than seven sons.” Naomi, who once said the LORD brought her home empty, now holds a grandson in her arms. She takes the child and becomes his nurse. The neighbor women say, “There is a son born to Naomi,” and they name him Obed.

The story ends with a surprise: Obed “is the father of Jesse, the father of David.” A foreign widow from Moab, who chose to trust Israel's God, became the great-grandmother of Israel's greatest king. Centuries later, Matthew names Ruth in the family line of Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5).

# 1 Samuel
When it happened: From the end of the judges to the death of King Saul, roughly 1100–1010 BC.
When it was written: 1 and 2 Samuel were originally one book. It continues past Samuel's death (1 Samuel 25:1), and 1 Chronicles 29:29 mentions records kept by Samuel, Nathan, and Gad. Many scholars think it was compiled from earlier records during the monarchy.
Where: Shiloh, Ramah, Mizpeh, Gibeah, Bethlehem, the valley of Elah, the wilderness of Judah around En-gedi, the Philistine cities of Gath and Ziklag, and Mount Gilboa.
The world at the time: The Philistines held the coastal plain and controlled iron-working, so that Israel had few swords (1 Samuel 13:19–22). Pressed by enemies, Israel asked for a king "like all the nations" (1 Samuel 8:5).

**Hannah Prays for a Son (1 Samuel 1:1–2:11)** — A woman named Hannah asks God for a child and promises to dedicate him to the Lord, beginning the life of Samuel.

Hannah, one of Elkanah's two wives, was childless, and the other wife, Peninnah, provoked her bitterly. At Shiloh Hannah prayed silently in deep distress and vowed to dedicate a son to the Lord. Eli the priest thought she was drunk, but when she explained, he blessed her. She bore a son and named him Samuel, “Because I have asked him of the LORD.” When he was weaned, Hannah brought him to Shiloh to serve under Eli. Her prayer praised God's holiness, power, and justice.

**God Rejects Eli's Unfaithful Household (1 Samuel 2:12–3:21)** — While Samuel grows in service to God, Eli's sons misuse their priestly positions, and God announces judgment against their family.

Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, took the best of the offerings by force and lay with the women who served at the door of the tabernacle, and Eli did not restrain them. A man of God warned Eli that both sons would die on the same day. God later called the boy Samuel by name three times; at Eli's direction Samuel answered, “Speak; for thy servant heareth.” Samuel received the message that judgment would come on Eli's family. As Samuel grew, all Israel recognized him as a prophet.

**Israel Loses the Ark of the Covenant (1 Samuel 4:1–7:2)** — Israel suffers defeat against the Philistines, and the captured ark brings judgment upon their cities before being returned to Israel.

Israel took the ark into battle but was defeated; Hophni and Phinehas died and the Philistines captured it. When Eli heard the ark was taken, he fell backward and died. Phinehas's widow named her newborn son Ichabod, saying, “The glory is departed from Israel.” Dagon fell before the ark and Philistine cities suffered plagues of tumors, so the Philistines returned it on a cart with golden offerings. Men of Bethshemesh were struck down for looking into the ark, and it came to Kirjathjearim, where it remained twenty years.

**Samuel Leads Israel Back to God (1 Samuel 7:3–17)** — Samuel calls Israel to abandon false gods, and God delivers the nation from the Philistines after the people turn toward Him.

Israel put away Baal and Ashtaroth at Samuel's call. At Mizpeh Samuel prayed and offered a sacrifice when Philistines approached; God thundered against them and Israel won. Samuel set up a stone and named it Ebenezer, saying, “Hitherto hath the LORD helped us,” and he continued judging Israel.

**Israel Demands a King (1 Samuel 8:1–22)** — Israel's elders demand a human king to lead them like neighboring nations, despite Samuel's warning about the burdens of monarchy.

Samuel's sons took bribes, so the elders asked for a king. God said they had rejected His rule, but told Samuel to listen after warning them that a king would take their children, property, and produce. Israel insisted on being like other nations.

**God Chooses Saul as Israel's First King (1 Samuel 9:1–10:27)** — God directs Saul to Samuel, who anoints him as king before Saul is publicly chosen to lead the nation.

Saul, a tall young Benjaminite, searched for his father's donkeys and came to Samuel. God had told Samuel to anoint a Benjaminite ruler. Samuel anointed Saul privately and gave confirming signs; God gave Saul another heart, and he prophesied, leading people to ask, “Is Saul also among the prophets?” At Mizpeh Saul was chosen publicly, though he hid among the baggage.

**Saul Wins His First Great Victory (1 Samuel 11:1–12:25)** — Saul delivers Jabeshgilead from an Ammonite threat, and Samuel reminds Israel that both king and people remain accountable to God.

When Nahash of Ammon threatened to put out the right eyes of the men of Jabeshgilead, the Spirit of God came upon Saul, and he gathered Israel and defeated Ammon. At Gilgal the kingdom was renewed. Samuel reviewed God's deliverances and warned that king and people must fear and obey God. God sent thunder and rain during harvest as a sign.

**Saul Disobeys God and Loses His Favor (1 Samuel 13:1–15:35)** — Saul repeatedly fails to follow God's instructions, and Samuel announces that the kingdom will not remain with Saul's family.

When Samuel was late in arriving at Gilgal, Saul offered the sacrifice himself. Samuel declared, “thy kingdom shall not continue: the LORD hath sought him a man after his own heart.” Jonathan's faith helped defeat Philistines, though Saul's rash oath endangered him. Saul later spared Agag and valuable Amalekite livestock against God's command. Samuel declared, “to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams,” and said that God had rejected Saul as king.

**God Chooses David to Replace Saul (1 Samuel 16:1–23)** — God sends Samuel to anoint David, the youngest son of Jesse, while Saul begins experiencing distress after God's Spirit departs from him.

God chose David, Jesse's youngest son, telling Samuel, “man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart.” Samuel anointed him, and the Spirit came upon David. The Spirit of the Lord departed from Saul, and an evil spirit from the Lord troubled him, so David was brought to play the harp and became Saul's armor-bearer.

**David Defeats Goliath (1 Samuel 17:1–58)** — A young shepherd named David confronts the Philistine champion Goliath, trusting God's power rather than weapons or physical strength.

Goliath challenged Israel for forty days. David, sent to bring food to his brothers, trusted God, declined Saul's armor, and went with a sling and five smooth stones. He told Goliath, “Thou comest to me with a sword, and with a spear, and with a shield: but I come to thee in the name of the LORD of hosts.” His stone struck Goliath in the forehead, and David cut off the giant's head with Goliath's own sword, and Israel pursued the fleeing Philistines.

**Saul Becomes Jealous of David (1 Samuel 18:1–20:42)** — David's growing success makes Saul increasingly hostile, while Saul's son Jonathan forms a loyal friendship with David and protects his life.

Jonathan made a covenant with David. When the women sang, “Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands,” Saul became jealous, threw his javelin at David, and kept trying to kill him. Saul offered his daughter Michal for a dowry of a hundred Philistine foreskins, hoping David would be killed, but David brought two hundred and married Michal. Michal helped David escape, and Jonathan confirmed Saul intended to kill him. The friends renewed their covenant and parted.

**David Flees from Saul's Pursuit (1 Samuel 21:1–23:29)** — David becomes a fugitive, finding temporary refuge and protection while Saul continues searching for opportunities to kill him.

David received consecrated bread and Goliath's sword at Nob, then fled to Achish king of Gath, where he pretended to be mad to escape. He gathered followers at the cave of Adullam. Saul had Doeg kill the priests of Nob, but Abiathar escaped to David. David rescued Keilah, fled Saul, and Jonathan encouraged him. A Philistine attack stopped Saul's near capture of David.

**David Spares Saul's Life (1 Samuel 24:1–22)** — David has an opportunity to kill Saul but refuses to harm the king whom God had appointed over Israel.

In a cave near Engedi David cut Saul's robe rather than kill him. He showed Saul the cloth and entrusted judgment to God. Saul wept, admitted David's righteousness, and said, “I know well that thou shalt surely be king.” He asked David not to destroy his descendants, and David promised.

**Abigail Prevents David from Taking Revenge (1 Samuel 25:1–44)** — After Samuel's death, Abigail wisely intervenes when her husband insults David, preventing David from carrying out a violent act of revenge.

Samuel died, and all Israel mourned him. Later Nabal, a harsh and foolish man, refused David's request for provisions despite David's protection of his shepherds. Abigail brought supplies and persuaded David not to take revenge. Nabal later died after the Lord struck him, and Abigail became David's wife.

**David Spares Saul Again and Leaves Israel (1 Samuel 26:1–27:12)** — David again refuses to kill Saul, but continued danger leads him to seek refuge among the Philistines.

David entered Saul's camp, took his spear and water container, and refused to kill the Lord's anointed. Saul again admitted wrong. David then sought refuge with Achish at Gath, who gave him Ziklag. From there David raided Israel's enemies while leading Achish to believe he was attacking Judah.

**Saul Seeks Guidance Before His Final Battle (1 Samuel 28:1–25)** — As the Philistines prepare for war, Saul receives no answer from God and turns to a forbidden medium who announces his approaching death.

Saul had driven mediums from the land, but when he received no answer from God, he disguised himself and sought a woman with a familiar spirit at Endor. Samuel's message declared that the kingdom would go to David and that Saul, his sons, and Israel would fall to the Philistines.

**David Recovers His Captured Families (1 Samuel 29:1–30:31)** — Philistine commanders reject David's participation in their campaign, and he returns to discover that Amalekites have attacked Ziklag.

Philistine commanders rejected David. Amalekites burned Ziklag and took captives, including David's wives. David's grieving men spoke of stoning him, “but David encouraged himself in the LORD his God.” David sought God, pursued them, and recovered everyone and everything. He established that those guarding supplies shared equally and sent gifts to elders in Judah.

**Saul and Jonathan Die in Battle (1 Samuel 31:1–13)** — The Philistines defeat Israel at Mount Gilboa, killing Jonathan and Saul's other sons before Saul takes his own life.

Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua died at Mount Gilboa. Saul, badly wounded, asked his armor-bearer to kill him, and when he refused, Saul fell on his own sword. Philistines displayed the bodies, but men of Jabeshgilead recovered them and fasted. Saul's reign ended in defeat, while David remained alive but had not yet taken the throne.

# 2 Samuel
When it happened: The forty-year reign of King David, roughly 1010–970 BC—seven and a half years in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:4–5).
When it was written: It continues 1 Samuel, with which it was originally one book. It likely draws on records kept by prophets of David's court such as Nathan and Gad.
Where: Hebron, Jerusalem (the stronghold of Zion), Mahanaim east of the Jordan, and the surrounding lands of the Philistines, Moab, Ammon, Edom, and Syria.
The world at the time: Egypt and Assyria were relatively weak in this era, which allowed a smaller kingdom to grow. David subdued Israel's neighbors and made Jerusalem the political and religious capital.

**David Mourns the Death of Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel 1:1–27)** — David learns that Saul and Jonathan have died in battle and responds with grief rather than celebration.

At Ziklag, David received news of Saul and Jonathan's deaths. He executed the Amalekite who claimed to have killed Saul, a claim that differs from the account of Saul's death in 1 Samuel 31. David mourned with his men and composed a lament honoring Saul and Jonathan: “how are the mighty fallen!”

**David Becomes King Over Judah (2 Samuel 2:1–3:39)** — David is anointed king in Hebron, but conflict develops between his supporters and those loyal to Saul's surviving son.

Judah anointed David king at Hebron, while Abner made Ishbosheth king over much of Israel. A long war followed, during which Abner killed Joab's brother Asahel. Abner later offered to bring Israel to David, but Joab killed him in revenge for Asahel. David publicly mourned and condemned the killing.

**David Becomes King Over All Israel (2 Samuel 4:1–5:25)** — Following Ishbosheth's death, Israel's tribes unite under David, who captures Jerusalem and defeats Philistine attacks.

Two of Ishbosheth's captains murdered him, and David executed them. Israel's elders made David king over all Israel. He was thirty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned forty years. He captured Jerusalem, made Zion his city, and defeated Philistines after seeking God's guidance.

**David Brings the Ark to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6:1–23)** — David brings the ark of the covenant into Jerusalem, and Uzzah's death shows that God's holiness must be respected.

David moved the ark on a new cart, and when the oxen stumbled, Uzzah took hold of it and God struck him. The ark stayed three months in the house of Obededom, and the Lord blessed his household. Then David brought it to Jerusalem with sacrifices and celebration, dancing before the Lord with all his might. Michal, Saul's daughter, despised him for it, and she had no child to the day of her death.

**God Establishes a Covenant with David (2 Samuel 7:1–29)** — God promises to establish David's royal household and kingdom, declaring that David's son will succeed him and build a house for God's name.

David wished to build a house for the ark, but God told Nathan that David would not build Him a house; instead God would establish David's house, an enduring royal line. David's son would build for God's name, and God promised, “thine house and thy kingdom shall be established for ever before thee.” David responded with humility and thanksgiving.

**David's Kingdom Grows in Strength (2 Samuel 8:1–10:19)** — David wins victories, establishes justice, shows kindness to Jonathan's son, and defeats an Ammonite alliance.

David defeated neighboring nations and dedicated captured wealth to the Lord. He restored Saul's land to Mephibosheth and gave him a place at the king's table. Israel defeated Ammonite and Syrian forces after Hanun humiliated David's messengers.

**David Sins Against Bathsheba and Uriah (2 Samuel 11:1–27)** — David commits adultery with Bathsheba and arranges her husband's death.

At the time when kings went out to battle, David stayed in Jerusalem. From his roof he saw Bathsheba, Uriah's wife, bathing; he slept with her, and she became pregnant. David brought Uriah home from the war, but Uriah refused to go to his house while the army was in the field. David then sent Uriah back carrying a letter to Joab: “Set ye Uriah in the forefront of the hottest battle.” David married Bathsheba, but what he had done displeased the Lord.

**Nathan Confronts David's Sin (2 Samuel 12:1–31)** — Nathan exposes David's wrongdoing, and although David confesses, painful consequences follow.

Nathan told a parable of a rich man who took a poor man's only lamb, and when David grew angry, Nathan said, “Thou art the man.” He declared that “the sword shall never depart from thine house.” David confessed, “I have sinned against the LORD,” and Nathan answered that the Lord had put away his sin and he would not die, but the child would die. The child died; later Bathsheba bore Solomon, also called Jedidiah. Joab and David then captured the Ammonite city of Rabbah.

**Violence Tears David's Family Apart (2 Samuel 13:1–39)** — Amnon assaults Tamar, leading Absalom to arrange Amnon's death and flee.

Amnon forced Tamar and then rejected her. Absalom sheltered Tamar. David was very angry but did not punish Amnon, and two years later Absalom had Amnon killed at a sheep-shearing feast. Absalom fled to Geshur for three years, while David mourned and longed for him.

**Absalom Returns and Turns Against David (2 Samuel 14:1–15:37)** — Absalom returns but wins public support and declares himself king.

Joab sent a wise woman of Tekoah to persuade David to allow Absalom's return, and two years later David received him. Absalom then gained support at the city gate and proclaimed himself king at Hebron. David fled Jerusalem, sent the ark back, and left Hushai to counter Absalom's plans.

**David Faces Betrayal During His Escape (2 Samuel 16:1–17:29)** — As David flees, he encounters loyalty and betrayal while Absalom receives competing advice.

Ziba accused Mephibosheth, and Shimei cursed David, but David would not let Abishai kill him. On Ahithophel's advice, Absalom went in to his father's concubines in the sight of all Israel, as Nathan had warned. Absalom then accepted Hushai's slower plan rather than Ahithophel's immediate attack, because the Lord had appointed to defeat Ahithophel's good counsel. Ahithophel went home and hanged himself. The delay gave David time to escape across the Jordan to Mahanaim.

**Absalom Dies and David Mourns (2 Samuel 18:1–19:8)** — David's forces defeat Absalom's rebellion, but Joab kills Absalom against the king's wishes.

David ordered his commanders to deal gently with Absalom. Absalom's head became caught in an oak as his mule ran beneath it, and Joab killed him. David wept, “O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee,” until Joab warned that his grief dishonored the soldiers who had saved him.

**David Returns to Jerusalem Amid Division (2 Samuel 19:9–20:26)** — David regains his throne, forgives opponents, and faces another rebellion.

David returned, forgave Shimei, and named Amasa, Absalom's commander, captain of the army in place of Joab. Mephibosheth said that Ziba had deceived him, and David divided Saul's land between them. Disagreement among the tribes led Sheba to rebel. Joab killed Amasa and pursued Sheba until a wise woman negotiated his death.

**David Addresses an Unresolved Wrong Against Gibeon (2 Samuel 21:1–22)** — A famine leads David to address Saul's violence against the Gibeonites.

God revealed that famine followed Saul's bloodguilt against Gibeon. David handed over seven of Saul's descendants, while sparing Jonathan's son Mephibosheth, and the Gibeonites hanged them. Rizpah, the mother of two of them, guarded their bodies from birds and beasts until the rain came. After David buried the bones of Saul, Jonathan, and the seven, God answered prayer for the land. Israel also fought Philistine giants.

**David Praises God for Deliverance (2 Samuel 22:1–23:7)** — David celebrates God's protection and reflects on righteous leadership and God's covenant.

David sang, “The LORD is my rock, and my fortress, and my deliverer,” and praised God as his shield. His final words declared, “He that ruleth over men must be just, ruling in the fear of God,” and honored and the everlasting covenant God had made with his house.

**David's Mighty Warriors Are Remembered (2 Samuel 23:8–39)** — The account honors courageous men who served David in Israel's military struggles.

The record names David's mighty men, including Adino the Eznite, Eleazar, Shammah, Abishai, and Benaiah. Three warriors brought David water from Bethlehem, which he poured out before the Lord. The list includes Uriah the Hittite.

**David's Census Brings Judgment Upon Israel (2 Samuel 24:1–25)** — David orders a census that brings judgment, but God's mercy stops the destruction after David builds an altar.

David ordered a census despite Joab's warning, and afterward his heart smote him and he confessed his sin. Offered a choice of three judgments through the prophet Gad, David said, “let us fall now into the hand of the LORD; for his mercies are great.” A pestilence killed seventy thousand until God stopped the angel near Jerusalem. Araunah offered his threshing floor freely, but David insisted on paying, saying he would not offer to the Lord “of that which doth cost me nothing.” He bought it, built an altar, and offered sacrifices, and the plague ended.
