# Matthew
When it happened: From Jesus' birth "in the days of Herod the king" (Matthew 2:1)—before Herod's death in 4 BC—through his ministry, death, and resurrection, around AD 27–30.
When it was written: Most scholars date it between the AD 50s and 80s. It was written for readers steeped in the Jewish Scriptures, often noting that events happened "that it might be fulfilled" (Matthew 1:22).
Where: Bethlehem, Egypt, Nazareth, Galilee (especially Capernaum and the Sea of Galilee), Judea, and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Judea was under Roman rule, governed by Herod the Great and later his sons and Roman governors such as Pontius Pilate. Many Jews longed for the promised Messiah, and the Pharisees and Sadducees were leading religious groups.

**The Promised King Is Born (Matthew 1:1–2:23)** — Matthew traces Jesus' family line to Abraham and David, then describes his birth and the events that bring his family to Nazareth.

Matthew opens with a family tree: “The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” It moves through three sets of fourteen generations: from Abraham to King David, from David to the exile in Babylon, and from the exile to Jesus. Surprisingly, it names four women with complicated stories: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Uriah's wife Bathsheba. Even Jesus' family line shows God's grace.

Mary is engaged to Joseph when “she was found with child of the Holy Ghost.” Joseph is a just man and plans to end the engagement quietly. But an angel tells him in a dream, “thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins.” Matthew explains that this fulfills Isaiah's words: “they shall call his name Emmanuel, which being interpreted is, God with us.”

Jesus is born in Bethlehem. Wise men from the east arrive in Jerusalem asking, “Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him.” King Herod is alarmed. The star leads the wise men to the house, and they bring “gold, and frankincense, and myrrh.” God warns them in a dream not to report back to Herod. Herod is furious and orders every boy two years old and under in Bethlehem killed. But an angel has already warned Joseph, and he flees to Egypt with Mary and the child. Matthew sees in this the words “Out of Egypt have I called my son.” After Herod dies, the family settles in Nazareth.

**Jesus Begins His Public Ministry (Matthew 3:1–4:25)** — John prepares the people for repentance, Jesus is baptized and tested, and his ministry begins with preaching, healing, and the calling of disciples.

Years later, John the Baptist appears in the wilderness of Judea. He preaches, “Repent ye: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.” He is “The voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord.” John wears camel's hair and eats “locusts and wild honey.” Crowds come to be baptized in the Jordan River. When Pharisees and Sadducees show up, John calls them “O generation of vipers,” and warns them that being Abraham's descendants will not save them. He says that one mightier than he is coming, who will baptize “with the Holy Ghost, and with fire.”

Jesus comes to be baptized. John protests that Jesus should baptize him instead, but Jesus insists. As he comes up from the water, the Spirit of God descends “like a dove,” and a voice from heaven says, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”

Then the Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness, where he fasts forty days and is tempted by the devil. First the devil tells him to turn stones into bread. Jesus answers, “It is written, Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God.” Next the devil takes him to the top of the temple and dares him to jump, quoting a psalm. Jesus replies, “Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God.” Last, the devil offers him all the kingdoms of the world if he will bow down. Jesus says, “Get thee hence, Satan: for it is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve.” Each answer comes from Deuteronomy.

After John is arrested, Jesus moves to Capernaum by the Sea of Galilee. By the shore he calls two pairs of fishermen brothers, Peter and Andrew and James and John: “Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men.” They leave their nets at once. Jesus travels through Galilee, teaching, preaching, and healing every kind of sickness, and great crowds follow him.

**Jesus Teaches the Way of God's Kingdom (Matthew 5:1–7:29)** — Jesus teaches his followers about righteousness, prayer, mercy, trust in God, and the obedience expected of those who enter his kingdom.

Seeing the crowds, Jesus goes up a mountain, sits down, and teaches. This is the Sermon on the Mount. It begins with the Beatitudes, or blessings: “Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven... Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth... Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God. Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God.” he tells his followers, “Ye are the salt of the earth,” and “Ye are the light of the world. A city that is set on an hill cannot be hid... Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”

Jesus says, “Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil.” Then, again and again, he says, “Ye have heard that it was said,” and takes the law deeper. Murder starts with anger. Adultery starts with a lustful look. Instead of “An eye for an eye,” he says, “whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.” And instead of hating enemies: “Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you.”

Jesus warns against doing good deeds to be seen by others. he teaches his disciples to pray: “Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread.” he tells them, “Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth... For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.” “Ye cannot serve God and mammon,” meaning money. Instead of worrying, they should look at the birds and “Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin.” “But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.”

he goes on: “Judge not, that ye be not judged.” “Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you.” he gives the Golden Rule: “all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them.” The way to life is narrow: “strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it.” he warns of false prophets “in sheep's clothing,” for “by their fruits ye shall know them.” Finally, he says that whoever hears and obeys his words is like a wise man who built his house upon a rock. When “the rain descended, and the floods came,” it stood. The foolish man built on sand, and his house fell. The crowds are amazed, “For he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.”

**Jesus Demonstrates His Authority and Sends His Disciples (Matthew 8:1–10:42)** — Jesus heals the sick, commands nature and evil spirits, forgives sins, and sends twelve apostles to proclaim the kingdom.

Coming down from the mountain, Jesus shows his power. A leper kneels and says, “Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.” Jesus touches him, and he is healed. A Roman centurion asks Jesus to heal his servant from a distance: “speak the word only, and my servant shall be healed.” Jesus marvels, “I have not found so great faith, no, not in Israel.” he heals Peter's mother-in-law and many others. When a man offers to follow him anywhere, Jesus warns, “Foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head.”

On the lake, a violent storm hits while Jesus sleeps. his disciples wake him in terror. he asks, “Why are ye fearful, O ye of little faith?” and calms the wind and sea. The men ask, “What manner of man is this, that even the winds and the sea obey him!” On the other side, he frees two men from demons, sending the demons into a herd of pigs that rushes into the sea.

Friends bring Jesus a paralyzed man, and Jesus says, “Son, be of good cheer; thy sins be forgiven thee.” When scribes object, he heals the man to prove he has power to forgive sins. Jesus calls Matthew, a tax collector, and then eats with tax collectors and sinners. To the critics he says, “They that be whole need not a physician, but they that are sick,” and quotes Hosea: “I will have mercy, and not sacrifice.” A woman who has bled for twelve years touches the hem of his garment and is healed: “Daughter, be of good comfort; thy faith hath made thee whole.” Then he raises a ruler's daughter, saying, “the maid is not dead, but sleepeth.”

Jesus sees the crowds as sheep without a shepherd. “The harvest truly is plenteous, but the labourers are few.” he chooses twelve apostles and sends them out to the lost sheep of Israel to preach, heal, and cast out demons. he warns that they will face hatred and arrest: “be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves.” But they should not fear, because God sees even a sparrow fall, and “the very hairs of your head are all numbered.”

**The Kingdom Meets Doubt and Opposition (Matthew 11:1–12:50)** — Questions about Jesus increase as he responds to John, confronts unbelief, and challenges religious leaders who reject his works.

John the Baptist, now in prison, sends messengers to ask, “Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?” Jesus tells them to report what they see: “The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them.” he praises John as more than a prophet. But he warns towns like Capernaum that saw his miracles and still did not repent. Then he gives a warm invitation: “Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart... For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.”

The Pharisees begin to oppose him openly. They complain when his hungry disciples pick grain on the Sabbath. Jesus answers that “the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath day.” Then he heals a man with a withered hand in the synagogue on the Sabbath. The Pharisees go out and plot how to kill him.

When Jesus heals a blind and mute man possessed by a demon, the Pharisees claim he does it by the power of Beelzebub, the prince of demons. Jesus answers that “Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation,” so Satan would not fight himself. he warns that speaking against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, and that people will give an account for every careless word. When they demand a sign, he says the only sign will be Jonah's: “For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.” When his mother and brothers come looking for him, he points to his disciples: “For whosoever shall do the will of my Father which is in heaven, the same is my brother, and sister, and mother.”

**Jesus Reveals the Mysteries of the Kingdom (Matthew 13:1–14:36)** — Through parables and miracles, Jesus teaches how God's kingdom grows while showing compassion and power despite rejection.

Sitting in a boat by the shore, Jesus teaches the crowd in parables, simple stories with deeper meanings. A sower scatters seed. Some falls by the path and birds eat it. Some falls on rocky ground and dies in the sun. Some is choked by thorns. But some falls on good ground and produces “some an hundredfold, some sixtyfold, some thirtyfold.” “Who hath ears to hear, let him hear.” Jesus explains that the seed is God's word, and the soils are the different ways people respond to it. In another parable, an enemy sows weeds among wheat, and both grow until the harvest, when they are separated. The kingdom is like a mustard seed, “the least of all seeds,” that grows into a tree. It is like yeast working through dough, like “treasure hid in a field” that a man sells everything to buy, and like a merchant who finds “one pearl of great price.”

When Jesus teaches in his hometown of Nazareth, people take offense: “Is not this the carpenter's son?” he says, “A prophet is not without honour, save in his own country, and in his own house.”

Meanwhile, Herod Antipas had put John the Baptist in prison for condemning his marriage to his brother's wife, Herodias. At Herod's birthday party, Herodias's daughter dances, and Herod promises her anything she wants. Prompted by her mother, she asks for “John Baptist's head in a charger,” a serving platter. Herod has John beheaded.

When Jesus hears, he goes away to a quiet place, but crowds follow. Moved with compassion, he heals their sick. In the evening he takes five loaves and two fish, gives thanks, and feeds “about five thousand men, beside women and children,” with twelve baskets left over. That night, the disciples are rowing against a storm when they see Jesus walking on the water. They think he is a ghost, but he says, “Be of good cheer; it is I; be not afraid.” Peter asks to come to him and walks on the water, until he sees the wind, panics, and begins to sink. “Lord, save me.” Jesus catches him: “O thou of little faith, wherefore didst thou doubt?” The disciples worship him: “Of a truth thou art the Son of God.”

**Jesus Teaches True Faith and Reveals His Coming Suffering (Matthew 15:1–17:27)** — Jesus exposes false religious traditions, welcomes expressions of faith, and begins preparing his disciples for his death and resurrection.

Pharisees from Jerusalem complain that Jesus' disciples do not wash their hands according to tradition. Jesus says they have put human traditions above God's commands. he quotes Isaiah: “This people draweth nigh unto me with their mouth... but their heart is far from me.” What makes a person unclean is not food, “but that which cometh out of the mouth,” because evil thoughts, murder, and lies come from the heart. he calls the Pharisees blind guides: “if the blind lead the blind, both shall fall into the ditch.”

In Gentile territory, a Canaanite woman begs him to heal her demon-possessed daughter. Jesus tests her, saying he was sent to Israel and it is not right to give the children's bread to the dogs. She answers, “Truth, Lord: yet the dogs eat of the crumbs which fall from their masters' table.” Jesus says, “O woman, great is thy faith,” and her daughter is healed. he then feeds four thousand men, plus women and children, with seven loaves and a few fish.

Near Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks his disciples, “But whom say ye that I am?” Peter answers, “Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God.” Jesus tells him that God revealed this to him, “And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it.” From then on, Jesus begins to tell them plainly that he must suffer in Jerusalem, be killed, and rise on the third day. Peter pulls him aside and argues, but Jesus says, “Get thee behind me, Satan.” he tells them, “If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me... For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”

Six days later, Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain. There he is transfigured: “his face did shine as the sun, and his raiment was white as the light.” Moses and Elijah appear and talk with him. A bright cloud covers them, and a voice says, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him.” Coming down, Jesus heals a boy the other disciples could not help. he tells them that with faith “as a grain of mustard seed,” they could move mountains. he predicts his death again. When collectors ask whether he pays the temple tax, Jesus sends Peter fishing. In the first fish's mouth, Peter finds a coin to pay for both of them.

**Jesus Teaches Humility, Forgiveness, and Service (Matthew 18:1–20:34)** — Jesus teaches his followers to care for the vulnerable, forgive others, honor marriage, and seek greatness through service rather than status.

The disciples ask who is greatest in the kingdom of heaven. Jesus calls a little child over and says, “Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven.” he warns that anyone who leads one of these little ones into sin would be better off drowned with a millstone around his neck. Like a shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to find one that is lost, God does not want any of them to perish. Jesus explains how to settle a wrong between believers, and he promises, “For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them.”

Peter asks, “Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?” Jesus answers, “I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven.” Then he tells of a servant whose king forgave him a debt of ten thousand talents, an amount he could never repay. Yet that servant went out and choked a fellow servant who owed him a hundred pence and had him thrown in prison. The king reversed his forgiveness. So will God treat those who will not forgive from the heart.

Pharisees test Jesus about divorce. he points back to creation, when God made them male and female: “What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder.” When the disciples try to send children away, Jesus says, “Suffer little children, and forbid them not, to come unto me: for of such is the kingdom of heaven.” A rich young man asks what he must do to have eternal life. Jesus tells him to “go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor,” and follow him. The young man “went away sorrowful: for he had great possessions.” Jesus says, “It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God.” The shocked disciples ask who can be saved. “With men this is impossible; but with God all things are possible.”

In a parable, a vineyard owner hires workers throughout the day, then pays the last ones the same as the first. When the early workers complain, he says he is free to be generous. “So the last shall be first, and the first last.” On the way to Jerusalem, Jesus predicts his death a third time. Then the mother of James and John asks for her sons to sit at his right and left. Jesus teaches that true greatness is serving others: “Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.” Leaving Jericho, he heals two blind men who cry out, “Have mercy on us, O Lord, thou son of David.”

**The King Enters Jerusalem and Confronts Its Leaders (Matthew 21:1–23:39)** — Jesus enters Jerusalem, challenges corrupt worship, and warns religious leaders whose outward devotion hides injustice and hypocrisy.

Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a donkey, fulfilling Zechariah's prophecy of a humble king. Crowds spread their cloaks and palm branches on the road and shout, “Hosanna to the Son of David: Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord; Hosanna in the highest.” he enters the temple and throws out the money changers and those selling animals, saying, “It is written, My house shall be called the house of prayer; but ye have made it a den of thieves.” he heals the blind and lame, and children cry out “Hosanna” in the temple. The next morning, he curses a fig tree with leaves but no fruit, and it withers. It pictures a nation that looks religious but bears no fruit.

The chief priests demand, “By what authority doest thou these things?” Jesus answers with a question about John's baptism that they refuse to answer. Then he tells parables aimed at them. One is about two sons, one who says yes but does not obey. Another is about tenant farmers who beat the owner's servants and kill his son. Jesus quotes the psalm, “The stone which the builders rejected, the same is become the head of the corner.” A third is about a king whose invited guests refuse to come to his son's wedding feast, so he invites everyone off the streets. “For many are called, but few are chosen.”

His opponents try to trap him with questions. Should Jews pay taxes to Caesar? Jesus looks at a coin with Caesar's picture and says, “Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's.” The Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, ask a trick question about a woman married to seven brothers. Jesus says that in the resurrection people “neither marry, nor are given in marriage.” A lawyer asks which commandment is greatest. Jesus answers, “Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.” After that, no one dares ask him anything more.

In chapter 23, Jesus warns the crowds against the scribes and Pharisees, who “say, and do not.” Seven times he says, “Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!” They tithe tiny spices but neglect justice, mercy, and faith. They “strain at a gnat, and swallow a camel.” They are like “whited sepulchres,” white-painted tombs that look beautiful outside but are full of dead men's bones. Then his anger turns to grief: “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killest the prophets... how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her chickens under her wings, and ye would not!”

**Jesus Warns of Future Trouble and Calls for Readiness (Matthew 24:1–25:46)** — Jesus foretells coming distress and judgment, urging his followers to remain watchful, faithful, and prepared for his return.

As they leave the temple, the disciples admire its huge stones. Jesus says, “There shall not be left here one stone upon another, that shall not be thrown down.” This happened when the Romans destroyed the temple in AD 70. On the Mount of Olives, the disciples ask when it will happen and what will signal his return and the end of the age. Jesus warns, “Take heed that no man deceive you.” There will be false messiahs, “wars and rumours of wars,” famines, earthquakes, and persecution. “But he that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved. And this gospel of the kingdom shall be preached in all the world.” he warns of “the abomination of desolation, spoken of by Daniel the prophet,” and a time of great trouble. Then the Son of man will come “in the clouds of heaven with power and great glory.” “Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away. But of that day and hour knoweth no man, no, not the angels of heaven, but my Father only.” It will come suddenly, as the flood came in Noah's day.

So his followers must be ready. Jesus tells of ten bridesmaids waiting for a bridegroom. Five wise ones bring extra oil for their lamps, and five foolish ones do not. When the bridegroom arrives at midnight, the foolish ones are out buying oil, and the door is shut. “Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh.” In another parable, a master gives his servants talents, large sums of money, before a long trip. Two invest theirs and double it, and he tells each, “Well done, thou good and faithful servant.” The third buries his talent out of fear and is thrown out.

Finally, Jesus describes the last judgment. The Son of man will sit on his throne and separate the nations “as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats.” To those on his right he says, “I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in.” They ask when they ever did this, and he answers, “Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.” Those who refused to help the needy are sent away into everlasting punishment, “but the righteous into life eternal.”

**Jesus Is Betrayed, Arrested, and Condemned (Matthew 26:1–27:26)** — As religious leaders plot his death, Jesus shares a final meal with his disciples, is betrayed, and stands before Jewish and Roman authorities.

Two days before Passover, the chief priests and elders meet at the palace of the high priest Caiaphas and plot to arrest Jesus quietly. In the village of Bethany, a woman pours very expensive perfume on Jesus' head. The disciples call it a waste, but Jesus says she has prepared his body for burial: “Wheresoever this gospel shall be preached in the whole world, there shall also this, that this woman hath done, be told for a memorial of her.” Then Judas Iscariot goes to the chief priests and agrees to betray Jesus for thirty pieces of silver, the price Zechariah had foretold.

At the Passover meal, Jesus says, “Verily I say unto you, that one of you shall betray me.” Each disciple asks, “Lord, is it I?” Then Jesus takes bread, blesses it, and says, “Take, eat; this is my body.” he takes the cup: “Drink ye all of it; For this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins.” On the way to the Mount of Olives, Peter insists he will never fall away. Jesus tells him, “before the cock crow, thou shalt deny me thrice.”

In the garden of Gethsemane, Jesus says, “My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death.” he falls on his face and prays, “O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt.” Three times he returns to find his disciples asleep: “the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak.” Then Judas arrives with an armed crowd and identifies Jesus with a kiss. One of the disciples cuts off a servant's ear, but Jesus says, “all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword.” All the disciples run away.

That night, the council looks for false witnesses but cannot find two who agree. Finally Caiaphas demands, “I adjure thee by the living God, that thou tell us whether thou be the Christ, the Son of God.” Jesus answers, “Thou hast said: nevertheless I say unto you, Hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven.” The high priest tears his clothes and cries blasphemy, and the council declares that Jesus deserves death. They spit in his face and beat him. In the courtyard, Peter denies three times that he knows Jesus, the last time with curses. Then the rooster crows, and Peter “went out, and wept bitterly.”

In the morning they bind Jesus and hand him over to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor. When Judas sees that Jesus is condemned, he cries, “I have sinned in that I have betrayed the innocent blood,” throws the silver into the temple, and hangs himself. The priests use the money to buy a potter's field for burying strangers. Pilate asks Jesus, “Art thou the King of the Jews?” and Jesus says, “Thou sayest,” but he does not answer the other charges. Pilate's wife sends a warning: “Have thou nothing to do with that just man.” Pilate offers to release either Jesus or a notorious prisoner named Barabbas. The crowd chooses Barabbas. “What shall I do then with Jesus which is called Christ?” They shout, “Let him be crucified.” Pilate washes his hands before the crowd and says, “I am innocent of the blood of this just person: see ye to it.” He has Jesus scourged, or whipped, and hands him over to be crucified.

**Jesus Dies and Rises from the Dead (Matthew 27:27–28:15)** — Jesus is crucified and buried, but on the third day his tomb is found empty, and his resurrection is announced.

The Roman soldiers put a scarlet robe on Jesus, press a crown of thorns onto his head, and put a reed in his hand as a fake scepter. They kneel and mock him, “Hail, King of the Jews!” Then they spit on him and beat him on the head. On the way out, they force a man named Simon of Cyrene to carry his cross. At Golgotha, “a place of a skull,” they crucify him between two thieves and gamble for his clothes. Above his head they post the charge: “THIS IS JESUS THE KING OF THE JEWS.” People passing by shake their heads. The chief priests mock, “He saved others; himself he cannot save.”

From noon until three in the afternoon, darkness covers the land. Then Jesus cries out with a loud voice, “Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani? that is to say, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” These are the first words of Psalm 22. he cries out again and “yielded up the ghost.” At that moment “the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom.” This was the thick curtain that blocked the way into the most holy place. The earth quakes, rocks split, and tombs open. The Roman centurion and his soldiers are terrified and say, “Truly this was the Son of God.” Many women who had followed Jesus from Galilee watch from a distance.

That evening a rich disciple named Joseph of Arimathaea asks Pilate for the body. He wraps it in clean linen, lays it in his own new tomb cut in the rock, and rolls a great stone over the door. The chief priests remember that Jesus said he would rise after three days, so Pilate lets them seal the stone and post a guard.

At dawn on the first day of the week, Mary Magdalene and the other Mary come to see the tomb. There is a great earthquake. An angel rolls back the stone and sits on it: “His countenance was like lightning, and his raiment white as snow.” The guards shake with fear “and became as dead men.” The angel tells the women, “Fear not ye: for I know that ye seek Jesus, which was crucified. He is not here: for he is risen, as he said. Come, see the place where the Lord lay.” As they run to tell the disciples, Jesus himself meets them, and they take hold of his feet and worship him. Meanwhile, some of the guards report to the chief priests. The priests pay them a large sum to say, “His disciples came by night, and stole him away while we slept.” Matthew notes that this story was still being told in his day.

**Jesus Sends His Disciples to All Nations (Matthew 28:16–20)** — The risen Jesus meets his disciples in Galilee and commands them to make disciples throughout the world, promising his continuing presence.

The eleven disciples go to the mountain in Galilee where Jesus told them to meet him. “And when they saw him, they worshipped him: but some doubted.” Jesus comes to them and gives what is called the Great Commission: “All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world.”

---

Matthew begins by tracing Jesus to Abraham and David, placing him within God's promises to Israel. It closes with the risen Jesus claiming authority over heaven and earth and extending his mission to every nation. The One introduced as Emmanuel, meaning God with us, sends his followers into the world with the assurance that his presence will continue with them.

# Mark
When it happened: Jesus' public ministry, from his baptism by John to his resurrection, around AD 27–30.
When it was written: Early church tradition says Mark recorded the preaching of Peter. Many scholars consider it the earliest Gospel, written in the AD 50s or 60s.
Where: Galilee, especially Capernaum and the Sea of Galilee; the surrounding regions of the Decapolis, Tyre and Sidon, and Caesarea Philippi; Judea; and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas under Rome, and Judea by a Roman governor. Mark explains Jewish customs for his readers (Mark 7:3–4), suggesting many of them were not Jewish.

**Jesus Begins His Ministry in Galilee (Mark 1:1–45)** — John the Baptist prepares the way for Jesus, who begins proclaiming God's kingdom and demonstrates His authority through teaching and healing.

Mark starts fast, with no birth story: “The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.” He quotes the prophets about a messenger who would prepare the way, “The voice of one crying in the wilderness.” That messenger is John the Baptist, who preaches in the wilderness and baptizes crowds in the Jordan River. John says, “There cometh one mightier than I after me.”

Jesus comes from Nazareth and is baptized. As He comes up out of the water, He sees the heavens open and the Spirit descending like a dove, and a voice says, “Thou art my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.” Right away the Spirit drives Him into the wilderness for forty days, where He is tempted by Satan and is “with the wild beasts.” Mark loves the word “straightway,” meaning immediately, and the story moves quickly.

After John is arrested, Jesus comes into Galilee preaching, “The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe the gospel.” By the Sea of Galilee He calls Simon Peter and Andrew, and then James and John: “Come ye after me, and I will make you to become fishers of men.” In the synagogue at Capernaum, people are amazed, “for he taught them as one that had authority, and not as the scribes.” A man with an unclean spirit cries out, “I know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God,” and Jesus commands the spirit to leave. He heals Simon's mother-in-law of a fever, and that evening the whole town crowds around the door with their sick.

Early the next morning, “rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed.” Then He moves on to other towns. A leper begs, “If thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.” Moved with compassion, Jesus touches him and says, “I will; be thou clean.” Jesus tells him to keep quiet, but the man spreads the news everywhere, and the crowds grow so large that Jesus can no longer openly enter a town.

**Jesus Confronts Opposition and Calls Twelve Apostles (Mark 2:1–3:35)** — Jesus challenges religious expectations, forgives sins, and appoints twelve apostles as opposition to His ministry grows.

Back in Capernaum, a crowd packs the house where Jesus is teaching. Four men carrying a paralyzed friend cannot get in, so they dig a hole in the roof and lower him down. Seeing their faith, Jesus says, “Son, thy sins be forgiven thee.” Some scribes think, “Who can forgive sins but God only?” Jesus knows their thoughts. To show that He has authority to forgive sins, He tells the man to get up, take his mat, and go home, and he does.

Jesus calls Levi, a tax collector, and eats at his house with tax collectors and sinners. Critics complain, but Jesus says, “They that are whole have no need of the physician, but they that are sick: I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.” When asked why His disciples do not fast, He says wedding guests do not fast while the bridegroom is with them. When Pharisees object to His disciples picking grain on the Sabbath, He answers, “The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath.”

In a synagogue, a man with a withered hand is present, and Jesus' enemies watch to see if He will heal on the Sabbath. Jesus looks around at them with anger, “being grieved for the hardness of their hearts,” and heals the man. The Pharisees immediately begin plotting with the Herodians, supporters of King Herod, how to destroy Him.

Huge crowds come from all over. Jesus goes up a mountain and appoints twelve apostles “that they should be with him, and that he might send them forth to preach.” Back home, His own family thinks, “He is beside himself,” and comes to take Him away. Scribes from Jerusalem claim He casts out demons by the prince of demons. Jesus replies, “if a house be divided against itself, that house cannot stand.” When His mother and brothers arrive outside, He looks at those sitting around Him: “Behold my mother and my brethren! For whosoever shall do the will of God, the same is my brother, and my sister, and mother.”

**Jesus Reveals the Kingdom Through Parables and Miracles (Mark 4:1–5:43)** — Jesus teaches how God's kingdom grows and demonstrates authority over storms, evil spirits, sickness, and death.

Jesus teaches the crowd from a boat using parables. A sower's seed falls on the path, on rocky ground, among thorns, and on good soil, where it produces “some thirty, and some sixty, and some an hundred.” A lamp is not meant to be hidden under a bushel basket. Seed grows secretly while the farmer sleeps, “first the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear.” And the kingdom is like a tiny mustard seed that grows into a large plant where birds can nest.

That evening, they cross the lake. A great windstorm hits, and waves fill the boat, while Jesus sleeps on a cushion in the back. The terrified disciples wake Him: “Master, carest thou not that we perish?” He rebukes the wind and says to the sea, “Peace, be still.” Everything goes completely calm. He asks, “Why are ye so fearful? how is it that ye have no faith?” They are filled with awe: “What manner of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?”

On the other shore, a man possessed by demons lives among the tombs. No chain can hold him, and he cries out day and night and cuts himself with stones. When Jesus asks his name, he answers, “My name is Legion: for we are many.” Jesus allows the demons to enter a herd of about two thousand pigs, which rush down a steep bank and drown in the lake. When the townspeople find the man sitting, clothed, and in his right mind, they are afraid and ask Jesus to leave. Jesus tells the man, “Go home to thy friends, and tell them how great things the Lord hath done for thee.”

Back across the lake, Jairus, a synagogue ruler, begs Jesus to come heal his dying twelve-year-old daughter. On the way, a woman who has suffered from bleeding for twelve years and spent all her money on doctors touches His clothes from behind and is instantly healed. Jesus stops and asks who touched Him. Trembling, she confesses, and He says, “Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace.” Just then, messengers arrive: the girl is dead. Jesus tells Jairus, “Be not afraid, only believe.” At the house, mourners laugh when He says she is only sleeping. He takes her hand and says, “Talitha cumi; which is, being interpreted, Damsel, I say unto thee, arise.” She gets up and walks, and Jesus tells them to give her something to eat.

**The Disciples Witness Jesus' Power (Mark 6:1–7:37)** — Jesus sends His disciples to preach, feeds thousands, walks upon the sea, and teaches that true uncleanness comes from within.

In His hometown, people are offended: “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary?” Jesus says, “A prophet is not without honour, but in his own country.” Because of their unbelief, “he could there do no mighty work,” except heal a few sick people. He sends the twelve out two by two, taking nothing for the trip but a walking stick, to preach repentance and heal.

King Herod hears about Jesus and fears that John the Baptist has come back from the dead. Mark tells how it happened. Herod had arrested John for saying it was wrong for him to marry his brother's wife, Herodias. Herodias wanted John dead, but Herod feared John and liked to listen to him. At Herod's birthday banquet, Herodias's daughter danced, and Herod swore to give her anything, up to half his kingdom. Her mother told her to ask for John's head on a platter. Herod was very sorry but would not break his oath in front of his guests, so John was beheaded in prison.

The apostles return, and Jesus says, “Come ye yourselves apart into a desert place, and rest a while.” But a great crowd gets there first. Jesus “was moved with compassion toward them, because they were as sheep not having a shepherd.” He teaches them, then has them sit in groups on the green grass. With five loaves and two fish He feeds five thousand men, and twelve baskets of leftovers are collected. That night the disciples struggle to row against the wind, and Jesus comes to them walking on the sea. They scream, thinking He is a ghost, but He says, “Be of good cheer: it is I; be not afraid.” When He gets in the boat, the wind stops.

Pharisees complain that His disciples eat without the ritual washing of hands. Jesus says they set aside God's commands to keep their own traditions. What makes a person unclean is not food that goes into the stomach, but what comes from the heart: evil thoughts, theft, murder, greed, deceit, and pride. In the region of Tyre, a Greek woman begs Him to free her daughter from a demon. When He tests her, she answers, “Yes, Lord: yet the dogs under the table eat of the children's crumbs,” and her daughter is healed. Then He heals a deaf man with a speech problem, saying, “Ephphatha, that is, Be opened.” The crowds are astonished: “He hath done all things well: he maketh both the deaf to hear, and the dumb to speak.”

**Peter Recognizes Jesus as the Christ (Mark 8:1–9:50)** — Jesus challenges His disciples' understanding, reveals His coming suffering, and teaches that following Him requires sacrifice.

Jesus feeds another crowd, about four thousand people, with seven loaves and a few small fish. The Pharisees demand a sign from heaven, and He refuses. In the boat He warns the disciples about “the leaven of the Pharisees,” but they think He is talking about bread. “Having eyes, see ye not? and having ears, hear ye not?” At Bethsaida, He heals a blind man in two steps. At first the man says, “I see men as trees, walking.” After Jesus touches his eyes again, he sees clearly. Like that man, the disciples are only beginning to see.

Near Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks, “But whom say ye that I am?” Peter answers, “Thou art the Christ.” Jesus then teaches them plainly that the Son of man must suffer many things, be rejected, be killed, and rise again after three days. Peter rebukes Him, and Jesus rebukes Peter: “Get thee behind me, Satan.” He calls the crowd and says, “Whosoever will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me... For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”

Six days later, on a high mountain, Jesus is transfigured before Peter, James, and John. His clothes become shining, “exceeding white as snow.” Elijah and Moses appear, talking with Him. Peter, not knowing what to say, offers to build three shelters. A cloud covers them, and a voice says, “This is my beloved Son: hear him.”

At the bottom of the mountain, the other disciples have failed to free a boy from a spirit that throws him into fire and water. His father pleads, “if thou canst do any thing, have compassion on us.” Jesus says, “If thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth.” The father cries, “Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief.” Jesus drives out the spirit. Again He predicts His death, but the disciples do not understand. On the road they argue about who is the greatest. Jesus sits them down: “If any man desire to be first, the same shall be last of all, and servant of all.” Taking a child in His arms, He says that whoever welcomes such a child welcomes Him. He warns that it would be better to cut off a hand or foot than to let it lead you into sin.

**Jesus Teaches About Marriage, Wealth, and Service (Mark 10:1–52)** — Jesus teaches about faithful relationships, welcomes children, challenges attachment to wealth, and shows that greatness comes through serving others.

Pharisees ask Jesus whether a man may divorce his wife. He says Moses allowed it only “For the hardness of your heart,” but from the beginning God made them male and female, and the two become one flesh. “What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder.” When the disciples scold people for bringing children, Jesus is displeased: “Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not: for of such is the kingdom of God.” He takes them in His arms and blesses them.

A man runs up, kneels, and asks, “Good Master, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?” He says he has kept the commandments since his youth. “Then Jesus beholding him loved him,” and tells him to sell everything, give to the poor, and follow Him. The man goes away sad, “for he had great possessions.” Jesus says, “It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God.” The disciples are amazed, but He tells them, “With men it is impossible, but not with God: for with God all things are possible.”

On the road to Jerusalem, Jesus walks ahead, and His followers are afraid. For the third time, He tells them He will be handed over, mocked, spit on, whipped, and killed, and that He will rise again. Right after this, James and John ask to sit at His right and left in His glory. The other ten are angry. Jesus tells them that among His followers, the leader must be a servant: “For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.”

Leaving Jericho, they pass a blind beggar named Bartimaeus, who shouts, “Jesus, thou son of David, have mercy on me.” People tell him to be quiet, but he shouts even louder. Jesus calls him, and he throws off his cloak and comes. “What wilt thou that I should do unto thee?” “Lord, that I might receive my sight.” Jesus says, “Go thy way; thy faith hath made thee whole.” He sees at once and follows Jesus down the road.

**Jesus Enters Jerusalem and Challenges Its Leaders (Mark 11:1–12:44)** — Jesus enters Jerusalem as the promised king, confronts corruption in the temple, and exposes the failures of religious leadership.

Near Jerusalem, Jesus sends two disciples to bring a young donkey that has never been ridden. He rides it into the city as crowds spread cloaks and branches on the road and shout, “Hosanna; Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord.” The next day, He curses a fig tree that has leaves but no fruit. Then He enters the temple and drives out the buyers and sellers, overturning the money changers' tables: “Is it not written, My house shall be called of all nations the house of prayer? but ye have made it a den of thieves.” The chief priests begin looking for a way to kill Him, because the people hang on His every word. The next morning, the fig tree has withered from the roots. Jesus tells His disciples, “Have faith in God,” and teaches them to pray believing and to forgive.

The religious leaders demand to know by what authority He acts. He tells a parable about tenant farmers who beat the owner's servants and finally kill his “one son, his wellbeloved.” The leaders know the story is about them. Next, Pharisees and Herodians try to trap Him about taxes. Holding a coin, He says, “Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's.” Then Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, ask about a woman widowed by seven brothers. Jesus says they know neither the Scriptures nor God's power, for God is “not the God of the dead, but the God of the living.”

A scribe asks which commandment is first. Jesus answers, “Hear, O Israel; The Lord our God is one Lord: And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind, and with all thy strength.” The second is, “Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.” When the scribe agrees, Jesus tells him, “Thou art not far from the kingdom of God.” Jesus warns about scribes who love long robes and seats of honor but “devour widows' houses.”

Then He sits across from the temple treasury and watches people give. Rich people throw in large amounts. A poor widow puts in “two mites, which make a farthing,” two of the smallest coins. Jesus calls His disciples: “this poor widow hath cast more in, than all they which have cast into the treasury.” The others gave from their extra, but she gave “all that she had, even all her living.”

**Jesus Warns of Coming Trouble and His Return (Mark 13:1–37)** — Jesus predicts the temple's destruction, warns of persecution and deception, and commands His followers to remain watchful for His coming.

Leaving the temple, a disciple says, “Master, see what manner of stones and what buildings are here!” Jesus replies, “There shall not be left one stone upon another, that shall not be thrown down.” Sitting on the Mount of Olives, Peter, James, John, and Andrew ask Him privately when this will happen.

Jesus warns, “Take heed lest any man deceive you.” Many will come claiming to be the Christ. There will be wars, earthquakes, and famines, but “these are the beginnings of sorrows.” His followers will be beaten in synagogues and brought before rulers. But “the gospel must first be published among all nations.” When they are arrested, they should not worry about what to say, for the Holy Spirit will speak through them. Families will turn against each other, “But he that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved.”

When they see “the abomination of desolation, spoken of by Daniel the prophet,” those in Judea must flee to the mountains without even going back for a coat. Those days will bring suffering unlike any since creation. After that, the sun and moon will go dark, and “then shall they see the Son of man coming in the clouds with great power and glory.” He will send His angels to gather His chosen people from the four winds.

Just as fig leaves show that summer is near, these signs will show that the end is near. “Heaven and earth shall pass away: but my words shall not pass away. But of that day and that hour knoweth no man, no, not the angels which are in heaven, neither the Son, but the Father.” So they must stay alert, like servants whose master could come home at evening, at midnight, at the rooster's crow, or in the morning. Jesus ends, “And what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch.”

**Jesus Is Betrayed and Arrested (Mark 14:1–72)** — Religious leaders arrange Jesus' death, Judas betrays Him, and Jesus is arrested after sharing a final meal with His disciples.

Two days before Passover, the chief priests and scribes look for a way to arrest Jesus secretly. At Bethany, a woman breaks open an alabaster jar of very costly perfume and pours it on His head. Some grumble that it could have been sold for over three hundred pence, a year's wages, and given to the poor. Jesus defends her: “She hath done what she could: she is come aforehand to anoint my body to the burying.” Then Judas Iscariot goes to the chief priests, and they promise to pay him.

At the Passover meal, Jesus says, “One of you which eateth with me shall betray me.” Then He takes bread, blesses it, and breaks it: “Take, eat: this is my body.” He gives them the cup: “This is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many.” On the Mount of Olives, He tells them they will all fall away. Peter insists he will not, but Jesus says, “before the cock crow twice, thou shalt deny me thrice.”

In Gethsemane, Jesus is deeply distressed: “My soul is exceeding sorrowful unto death.” He falls to the ground and prays, “Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me: nevertheless not what I will, but what thou wilt.” Abba is an Aramaic word for father. Three times He finds the disciples asleep. “The spirit truly is ready, but the flesh is weak.” Then Judas arrives with a crowd armed with swords and clubs and greets Jesus with a kiss. They seize Him, and all the disciples run away. Mark adds a strange detail: a young man wearing only a linen cloth follows, and when they grab him, he leaves the cloth behind and flees naked. Some think this young man was Mark himself.

At the high priest's house, witnesses give false testimony that does not agree. Finally the high priest asks, “Art thou the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?” Jesus answers, “I am: and ye shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven.” The high priest tears his clothes. They condemn Him to death, spit on Him, blindfold Him, and hit Him, saying, “Prophesy.” Meanwhile, down in the courtyard, a servant girl recognizes Peter. He denies knowing Jesus three times, finally with curses and oaths. Then the rooster crows the second time. Peter remembers Jesus' words, “And when he thought thereon, he wept.”

**Jesus Is Condemned and Crucified (Mark 15:1–47)** — Jesus faces Roman judgment, suffers crucifixion, and is buried while His followers witness the events surrounding His death.

At dawn, the council ties Jesus up and hands Him to Pilate. Pilate asks, “Art thou the King of the Jews?” Jesus answers, “Thou sayest it,” but says nothing more, and Pilate is amazed. It was Pilate's custom to release one prisoner at the feast. The crowd, stirred up by the chief priests, asks for Barabbas, a rebel who had committed murder. “What will ye then that I shall do unto him whom ye call the King of the Jews?” They cry, “Crucify him.” Pilate asks, “Why, what evil hath he done?” But they shout louder. Wanting to please the crowd, Pilate releases Barabbas, has Jesus whipped, and hands Him over to be crucified.

Soldiers dress Jesus in purple, twist a crown of thorns onto His head, and salute Him, “Hail, King of the Jews!” They beat Him and spit on Him. They force a passerby, Simon of Cyrene, “the father of Alexander and Rufus,” to carry the cross. At Golgotha they offer Him wine mixed with myrrh, a drink to dull pain, but He refuses it. It is the third hour, about nine in the morning, when they crucify Him. They gamble for His clothes, and the sign over Him reads, “THE KING OF THE JEWS.” Two thieves are crucified with Him. People passing by and the chief priests mock, “He saved others; himself he cannot save.”

At noon, darkness covers the land until three o'clock. Then Jesus cries with a loud voice, “Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani? which is, being interpreted, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?” After another loud cry, He breathes His last, and the curtain of the temple is torn in two from top to bottom. The centurion standing in front of the cross sees how He died and says, “Truly this man was the Son of God.” Women watch from a distance, including Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses, and Salome. They had followed and cared for Jesus in Galilee.

That evening, Joseph of Arimathaea, “an honourable counsellor, which also waited for the kingdom of God,” boldly asks Pilate for the body. Pilate is surprised Jesus is already dead and confirms it with the centurion. Joseph wraps the body in linen, lays it in a tomb cut out of rock, and rolls a stone against the door. Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses see where He is laid.

**The Tomb Is Empty and Jesus Is Risen (Mark 16:1–20)** — Women discover Jesus' empty tomb, and Mark's closing passage describes resurrection appearances and the disciples' mission to proclaim the gospel.

When the Sabbath is over, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome buy spices to anoint Jesus' body. Very early on Sunday morning, they walk to the tomb, wondering, “Who shall roll us away the stone from the door of the sepulchre?” But the very large stone has already been rolled back. Inside they see “a young man sitting on the right side, clothed in a long white garment,” and they are alarmed. He says, “Be not affrighted: Ye seek Jesus of Nazareth, which was crucified: he is risen; he is not here: behold the place where they laid him.” He tells them, “go your way, tell his disciples and Peter that he goeth before you into Galilee.” Peter, who had denied Jesus, is named personally. The women flee from the tomb, “for they trembled and were amazed: neither said they any thing to any man; for they were afraid.”

The oldest surviving manuscripts of Mark end at that verse. The King James Version includes a longer ending, verses 9–20. In it, Jesus appears first to Mary Magdalene, “out of whom he had cast seven devils,” but the disciples do not believe her. He then appears to two followers walking in the country, and finally to the eleven as they eat. He scolds them for their unbelief and says, “Go ye into all the world, and preach the gospel to every creature. He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved.” Then “he was received up into heaven, and sat on the right hand of God.” The disciples go out and preach everywhere, “the Lord working with them.”

---

Mark's account begins with Jesus announcing God's kingdom and demonstrating His authority through teaching, healing, and deliverance. It follows Him through growing opposition, His willing suffering, and His death upon the cross.

The final chapter announces that the crucified Jesus has risen. The mission that began in Galilee extends outward through His followers, who are sent to proclaim the good news.

# Luke
When it happened: From the announcement of John the Baptist's birth "in the days of Herod, the king of Judaea" (Luke 1:5) to Jesus' ascension. John's ministry began in the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar (Luke 3:1), around AD 28.
When it was written: The book is addressed to Theophilus, and its writer explains that he carefully followed the accounts of eyewitnesses (Luke 1:1–4). Luke and Acts are two volumes by the same writer (Acts 1:1). It is usually dated to the AD 60s–80s.
Where: The temple in Jerusalem, Nazareth, Bethlehem, Galilee, the long journey toward Jerusalem, Emmaus, and Bethany.
The world at the time: Luke ties his account to Roman history—the census of Caesar Augustus (Luke 2:1), the reign of Tiberius, the governor Pontius Pilate, and Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee.

**God Announces the Births of John and Jesus (Luke 1:1–80)** — Luke begins his orderly account with angelic announcements, miraculous pregnancies, and promises that God is preparing salvation for His people.

Luke begins like a careful historian. Many have written about Jesus, he says, and he has “perfect understanding of all things from the very first.” He writes an orderly account for a man named Theophilus so that he may know the truth for certain.

An old priest named Zacharias and his wife Elisabeth are faithful to God but have no children, and they are now too old. While Zacharias is burning incense in the temple, the angel Gabriel appears: “Fear not, Zacharias: for thy prayer is heard; and thy wife Elisabeth shall bear thee a son, and thou shalt call his name John.” John will turn many in Israel back to God, in the spirit and power of Elijah. When Zacharias doubts, Gabriel says, “I am Gabriel, that stand in the presence of God,” and Zacharias is struck unable to speak until the child is born.

Six months later, Gabriel is sent to Nazareth to a young virgin named Mary, engaged to Joseph, a descendant of David. “Hail, thou that art highly favoured, the Lord is with thee: blessed art thou among women.” He tells her she will have a son named Jesus who “shall be called the Son of the Highest,” and “of his kingdom there shall be no end.” Mary asks, “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” Gabriel explains that the Holy Ghost will come upon her, “For with God nothing shall be impossible.” Mary answers, “Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word.”

Mary hurries to visit her relative Elisabeth. When Elisabeth hears Mary's greeting, the baby leaps in her womb, and she cries, “Blessed art thou among women, and blessed is the fruit of thy womb.” Mary responds with a song of praise called the Magnificat: “My soul doth magnify the Lord, And my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour.” God, she sings, “hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree. He hath filled the hungry with good things; and the rich he hath sent empty away.”

When Elisabeth's son is born, the relatives want to name him after his father. Zacharias asks for a writing tablet and writes, “His name is John.” Instantly he can speak again, and he praises God: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel; for he hath visited and redeemed his people.” He says of his son, “And thou, child, shalt be called the prophet of the Highest,” who will “give light to them that sit in darkness and in the shadow of death, to guide our feet into the way of peace.”

**Jesus Is Born and Grows in Wisdom (Luke 2:1–52)** — Jesus is born in Bethlehem, welcomed by shepherds, recognized at the temple, and later found discussing God's word with teachers.

“And it came to pass in those days, that there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed.” Everyone must register in their family's hometown, so Joseph travels from Nazareth to Bethlehem, the city of David, with Mary, who is about to give birth. There “she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn.” A manger is an animals' feeding trough.

“And there were in the same country shepherds abiding in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night.” An angel appears, and God's glory shines around them. They are terrified, but the angel says, “Fear not: for, behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people. For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord.” Suddenly a huge choir of angels appears, praising God: “Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men.” The shepherds hurry to Bethlehem, find the baby just as they were told, and spread the news everywhere. “But Mary kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart.”

Forty days later, Joseph and Mary bring Jesus to the temple in Jerusalem to present Him to the Lord. They offer the sacrifice of poor families, two birds. An old man named Simeon had been promised by the Holy Ghost that he would not die before seeing the Messiah. He takes the baby in his arms and says, “Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, according to thy word: For mine eyes have seen thy salvation,” “A light to lighten the Gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel.” But he warns Mary, “a sword shall pierce through thy own soul also.” Anna, an eighty-four-year-old prophetess who worships in the temple night and day, also gives thanks and tells everyone waiting for redemption about the child.

The family returns to Nazareth. When Jesus is twelve, they go to Jerusalem for Passover. On the way home, His parents discover He is not in their traveling group. After three days of searching, they find Him in the temple, sitting among the teachers, listening and asking questions. Everyone is amazed at His understanding. When Mary says they have been worried sick, He answers, “How is it that ye sought me? wist ye not that I must be about my Father's business?” He goes home and obeys them. “And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and man.”

**Jesus Prepares for His Ministry (Luke 3:1–4:44)** — John calls Israel to repentance, Jesus is baptized and tested, and His preaching and miracles begin attracting attention.

Luke dates the next events carefully, to the fifteenth year of the emperor Tiberius, when Pontius Pilate governed Judea. Then the word of God comes to John in the wilderness. He fulfills Isaiah's words: “Every valley shall be filled... And all flesh shall see the salvation of God.” When people ask what they should do, John gets practical: “He that hath two coats, let him impart to him that hath none.” He tells tax collectors, “Exact no more than that which is appointed you,” and soldiers, “Do violence to no man... and be content with your wages.” Some wonder if John is the Christ, but he says one mightier is coming. Later, Herod Antipas locks John in prison.

When all the people are baptized, Jesus is baptized too. As He prays, heaven opens, and the Holy Ghost descends “in a bodily shape like a dove,” and a voice says, “Thou art my beloved Son; in thee I am well pleased.” “And Jesus himself began to be about thirty years of age.” Luke then traces His family line all the way back past David and Abraham to “Adam, which was the son of God,” showing that Jesus came for all humanity.

Full of the Holy Ghost, Jesus is tempted by the devil for forty days in the wilderness and answers every temptation with Scripture. Then He returns to Galilee in the power of the Spirit. In His hometown synagogue in Nazareth, He stands to read from the scroll of Isaiah: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor; he hath sent me to heal the brokenhearted, to preach deliverance to the captives, and recovering of sight to the blind, to set at liberty them that are bruised, To preach the acceptable year of the Lord.” He rolls up the scroll, sits down, and says, “This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears.” At first they speak well of Him: “Is not this Joseph's son?” But when He reminds them that God once sent Elijah to a foreign widow and Elisha healed only a foreign leper, Naaman, they are furious. They drive Him to the edge of a cliff to throw Him off, “But he passing through the midst of them went his way.”

In Capernaum, Jesus casts out a demon in the synagogue and heals Simon's mother-in-law. At sunset, people bring all their sick, and He lays His hands on every one of them. When the crowd begs Him to stay, He says, “I must preach the kingdom of God to other cities also: for therefore am I sent.”

**Jesus Calls Disciples and Teaches a New Way (Luke 5:1–6:49)** — Jesus gathers followers, forgives sins, confronts religious objections, and teaches a life marked by mercy, humility, and obedience.

Jesus teaches the crowd from Simon Peter's boat, then tells him to go out deeper and let down the nets. Simon says, “Master, we have toiled all the night, and have taken nothing: nevertheless at thy word I will let down the net.” They catch so many fish that the nets begin to break and two boats start to sink. Simon falls at Jesus' knees: “Depart from me; for I am a sinful man, O Lord.” Jesus says, “Fear not; from henceforth thou shalt catch men.” Simon, James, and John leave everything and follow Him.

Jesus heals a man “full of leprosy.” Friends lower a paralyzed man through the roof tiles, and Jesus forgives his sins and heals him. He calls Levi the tax collector, who throws a great feast for Him. To critics He says, “I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.” He explains that new wine needs new wineskins. When Pharisees watch to see if He will heal on the Sabbath, He asks, “Is it lawful on the sabbath days to do good, or to do evil? to save life, or to destroy it?” and heals a man's withered hand.

Jesus goes up a mountain and prays all night. In the morning, He chooses twelve apostles. Then He stands on a level place and teaches a great crowd. “Blessed be ye poor: for yours is the kingdom of God. Blessed are ye that hunger now: for ye shall be filled.” He also warns, “But woe unto you that are rich! for ye have received your consolation.” “Love your enemies, do good to them which hate you.” “And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise.” “Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.” He promises, “give, and it shall be given unto you; good measure, pressed down, and shaken together, and running over.” He asks, “why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother's eye, but perceivest not the beam that is in thine own eye?” A mote is a speck, and a beam is a large board. “And why call ye me, Lord, Lord, and do not the things which I say?” The one who obeys is like a man who “digged deep, and laid the foundation on a rock,” so the flood could not shake his house.

**Jesus Shows Compassion and Reveals His Identity (Luke 7:1–9:50)** — Jesus heals, forgives, teaches through parables, and reveals His power while His disciples gradually recognize Him as the Christ.

A Roman centurion sends Jewish elders to ask Jesus to heal his dying servant. They say he loves their nation and built their synagogue. The centurion sends word that Jesus need only say the word, and Jesus marvels at his faith. At the town of Nain, Jesus meets a funeral procession for a widow's only son. He has compassion on her and says, “Weep not.” He touches the coffin: “Young man, I say unto thee, Arise.” The dead man sits up and begins to speak, and the people say, “God hath visited his people.”

John the Baptist sends two disciples to ask if Jesus is the one. Jesus heals many in front of them and tells them to report what they have seen. Then a Pharisee named Simon invites Jesus to dinner. A woman known in town as a sinner comes in with an alabaster jar of ointment. Weeping, she washes His feet with her tears, wipes them with her hair, kisses them, and anoints them. Simon thinks that if Jesus were a prophet, He would know what kind of woman she is. Jesus tells of two debtors, one who owed five hundred pence and one who owed fifty. Both were forgiven. Which would love more? Then He says, “Her sins, which are many, are forgiven; for she loved much: but to whom little is forgiven, the same loveth little.” To her He says, “Thy faith hath saved thee; go in peace.”

As Jesus travels, the twelve are with Him, along with women He had healed. These include Mary Magdalene, “out of whom went seven devils,” Joanna, the wife of Herod's steward, and Susanna, “which ministered unto him of their substance,” paying for His ministry from their own money. He tells the parable of the sower. He calms a storm and asks the disciples, “Where is your faith?” He frees the man possessed by Legion, heals the bleeding woman, and raises Jairus's daughter.

He sends the twelve out to preach and heal. Then He feeds five thousand men with five loaves and two fish. Alone with His disciples, He asks who they say He is, and Peter answers, “The Christ of God.” Jesus warns that He must suffer and be killed and rise the third day, and that His followers must “take up his cross daily.” About eight days later, as He prays on a mountain, His face changes and His clothes become dazzling white. Moses and Elijah appear and speak with Him about “his decease which he should accomplish at Jerusalem.” A voice from a cloud says, “This is my beloved Son: hear him.” The next day He heals a boy the disciples could not help. But the disciples argue about who is greatest, and He sets a child beside Him: “he that is least among you all, the same shall be great.”

**Jesus Begins His Journey Toward Jerusalem (Luke 9:51–11:54)** — As Jesus travels toward Jerusalem, He teaches the cost of discipleship, compassion for neighbors, prayer, and the danger of religious hypocrisy.

This section marks a turning point: “he stedfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem.” Much of the rest of Luke happens on this long journey. When a Samaritan village refuses to welcome Him, James and John ask, “wilt thou that we command fire to come down from heaven, and consume them?” Jesus rebukes them. To would-be followers who want to wait, He says, “No man, having put his hand to the plough, and looking back, is fit for the kingdom of God.”

Jesus sends out seventy others in pairs: “I send you forth as lambs among wolves.” They return full of joy: “Lord, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name.” Jesus says, “I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven,” but tells them to rejoice instead “because your names are written in heaven.”

A lawyer asks, “Master, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?” He agrees that the law says to love God and to love your neighbor as yourself. But then he asks, “And who is my neighbour?” Jesus tells of a man who is robbed, beaten, and left half dead on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. A priest sees him and passes by on the other side. So does a Levite. But a Samaritan, a member of a group the Jews despised, “had compassion on him.” He bandages his wounds, takes him to an inn, and pays for his care. Jesus asks which one was a neighbor. The lawyer says, “He that shewed mercy on him.” Jesus says, “Go, and do thou likewise.”

In the village of Bethany, Martha welcomes Jesus and is busy serving, while her sister Mary sits at His feet listening. Martha complains, but Jesus says, “Martha, Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things: But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part, which shall not be taken away from her.” When a disciple asks, “Lord, teach us to pray,” Jesus teaches them the Lord's Prayer. He tells of a friend who knocks at midnight asking for bread and gets it because he keeps asking. “If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him?”

When critics claim He casts out demons by Beelzebub, Jesus says He does it “with the finger of God.” A woman calls out a blessing on His mother, but He says, “Yea rather, blessed are they that hear the word of God, and keep it.” At a Pharisee's dinner, He condemns the Pharisees for cleaning the outside of the cup while their hearts are full of greed. He tells the lawyers, “ye have taken away the key of knowledge.” From then on, they watch Him closely, hoping to catch Him in His words.

**Jesus Warns Against Greed and Calls for Readiness (Luke 12:1–13:35)** — Jesus urges trust in God, repentance, and watchfulness while warning that wealth and outward religion cannot provide lasting security.

As thousands crowd around, Jesus warns, “Beware ye of the leaven of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy.” He tells His friends not to fear those who can only kill the body. God does not forget even one sparrow, and “the very hairs of your head are all numbered.”

A man asks Jesus to make his brother share an inheritance. Jesus warns, “a man's life consisteth not in the abundance of the things which he possesseth.” He tells of a rich farmer whose crops were so large that he planned to build bigger barns and say to himself, “Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry. But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee.” So His followers should not worry. “Consider the ravens,” which God feeds, and the lilies, which He clothes. “Fear not, little flock; for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom.” They should be like servants ready for their master's return, “For unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required.” He warns that following Him will divide even families.

Some people tell Jesus about Galileans whom Pilate killed while they offered sacrifices. Jesus asks if they were worse sinners than others. He also mentions eighteen people killed when the tower in Siloam fell. “I tell you, Nay: but, except ye repent, ye shall all likewise perish.” He tells of a fig tree that has borne no fruit for three years. The gardener asks for one more year to dig around it and fertilize it. God's patience is real, but it will not last forever.

On the Sabbath, Jesus sees a woman who has been bent over for eighteen years and cannot stand up straight. “Woman, thou art loosed from thine infirmity.” She immediately stands up and praises God. When the synagogue ruler objects, Jesus points out that they untie their animals on the Sabbath to give them water. Should not this daughter of Abraham be set free? Someone asks if only a few will be saved. He answers, “Strive to enter in at the strait gate.” People will come from east and west to the kingdom, and “there are last which shall be first.” When warned that Herod wants to kill Him, Jesus says, “Go ye, and tell that fox” that He will finish His work. A prophet cannot die outside Jerusalem. Then He grieves, “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, which killest the prophets.”

**God Seeks the Lost and Welcomes the Repentant (Luke 14:1–16:31)** — Through meals and parables, Jesus confronts pride and selfishness while revealing God's joy over repentance and the responsibility that comes with wealth.

At a Pharisee's Sabbath dinner, Jesus heals a man with swelling, then notices guests grabbing the best seats. He tells them to take the lowest seat instead: “For whosoever exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.” He tells the host to invite “the poor, the maimed, the lame, the blind,” who cannot pay him back. Then He tells of a man who prepares a great supper, but the invited guests make excuses. One bought land, one bought oxen, and one just got married. The angry host sends his servant into the streets for the poor and disabled, and then out to “the highways and hedges, and compel them to come in.” To the large crowds He warns, “whosoever doth not bear his cross, and come after me, cannot be my disciple,” and tells them to count the cost, like a builder planning a tower.

Tax collectors and sinners gather to hear Jesus, and the Pharisees grumble, “This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them.” So He tells three stories about lost things. A shepherd leaves ninety-nine sheep to find one lost sheep, carries it home on his shoulders, and says, “Rejoice with me; for I have found my sheep which was lost.” “Joy shall be in heaven over one sinner that repenteth, more than over ninety and nine just persons.” A woman sweeps her whole house to find one lost silver coin and throws a party when she finds it.

Then comes the most famous of all, the parable of the prodigal son. A younger son demands his inheritance early, leaves home, and “wasted his substance with riotous living.” When famine comes, he is so hungry that he wishes he could eat the pigs' food. “And when he came to himself,” he decides to go home and say, “Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, And am no more worthy to be called thy son.” “But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.” The father calls for the best robe, a ring, and the fatted calf: “For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found.” But the older brother refuses to come to the party. He complains that he has served faithfully for years. The father pleads with him, “Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.” But they had to celebrate, for “this thy brother was dead, and is alive again.” The story ends without saying whether the older brother came in, leaving the question for the grumbling Pharisees.

Jesus tells of a dishonest manager who cleverly uses his master's money to make friends before he is fired. He teaches, “He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much,” and “Ye cannot serve God and mammon.” Finally He tells of a rich man “clothed in purple and fine linen,” who feasted every day while a beggar named Lazarus lay at his gate, covered with sores that dogs licked. Both die. Lazarus is carried by angels to Abraham's side. The rich man is in torment. Abraham says that between them “there is a great gulf fixed.” The rich man begs Abraham to send Lazarus to warn his five brothers. Abraham replies, “If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead.”

**Faith, Humility, and Salvation on the Road (Luke 17:1–19:27)** — Jesus teaches forgiveness, gratitude, prayer, and humility while bringing restoration to people who are overlooked or rejected.

Jesus tells His disciples to forgive a brother who repents, even seven times in a day. They respond, “Lord, Increase our faith.” He says that faith as small as a mustard seed can uproot a tree.

On the border between Samaria and Galilee, ten lepers stand at a distance and call out, “Jesus, Master, have mercy on us.” He sends them to show themselves to the priests, and on the way they are healed. Only one comes back, praising God loudly and falling at Jesus' feet, “and he was a Samaritan.” Jesus asks, “Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine?” When Pharisees ask when the kingdom will come, Jesus says, “the kingdom of God is within you,” or in their midst. He warns that the day of His return will come suddenly, as in the days of Noah and Lot: “Remember Lot's wife.”

Jesus tells a parable to show “that men ought always to pray, and not to faint.” A widow keeps coming to an unjust judge until he gives her justice just to get rid of her. How much more will God answer His people? Then He speaks to those who trust in their own goodness. Two men go to the temple to pray. The Pharisee stands and prays, “God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are.” But the tax collector, “standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner.” Jesus says the tax collector “went down to his house justified rather than the other.”

Jesus welcomes little children. A rich ruler turns away sad when told to sell all he has. Near Jericho, Jesus heals a blind beggar. In Jericho lives Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector who is rich. Zacchaeus wants to see Jesus but is “little of stature,” so he climbs a sycomore tree. Jesus stops under the tree: “Zacchaeus, make haste, and come down; for to day I must abide at thy house.” The crowd grumbles that Jesus is staying with a sinner. But Zacchaeus stands and says, “Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold.” Jesus says, “This day is salvation come to this house... For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost.” Because people expect God's kingdom to appear right away, He tells of a nobleman who leaves to receive a kingdom and gives his servants money to invest. Some of his citizens send word, “We will not have this man to reign over us.” When he returns, he rewards the faithful servants and judges the rest.

**Jesus Enters Jerusalem and Faces Opposition (Luke 19:28–21:38)** — Jesus enters Jerusalem, confronts corrupt worship, answers His opponents, and warns of coming destruction and the need for watchfulness.

As Jesus rides a colt down the Mount of Olives, His disciples praise God: “Blessed be the King that cometh in the name of the Lord: peace in heaven, and glory in the highest.” Some Pharisees say, “Master, rebuke thy disciples.” He answers, “I tell you that, if these should hold their peace, the stones would immediately cry out.” But when He sees the city, He weeps over it. He says that if only it had known “the things which belong unto thy peace!” Enemies will surround it and tear it down, “because thou knewest not the time of thy visitation.” He enters the temple and drives out the sellers: “My house is the house of prayer: but ye have made it a den of thieves.” Every day He teaches there, while the leaders look for a way to destroy Him.

The leaders challenge His authority, and He tells the parable of the tenants who kill the owner's son. Spies try to trap Him about taxes, and He says, “Render therefore unto Caesar the things which be Caesar's, and unto God the things which be God's.” To the Sadducees He says that God “is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.” He warns against scribes who “devour widows' houses.” Then He watches a poor widow put two small coins in the offering: “this poor widow hath cast in more than they all.”

When people admire the temple's beautiful stones, He predicts it will be torn down. He warns of wars, earthquakes, famines, and persecution: “In your patience possess ye your souls.” “And when ye shall see Jerusalem compassed with armies, then know that the desolation thereof is nigh.” “Jerusalem shall be trodden down of the Gentiles, until the times of the Gentiles be fulfilled.” Then there will be signs in the sun, moon, and stars, and they will see the Son of man coming in a cloud. “Look up, and lift up your heads; for your redemption draweth nigh.” “Heaven and earth shall pass away: but my words shall not pass away.” He warns them not to let their hearts be weighed down with partying, drunkenness, “and cares of this life.” Each day He teaches in the temple, and each night He stays on the Mount of Olives. Early in the morning the people come to hear Him.

**Jesus Shares His Final Meal and Is Arrested (Luke 22:1–71)** — As Passover approaches, Jesus prepares His disciples for His suffering, institutes the Lord's Supper, and is betrayed and arrested.

As Passover approaches, “Then entered Satan into Judas surnamed Iscariot,” and he agrees to betray Jesus for money. Jesus sends Peter and John to prepare the meal in a large upper room. At the table He says, “With desire I have desired to eat this passover with you before I suffer.” He takes bread: “This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me.” After supper He takes the cup: “This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you.” Even then, the disciples argue about who is greatest. Jesus tells them that among them, the leader must be like a servant: “I am among you as he that serveth.”

Jesus turns to Peter: “Simon, Simon, behold, Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat: But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not: and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren.” Peter insists, “Lord, I am ready to go with thee, both into prison, and to death.” Jesus tells him that before the rooster crows that day, he will deny Him three times.

On the Mount of Olives, Jesus kneels and prays, “Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done.” An angel comes to strengthen Him. “And being in an agony he prayed more earnestly: and his sweat was as it were great drops of blood falling down to the ground.” He finds the disciples “sleeping for sorrow.” Then Judas arrives with a crowd. Jesus asks, “Judas, betrayest thou the Son of man with a kiss?” One disciple cuts off the right ear of the high priest's servant, but Jesus says, “Suffer ye thus far,” touches the man's ear, and heals him. To the officials He says, “this is your hour, and the power of darkness.”

They take Jesus to the high priest's house. Peter follows at a distance and sits by a fire in the courtyard. Three times people say he was with Jesus, and three times he denies it. While he is still speaking, the rooster crows. “And the Lord turned, and looked upon Peter.” Peter remembers Jesus' words, “And Peter went out, and wept bitterly.” The guards blindfold Jesus, beat Him, and mock Him, saying, “Prophesy, who is it that smote thee?” At daybreak the council asks, “Art thou then the Son of God?” He answers, “Ye say that I am.” They say they need no more witnesses.

**Jesus Is Condemned, Crucified, and Buried (Luke 23:1–56)** — Jesus is sentenced under Roman authority, forgives His enemies, promises paradise to a condemned man, and dies upon the cross.

The council brings Jesus to Pilate, accusing Him of “perverting the nation, and forbidding to give tribute to Caesar, saying that he himself is Christ a King.” Pilate questions Him and says, “I find no fault in this man.” Hearing that Jesus is from Galilee, Pilate sends Him to Herod Antipas, who is in Jerusalem. Herod hopes to see a miracle, but Jesus does not answer him at all. Herod and his soldiers mock Him, dress Him in a gorgeous robe, and send Him back. “And the same day Pilate and Herod were made friends together.” Three times Pilate says Jesus has done nothing deserving death. But the crowd shouts, “Crucify him, crucify him,” and demands the release of Barabbas, who was in prison for rebellion and murder. Pilate gives in.

Soldiers make Simon of Cyrene carry the cross behind Jesus. A great crowd follows, including women who weep for Him. He turns and says, “Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me, but weep for yourselves, and for your children,” because hard days are coming. At the place called Calvary, they crucify Him between two criminals. Jesus prays, “Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.” The soldiers gamble for His clothes, and rulers and soldiers mock Him. Above Him is a sign written in Greek, Latin, and Hebrew: “THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS.”

One of the criminals sneers, “If thou be Christ, save thyself and us.” But the other rebukes him: “Dost not thou fear God...? And we indeed justly; for we receive the due reward of our deeds: but this man hath done nothing amiss.” Then he says, “Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom.” Jesus answers, “Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise.”

From noon until three o'clock darkness covers the land, the sun is darkened, and the temple curtain is torn down the middle. Jesus cries with a loud voice, “Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit,” and breathes His last. The centurion praises God: “Certainly this was a righteous man.” The crowds go home beating their chests in grief. Joseph of Arimathaea, a council member who had not agreed with their decision, asks for the body. He wraps it in linen and lays it in a rock tomb “wherein never man before was laid.” The women from Galilee see the tomb, then go home to prepare spices. They “rested the sabbath day according to the commandment.”

**Jesus Rises and Commissions His Followers (Luke 24:1–53)** — The empty tomb, appearances of the risen Jesus, and His ascension bring Luke's account to its conclusion.

Very early Sunday morning, the women bring their spices to the tomb. They find the stone rolled away and the body gone. Two men in shining clothes stand beside them and ask, “Why seek ye the living among the dead? He is not here, but is risen.” They remind the women that Jesus had said He would be crucified and rise on the third day. Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and other women tell the apostles, but “their words seemed to them as idle tales.” Peter runs to the tomb, sees the linen cloths lying by themselves, and goes home wondering.

That same day, two followers, one named Cleopas, are walking to the village of Emmaus, about seven miles from Jerusalem. Jesus Himself joins them, but they do not recognize Him. Sadly they tell Him about Jesus' death: “But we trusted that it had been he which should have redeemed Israel.” He answers, “O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken.” Then, “beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.” At the village they urge Him, “Abide with us: for it is toward evening.” At supper, “he took bread, and blessed it, and brake, and gave to them. And their eyes were opened, and they knew him; and he vanished out of their sight.” They say to each other, “Did not our heart burn within us, while he talked with us by the way?” They rush back to Jerusalem that night and hear the news: “The Lord is risen indeed, and hath appeared to Simon.”

While they are still talking, Jesus stands among them: “Peace be unto you.” They are terrified, thinking He is a spirit. “Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have.” To prove it, He eats a piece of broiled fish in front of them. Then He opens their minds to understand the Scriptures. “Thus it is written, and thus it behoved Christ to suffer, and to rise from the dead the third day: And that repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name among all nations, beginning at Jerusalem.” He tells them to wait in the city “until ye be endued with power from on high.”

He leads them out as far as Bethany, lifts up His hands, and blesses them. As He blesses them, “he was parted from them, and carried up into heaven.” They worship Him and return to Jerusalem “with great joy: And were continually in the temple, praising and blessing God.” Luke's second volume, the book of Acts, picks up the story from there.

# John
When it happened: Jesus' ministry, which John's Gospel spans across at least three Passovers (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55), around AD 27–30.
When it was written: It is usually dated to around AD 85–95, likely the last of the four Gospels, and early tradition places its writing in Ephesus. It states its own purpose: "that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ" (John 20:31).
Where: Judea, Galilee (including Cana), Samaria (at Sychar), Jerusalem—where Jesus attended the feasts at the temple—and Bethany.
The world at the time: As in the other Gospels, Judea was under Roman rule. John pays special attention to the Jewish feasts—Passover, tabernacles, and dedication—and to the conflict between Jesus and the religious leaders in Jerusalem.

**The Word Becomes Flesh (John 1:1–51)** — John introduces Jesus as the eternal Word who became human, while John the Baptist identifies Him and His first disciples begin following Him.

John begins before time itself: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” “All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made. In him was life; and the life was the light of men. And the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not.” The Word came into the world He made, but “He came unto his own, and his own received him not. But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name.” Then John says plainly who the Word is: “And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth.” “No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son... he hath declared him.”

Priests from Jerusalem ask John the Baptist who he is. He says he is not the Christ, but “the voice of one crying in the wilderness.” The next day he sees Jesus coming and says, “Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world.” He says he saw the Spirit come down like a dove and stay on Jesus, “and bare record that this is the Son of God.”

The next day, two of John's disciples follow Jesus. He turns and asks, “What seek ye?” They spend the day with Him. One of them, Andrew, finds his brother Simon: “We have found the Messias,” which means the Christ. Jesus looks at Simon and gives him a new name, Cephas, which means a stone, or Peter. Jesus calls Philip, who tells Nathanael, “We have found him, of whom Moses in the law, and the prophets, did write, Jesus of Nazareth.” Nathanael scoffs, “Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth?” Philip says, “Come and see.” When Jesus tells Nathanael He saw him under a fig tree before Philip called him, Nathanael exclaims, “thou art the Son of God; thou art the King of Israel.” Jesus promises he will see even greater things.

**Jesus Reveals His Authority and Offers New Life (John 2:1–3:36)** — Jesus performs His first sign, confronts improper temple practices, and teaches that eternal life comes through believing in God's Son.

At a wedding in Cana of Galilee, the wine runs out. Jesus' mother tells Him, then tells the servants, “Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it.” Jesus has them fill six large stone water jars to the brim, then draw some out and take it to the master of the feast. It has become wine, and the best wine of all. The master tells the groom, “thou hast kept the good wine until now.” John calls this the “beginning of miracles,” the first of the signs that show who Jesus is, “and his disciples believed on him.”

At Passover in Jerusalem, Jesus finds the temple full of people selling cattle, sheep, and doves, and money changers at their tables. He makes a whip of cords, drives them all out, and overturns the tables: “make not my Father's house an house of merchandise.” When the leaders demand a sign, He says, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” They think He means the building, which took forty-six years to build, but “he spake of the temple of his body.”

A Pharisee named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews, comes to Jesus at night. Jesus tells him, “Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God.” Nicodemus asks how a grown man can be born again. Jesus explains that one must be “born of water and of the Spirit,” and that the Spirit is like the wind, which no one can see. Just as Moses lifted up the bronze serpent in the wilderness, the Son of man must be lifted up. Then come the most famous words in the Bible: “For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. For God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved.” Yet people loved darkness rather than light.

Later, John the Baptist's disciples are upset that more people are going to Jesus. John answers, “He must increase, but I must decrease.” The chapter ends, “He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life.”

**Jesus Offers Living Water and Reveals His Power (John 4:1–5:47)** — Jesus reveals God's salvation to a Samaritan woman, heals a child, and declares His authority to give life and execute judgment.

Traveling to Galilee, Jesus goes through Samaria, a region most Jews avoided. Tired, He sits by Jacob's well at noon. A Samaritan woman comes to draw water, and He asks her for a drink. She is shocked, because Jews and Samaritans do not get along. Jesus says that if she knew who He was, she would ask Him for “living water.” “Whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst.” When He tells her to call her husband, she says she has none. He answers that she has had five husbands, and the man she lives with now is not her husband. Amazed, she changes the subject to where people should worship. Jesus says, “God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.” She says the Messiah will explain everything when He comes. Jesus says, “I that speak unto thee am he.” She leaves her water jar and runs to town: “Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?” Many Samaritans believe and say, “this is indeed the Christ, the Saviour of the world.” Meanwhile Jesus tells His disciples, “Lift up your eyes, and look on the fields; for they are white already to harvest.”

Back in Cana, a royal official begs Jesus to come heal his dying son in Capernaum. Jesus says, “Go thy way; thy son liveth.” The man believes and goes. On the way, his servants meet him with the news that the boy recovered at the very hour Jesus spoke.

In Jerusalem, at the pool of Bethesda, many sick people lie waiting for the water to stir. One man has been ill for thirty-eight years. Jesus asks, “Wilt thou be made whole?” and tells him, “Rise, take up thy bed, and walk.” He is healed at once. But it is the Sabbath, and the leaders are angry. Jesus answers, “My Father worketh hitherto, and I work.” They try even harder to kill Him, because He is “making himself equal with God.” Jesus explains that the Son does what He sees the Father doing. “He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life.” He tells them, “Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me.”

**Jesus Feeds Thousands and Calls Himself the Bread of Life (John 6:1–71)** — Jesus feeds a vast crowd, walks upon the sea, and teaches that He alone gives the lasting life people truly need.

Near Passover, a great crowd follows Jesus to a mountainside by the Sea of Galilee. He tests Philip by asking where they could buy bread for so many. Andrew points out a boy “which hath five barley loaves, and two small fishes: but what are they among so many?” Jesus has the people sit on the grass, gives thanks, and feeds about five thousand men. The leftovers fill twelve baskets. The people want to make Him king by force, so He slips away to the mountain alone.

That night the disciples row across the lake in a strong wind. They see Jesus walking on the sea toward them and are afraid, but He says, “It is I; be not afraid.” As soon as they take Him into the boat, they reach the shore.

The next day the crowd finds Him in Capernaum. Jesus tells them they came for more free bread, not because they understood the sign. They ask for a sign like the manna Moses gave. Jesus says, “I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst.” This is the first of seven “I am” sayings in John. “Him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out.” The people grumble, “Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph?” Then Jesus says something even harder: “Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you.” Many find this too hard to accept. “From that time many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him.”

Jesus asks the twelve, “Will ye also go away?” Peter answers, “Lord, to whom shall we go? thou hast the words of eternal life. And we believe and are sure that thou art that Christ, the Son of the living God.” Jesus replies that He chose all twelve, “and one of you is a devil.” He means Judas Iscariot, who would betray Him.

**Jesus Confronts Unbelief and Offers Living Water (John 7:1–8:59)** — During a Jewish festival, Jesus teaches publicly in Jerusalem, faces divided opinions, and declares that He is the light of the world.

Jesus' brothers do not believe in Him. They urge Him to go to Jerusalem for the Feast of Tabernacles to show Himself. He goes later, in secret. Halfway through the feast, He begins teaching openly in the temple. People argue about Him. Some say, “He is a good man,” and others, “Nay; but he deceiveth the people.” The leaders send officers to arrest Him. On the last great day of the feast, Jesus stands and cries out, “If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink. He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water.” John explains that He meant the Holy Spirit. The officers come back empty-handed and say, “Never man spake like this man.” Nicodemus asks the council to give Jesus a fair hearing, but they mock him.

The next morning, scribes and Pharisees bring a woman caught in the act of adultery and ask whether she should be stoned, as the law of Moses says. They want to trap Him. Jesus stoops and writes on the ground with His finger. When they keep asking, He stands and says, “He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her.” One by one, from the oldest to the youngest, they leave. Jesus asks the woman, “Woman, where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee?” “No man, Lord.” “Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more.” Many early manuscripts do not include this story, but it has long been part of John.

Jesus declares, “I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life.” To those who believe, He says, “If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed; And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.” His listeners insist they are Abraham's children and have never been slaves. Jesus says that whoever sins is a slave to sin, “If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed.” He tells them that if God were their Father they would love Him. Instead, they are doing the work of the devil, “a liar, and the father of it.” Finally He says, “Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am.” This echoes God's name from the burning bush, “I AM.” They understand what He is claiming and pick up stones to kill Him, but He slips away.

**Jesus Opens Blind Eyes and Calls His Sheep (John 9:1–10:42)** — Jesus gives sight to a man born blind and teaches that He is the good shepherd who knows and protects His people.

Jesus sees a man blind from birth. His disciples ask, “who did sin, this man, or his parents, that he was born blind?” Jesus answers, “Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him.” He spits on the ground, makes mud, puts it on the man's eyes, and tells him, “Go, wash in the pool of Siloam.” The man goes, washes, and comes back seeing.

Because it is the Sabbath, the Pharisees investigate. They question the man, then his parents, who are afraid of being thrown out of the synagogue. They tell the man Jesus is a sinner. He answers, “Whether he be a sinner or no, I know not: one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see.” When they keep questioning him, he asks, “will ye also be his disciples?” He reasons that if Jesus were not from God, He could do nothing. They throw him out. Jesus finds him and asks if he believes in the Son of God. “Lord, I believe,” he says, and worships Him. Jesus says He came so that the blind might see and those who think they see might be shown to be blind.

Then Jesus describes Himself as a shepherd. The sheep know their shepherd's voice and follow him, but they run from strangers. “I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved.” “The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy: I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly. I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep.” A hired hand runs when the wolf comes, but Jesus knows His sheep and lays down His life for them. “No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself.” He has other sheep too, not of this fold, “and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd.”

In winter, at the Feast of Dedication, the leaders surround Him: “If thou be the Christ, tell us plainly.” He says His works already tell them. “My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me: And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand.” Then He says, “I and my Father are one.” Again they pick up stones, and again He escapes, going back across the Jordan, where many believe in Him.

**Jesus Raises Lazarus and Approaches His Final Hour (John 11:1–12:50)** — Jesus raises Lazarus from the dead, leading many to believe while religious leaders decide that Jesus must be killed.

Lazarus of Bethany, the brother of Mary and Martha, is very sick. The sisters send word to Jesus: “Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick.” Jesus loves all three, yet He waits two more days. Then He tells His disciples, “Our friend Lazarus sleepeth,” meaning he has died. By the time Jesus arrives, Lazarus has been in the tomb four days. Martha meets Him: “Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died.” Jesus tells her, “I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live.” He asks, “Believest thou this?” She answers, “Yea, Lord: I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God.” Mary comes weeping, and the mourners with her are weeping too. Jesus is deeply moved. Then comes the shortest verse in the Bible: “Jesus wept.”

At the tomb, a cave with a stone over it, Jesus says, “Take ye away the stone.” Martha warns that the body will smell after four days. They roll the stone away, and Jesus prays aloud. Then He cries with a loud voice, “Lazarus, come forth.” The dead man comes out, still wrapped in grave cloths. “Loose him, and let him go.”

Many believe, but some report it to the Pharisees. The council fears that if everyone believes, the Romans will destroy their nation. The high priest Caiaphas says “that it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people.” John notes that without realizing it, Caiaphas was prophesying that Jesus would die for the nation and for God's scattered children. From that day, they plot to kill Him.

Six days before Passover, at a dinner in Bethany, Mary pours a pound of costly spikenard on Jesus' feet and wipes them with her hair, “and the house was filled with the odour of the ointment.” Judas complains that it could have been sold for the poor. John notes that Judas “was a thief,” stealing from the money bag he kept. The next day, crowds carrying palm branches meet Jesus as He rides into Jerusalem on a young donkey, shouting, “Hosanna: Blessed is the King of Israel that cometh in the name of the Lord.” The Pharisees say, “behold, the world is gone after him.”

Some Greeks come asking to see Jesus. He says, “The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified.” “Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit.” He is troubled but will not ask to be spared, “for this cause came I unto this hour.” A voice from heaven answers Him. “And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me.” Even so, many will not believe, and some leaders who do believe keep quiet, “For they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God.”

**Jesus Serves His Disciples and Promises the Spirit (John 13:1–14:31)** — During His final evening with the disciples, Jesus demonstrates humble service, announces His betrayal, and promises continuing help from the Father.

Before Passover, “having loved his own which were in the world, he loved them unto the end.” During supper, Jesus gets up, takes off His outer garment, wraps a towel around His waist, pours water into a basin, and begins washing His disciples' feet, the job of the lowest servant. Peter objects, “Thou shalt never wash my feet.” Jesus says, “If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me.” Then Peter asks Him to wash his hands and head too. Afterward Jesus explains, “If I then, your Lord and Master, have washed your feet; ye also ought to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example.”

Jesus is troubled and says, “one of you shall betray me.” The disciple “whom Jesus loved,” traditionally John, asks who it is. Jesus says it is the one He gives a piece of bread to, and He gives it to Judas. “Satan entered into him,” and Jesus tells him, “That thou doest, do quickly.” Judas goes out, “and it was night.” Then Jesus gives a new command: “A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you... By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another.” Peter promises to lay down his life for Jesus. Jesus tells him he will deny Him three times before the rooster crows.

Jesus comforts them: “Let not your heart be troubled: ye believe in God, believe also in me. In my Father's house are many mansions: if it were not so, I would have told you. I go to prepare a place for you.” Thomas says they do not know the way. Jesus answers, “I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me.” Philip asks to see the Father. Jesus says, “he that hath seen me hath seen the Father.”

He promises that He will not leave them alone. “If ye love me, keep my commandments. And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever.” The Comforter is the Holy Ghost, who “shall teach you all things, and bring all things to your remembrance.” “Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid.” Then He says, “Arise, let us go hence.”

**Jesus Prepares His Followers for What Comes Next (John 15:1–17:26)** — Jesus teaches His disciples to remain in Him, warns of persecution, and prays for their protection and unity.

Jesus says, “I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman,” or gardener. “I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing.” Branches that do not bear fruit are cut off, and fruitful ones are pruned to bear more. “As the Father hath loved me, so have I loved you: continue ye in my love.” “This is my commandment, That ye love one another, as I have loved you. Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends.” “Henceforth I call you not servants... but I have called you friends.” “Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you.”

He warns that the world will hate them because it hated Him first. The servant is not greater than his master. The time will come when people kill them and think they are serving God. But it is better for them that He goes away, because then the Comforter will come. He will convict the world of sin, righteousness, and judgment, and He will “guide you into all truth.” Their sorrow will turn to joy, like a mother's joy after giving birth. “In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world.”

Then Jesus lifts His eyes to heaven and prays. “Father, the hour is come; glorify thy Son, that thy Son also may glorify thee.” “And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent.” “I have finished the work which thou gavest me to do.” He prays for His disciples, who will stay in the world: “I pray not that thou shouldest take them out of the world, but that thou shouldest keep them from the evil.” “Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth.” Then He prays for everyone who will believe through their message, “That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee... that the world may believe that thou hast sent me.”

**Jesus Is Arrested and Sentenced to Death (John 18:1–19:16)** — Judas betrays Jesus, Peter denies knowing Him, and Jewish leaders bring Jesus before the Roman governor to demand His execution.

Jesus crosses the Kidron Valley to a garden. Judas, who knows the place, comes with soldiers and officers carrying lanterns, torches, and weapons. Jesus steps forward and asks, “Whom seek ye?” “Jesus of Nazareth.” When He answers, “I am he,” they step back and fall to the ground. He tells them to let His disciples go. Simon Peter draws a sword and cuts off the right ear of the high priest's servant, Malchus. Jesus tells him, “Put up thy sword into the sheath: the cup which my Father hath given me, shall I not drink it?”

They take Jesus first to Annas, the former high priest and father-in-law of Caiaphas. Peter and another disciple follow. At the door, a servant girl asks Peter if he is one of Jesus' disciples. He says, “I am not.” Inside, Annas questions Jesus, and an officer slaps Him. They send Him to Caiaphas. Warming himself at a fire, Peter denies Jesus a second time, and then a third time, to a relative of Malchus. Immediately the rooster crows.

Early in the morning they bring Jesus to Pilate's headquarters. They do not go inside, so they will not become ritually unclean before Passover. Pilate asks, “Art thou the King of the Jews?” Jesus answers, “My kingdom is not of this world.” He says He came “that I should bear witness unto the truth. Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice.” Pilate replies, “What is truth?” He goes out and tells the crowd, “I find in him no fault at all,” and offers to release Him. They shout for Barabbas instead, and John adds, “Now Barabbas was a robber.”

Pilate has Jesus whipped. The soldiers put a crown of thorns and a purple robe on Him and slap Him. Pilate brings Him out and says, “Behold the man!” The chief priests cry, “Crucify him, crucify him.” They say He must die “because he made himself the Son of God.” This frightens Pilate even more. He asks Jesus, “Whence art thou?” but Jesus gives no answer. When Pilate claims power over Him, Jesus says, “Thou couldest have no power at all against me, except it were given thee from above.” Pilate tries to release Him, but the leaders warn, “If thou let this man go, thou art not Caesar's friend.” At noon on the day of preparation for Passover, Pilate says, “Behold your King!” They shout, “We have no king but Caesar.” So Pilate hands Him over to be crucified.

**Jesus Is Crucified and Buried (John 19:17–42)** — Jesus dies upon the cross, fulfilling the work He came to accomplish, and His body is placed in a nearby tomb.

Carrying His own cross, Jesus goes out to “a place called the place of a skull, which is called in the Hebrew Golgotha.” They crucify Him between two others. Pilate has a sign written in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin: “JESUS OF NAZARETH THE KING OF THE JEWS.” The chief priests ask him to change it to say that Jesus only claimed to be king, but Pilate answers, “What I have written I have written.” The soldiers divide His clothes. His coat is woven in one piece without a seam, so they cast lots for it, fulfilling Psalm 22.

Near the cross stand His mother, her sister, Mary the wife of Cleophas, and Mary Magdalene. Seeing His mother and the disciple He loved, Jesus says, “Woman, behold thy son!” and to the disciple, “Behold thy mother!” From that hour the disciple takes her into his home. Knowing that everything is now done, Jesus says, “I thirst.” They lift a sponge of vinegar to His mouth. Then He says, “It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost.”

The leaders do not want bodies on crosses during the Sabbath, so they ask Pilate to have the legs broken to speed up death. The soldiers break the legs of the two men beside Jesus. But when they come to Jesus, He is already dead, so they do not break His legs. Instead, a soldier pierces His side with a spear, “and forthwith came there out blood and water.” John insists he is telling the truth as an eyewitness, “that ye might believe,” and he points to two Scriptures: “A bone of him shall not be broken,” and “They shall look on him whom they pierced.”

Joseph of Arimathaea, a secret disciple “for fear of the Jews,” asks Pilate for the body. Nicodemus, who first came to Jesus at night, brings about a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes. Together they wrap the body in linen with the spices, as was the Jewish burial custom. Nearby is a garden with a new tomb where no one had ever been laid. Because the Sabbath is about to begin, they lay Jesus there.

**Jesus Rises and Appears to His Disciples (John 20:1–31)** — Mary Magdalene discovers the empty tomb, the risen Jesus appears to His followers, and Thomas comes to believe after seeing Him.

Early on the first day of the week, while it is still dark, Mary Magdalene comes to the tomb and sees the stone taken away. She runs to Peter and the other disciple: “They have taken away the Lord out of the sepulchre, and we know not where they have laid him.” The two men race to the tomb. The other disciple gets there first and looks in. Peter goes inside and sees the linen cloths lying there, with the cloth that had been around Jesus' head folded in a place by itself. Then the other disciple goes in, “and he saw, and believed.”

Mary stays outside the tomb, weeping. She looks in and sees two angels in white sitting where the body had been. “Woman, why weepest thou?” She turns and sees Jesus standing there but thinks He is the gardener. She begs Him to tell her where He has put the body. Then Jesus says, “Mary.” She turns and cries, “Rabboni,” which means Master. Jesus tells her, “Touch me not; for I am not yet ascended to my Father,” and sends her to tell His brothers. She goes and tells the disciples that she has seen the Lord.

That evening, the disciples are together behind locked doors, afraid of the leaders. Jesus comes and stands among them: “Peace be unto you.” He shows them His hands and His side, and they are overjoyed. “As my Father hath sent me, even so send I you.” He breathes on them and says, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost.”

Thomas was not there. When the others tell him, he says, “Except I shall see in his hands the print of the nails, and put my finger into the print of the nails, and thrust my hand into his side, I will not believe.” Eight days later, Jesus comes again through locked doors and says to Thomas, “Reach hither thy finger, and behold my hands; and reach hither thy hand, and thrust it into my side: and be not faithless, but believing.” Thomas answers, “My Lord and my God.” Jesus says, “Thomas, because thou hast seen me, thou hast believed: blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed.” John then explains why he wrote his book: “But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.”

**Jesus Restores Peter and Calls Him to Follow (John 21:1–25)** — The risen Jesus meets His disciples beside the Sea of Tiberias, provides a miraculous catch of fish, and renews Peter's responsibility to care for His followers.

Later, by the Sea of Tiberias, which is another name for the Sea of Galilee, Peter says, “I go a fishing,” and six other disciples go with him. They fish all night and catch nothing. At dawn, a man on the shore calls out, “Children, have ye any meat?” They answer no. He tells them, “Cast the net on the right side of the ship, and ye shall find.” Suddenly the net is so full they cannot haul it in. The disciple whom Jesus loved says, “It is the Lord.” Peter throws on his coat, jumps into the sea, and swims to shore. There Jesus has a charcoal fire going with fish and bread. The net holds 153 large fish, yet it does not break. Jesus says, “Come and dine,” and serves them breakfast.

After breakfast, Jesus asks Peter, “Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me more than these?” Peter says, “Yea, Lord; thou knowest that I love thee.” Jesus says, “Feed my lambs.” He asks a second time, and Peter answers the same. “Feed my sheep.” Then a third time: “lovest thou me?” Peter is grieved that Jesus asks a third time, matching his three denials beside another charcoal fire. “Lord, thou knowest all things; thou knowest that I love thee.” Jesus says, “Feed my sheep.” Then He tells Peter that when he is old, someone else will lead him where he does not want to go. John explains that this showed how Peter would die and glorify God. Then Jesus says, “Follow me.”

Peter sees the disciple whom Jesus loved and asks, “Lord, and what shall this man do?” Jesus answers, “If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee? follow thou me.” A rumor spread that this disciple would not die, but John corrects it. Jesus did not say that. The writer adds that he is that disciple, and that his testimony is true. He closes by saying that Jesus did so many other things that “if they should be written every one, I suppose that even the world itself could not contain the books that should be written.”

# Acts
When it happened: From Jesus' ascension (around AD 30) to the end of Paul's two years under house arrest in Rome (Acts 28:30), around AD 60–62.
When it was written: Acts is the second volume written to Theophilus (Acts 1:1). Several passages say "we," suggesting the writer travelled with Paul (for example Acts 16:10). It is usually dated to the AD 60s–80s.
Where: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, Damascus, Antioch in Syria, Cyprus, the cities of Asia Minor (including Galatia and Ephesus), Macedonia (Philippi and Thessalonica), Greece (Athens and Corinth), Caesarea, the island of Melita, and Rome.
The world at the time: The Roman Empire's roads, sea routes, and relative peace made travel possible, and Jewish synagogues were found in cities across the empire. Roman officials such as the governors Felix and Festus, and King Agrippa, appear in the story.

**Jesus Promises Power to His Followers (Acts 1:1–26)** — Before ascending into heaven, Jesus promises the Holy Ghost, and His followers prepare to continue His work by choosing a replacement for Judas.

Luke begins his second book by reminding Theophilus of the first, which told “all that Jesus began both to do and teach.” After His resurrection, Jesus appears to the apostles over forty days and tells them to wait in Jerusalem for the promised Holy Ghost. They ask if He will now restore the kingdom to Israel. He answers, “It is not for you to know the times or the seasons... But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth.” That verse is an outline of the whole book, as the message spreads outward from Jerusalem.

Then “while they beheld, he was taken up; and a cloud received him out of their sight.” Two men in white appear beside them: “Ye men of Galilee, why stand ye gazing up into heaven? this same Jesus, which is taken up from you into heaven, shall so come in like manner as ye have seen him go into heaven.”

The apostles return to Jerusalem and pray together in an upper room with the women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and His brothers, about 120 believers in all. Peter explains that Judas, who betrayed Jesus, had died, and the field bought with his reward came to be called Aceldama, “The field of blood.” They need someone to replace him, a man who has been with them since John's baptism and saw the risen Jesus. They pray and cast lots, and the lot falls on Matthias.

**The Holy Ghost Comes at Pentecost (Acts 2:1–47)** — God pours out the Holy Ghost upon the believers, empowering their witness and bringing thousands into a new community centered on Jesus.

On the day of Pentecost, a harvest feast fifty days after Passover, the believers are “all with one accord in one place.” “And suddenly there came a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind, and it filled all the house.” Then “cloven tongues like as of fire” appear and rest on each of them. “And they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and began to speak with other tongues, as the Spirit gave them utterance.”

Jerusalem is crowded with Jews from many nations for the feast. When they hear the noise, a crowd gathers, and they are amazed that “every man heard them speak in his own language.” Some mock, “These men are full of new wine.” Peter, the same man who had denied Jesus, stands up with the eleven and speaks boldly: “these are not drunken, as ye suppose, seeing it is but the third hour of the day. But this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel.” God has poured out His Spirit, just as He promised. Peter tells them about Jesus, whom they crucified but “God hath raised up,” and quotes David's psalms as predicting it. He concludes, “God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ.”

The listeners “were pricked in their heart” and cry, “Men and brethren, what shall we do?” Peter answers, “Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost.” That day “about three thousand souls” are added to them.

The new believers form a close community. “They continued stedfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers.” They share their possessions, selling property to help anyone in need. Every day they meet in the temple and eat together in their homes with glad hearts. “And the Lord added to the church daily such as should be saved.”

**The Apostles Face Their First Opposition (Acts 3:1–5:42)** — Miraculous healing draws attention to Jesus, while religious authorities challenge the apostles and the growing community faces judgment within its own ranks.

One afternoon, Peter and John go up to the temple to pray. At the gate called Beautiful sits a man who has been lame from birth, begging. Peter looks at him and says, “Silver and gold have I none; but such as I have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk.” He takes him by the hand, and the man's feet and ankles become strong. He goes into the temple “walking, and leaping, and praising God.” A crowd rushes over, and Peter tells them that the power came from Jesus, whom they “killed the Prince of life, whom God hath raised from the dead.” “Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out.”

The temple leaders arrest Peter and John, but the number of believing men grows to about five thousand. The next day, standing before the same council that condemned Jesus, Peter declares, “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.” The rulers are amazed at the boldness of these “unlearned and ignorant men,” and they realize “that they had been with Jesus.” They order them to stop speaking about Jesus. Peter and John answer, “Whether it be right in the sight of God to hearken unto you more than unto God, judge ye. For we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard.” When they are released, the believers pray for boldness, and the place where they are gathered shakes.

The church is “of one heart and of one soul.” A man named Joses, whom the apostles call Barnabas, “The son of consolation,” sells a field and gives the money to the apostles. But a couple named Ananias and Sapphira sell property, secretly keep part of the money, and pretend to give it all. Peter tells Ananias, “thou hast not lied unto men, but unto God.” Ananias falls down dead. Three hours later, Sapphira tells the same lie and also dies. “And great fear came upon all the church.”

Many are healed. People even lay the sick in the streets hoping that Peter's shadow will fall on them. The jealous high priest throws the apostles in prison, but during the night an angel opens the doors and sends them back to the temple to preach. Brought before the council again, Peter says, “We ought to obey God rather than men.” The council wants to kill them, but a respected teacher named Gamaliel warns, “if this counsel or this work be of men, it will come to nought: But if it be of God, ye cannot overthrow it.” So they beat the apostles and let them go. The apostles leave “rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name,” and they never stop teaching and preaching Jesus Christ.

**Stephen's Death Scatters the Believers (Acts 6:1–8:40)** — The church appoints servants to meet practical needs, Stephen is killed for his testimony, and persecution carries the message beyond Jerusalem.

As the church grows, the Greek-speaking Jewish believers complain that their widows are being overlooked in the daily food distribution. The apostles say, “It is not reason that we should leave the word of God, and serve tables.” So the church chooses seven men “full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom” to manage the work. Among them are Stephen and Philip.

Stephen, “full of faith and power,” does great wonders. When opponents cannot win arguments with him, they bring false witnesses who say he speaks against the temple and the law of Moses. As the council stares at him, they see “his face as it had been the face of an angel.” Stephen answers with a long speech retelling Israel's history from Abraham to Joseph, Moses, and Solomon. Again and again, he shows, Israel rejected the leaders God sent. He ends, “Ye stiffnecked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, ye do always resist the Holy Ghost: as your fathers did, so do ye.” The council is furious. But Stephen looks up and says, “Behold, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing on the right hand of God.” They cover their ears, rush at him, drag him out of the city, and stone him. The witnesses lay their coats “at a young man's feet, whose name was Saul.” Stephen prays, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit,” and then, kneeling, “Lord, lay not this sin to their charge.” “And when he had said this, he fell asleep.” “And Saul was consenting unto his death.”

That day a great persecution breaks out, and believers scatter throughout Judea and Samaria. Saul “made havock of the church,” going house to house and dragging men and women to prison. But the scattered believers preach wherever they go. Philip preaches in Samaria, and many believe and are healed. A famous magician named Simon believes too. When Peter and John come and lay hands on the new believers to receive the Holy Ghost, Simon offers them money for this power. Peter answers, “Thy money perish with thee, because thou hast thought that the gift of God may be purchased with money.”

An angel sends Philip to a desert road toward Gaza. There an Ethiopian eunuch, the treasurer of Queen Candace, is riding home in his chariot reading Isaiah aloud. Philip runs up and asks, “Understandest thou what thou readest?” He answers, “How can I, except some man should guide me?” He is reading, “He was led as a sheep to the slaughter.” Starting from that passage, Philip tells him about Jesus. When they come to some water, the man says, “See, here is water; what doth hinder me to be baptized?” Philip baptizes him, then the Spirit carries Philip away, and the Ethiopian “went on his way rejoicing.”

**Saul Encounters Jesus and Receives a New Calling (Acts 9:1–43)** — A fierce persecutor becomes a follower of Jesus, while Peter's ministry continues through remarkable healings and the raising of a woman from death.

“And Saul, yet breathing out threatenings and slaughter against the disciples of the Lord,” gets letters from the high priest to arrest believers in Damascus. Near the city, a light from heaven suddenly flashes around him. He falls to the ground and hears a voice: “Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?” “Who art thou, Lord?” “I am Jesus whom thou persecutest: it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks,” like an ox kicking against the sharp stick its driver uses to guide it. Saul gets up blind, and his companions lead him into Damascus. For three days he cannot see, and he does not eat or drink.

The Lord tells a believer in Damascus named Ananias to go to Saul. Ananias protests that Saul has done great harm to the believers. But the Lord says, “he is a chosen vessel unto me, to bear my name before the Gentiles, and kings, and the children of Israel.” Ananias goes, lays his hands on Saul, and calls him “Brother Saul.” Immediately “there fell from his eyes as it had been scales,” and he can see. He is baptized and right away begins preaching in the synagogues that Jesus “is the Son of God.” Everyone is amazed that the persecutor has become a preacher. When the Jews plot to kill him, the disciples lower him over the city wall at night in a basket.

In Jerusalem the believers are afraid of Saul, until Barnabas brings him to the apostles and vouches for him. When Greek-speaking Jews try to kill him, the believers send him home to Tarsus. “Then had the churches rest throughout all Judaea and Galilee and Samaria.”

Meanwhile Peter travels among the churches. In Lydda he heals Aeneas, who has been paralyzed for eight years. In the seaside town of Joppa, a beloved disciple named Tabitha, also called Dorcas, dies. She was “full of good works and almsdeeds.” The weeping widows show Peter the coats and clothing she had made for them. Peter sends everyone out, kneels, prays, and says, “Tabitha, arise.” She opens her eyes and sits up, and many in Joppa believe.

**God Opens the Door to the Gentiles (Acts 10:1–11:30)** — Peter learns that God's invitation extends beyond Israel, and the growing church recognizes that Gentiles can receive the Holy Ghost through faith.

In Caesarea lives Cornelius, a Roman centurion, “A devout man, and one that feared God with all his house,” who gives generously and prays to God always. But he is a Gentile, not a Jew. An angel tells him to send for a man named Peter, who is staying in Joppa.

The next day, Peter goes up on the roof to pray and becomes hungry. He falls into a trance and sees a great sheet let down from heaven, full of animals that Jewish law called unclean. A voice says, “Rise, Peter; kill, and eat.” Peter answers, “Not so, Lord; for I have never eaten any thing that is common or unclean.” The voice says, “What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common.” This happens three times. While Peter is wondering what it means, Cornelius's messengers arrive at the gate.

Peter goes with them to Caesarea, where Cornelius has gathered his relatives and close friends. Normally, a Jew would not enter a Gentile's home, but Peter says, “God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean.” Now he understands the vision: “Of a truth I perceive that God is no respecter of persons: But in every nation he that feareth him, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with him.” He tells them about Jesus. While he is still speaking, “the Holy Ghost fell on all them which heard the word.” The Jewish believers who came with Peter are astonished that the gift of the Holy Ghost is poured out on Gentiles too. Peter has them baptized.

Back in Jerusalem, some believers criticize Peter for eating with Gentiles. He tells them the whole story. When they hear it, they stop objecting and praise God: “Then hath God also to the Gentiles granted repentance unto life.” Meanwhile, believers scattered by the persecution reach Antioch in Syria and begin telling Greeks about Jesus, and many believe. The Jerusalem church sends Barnabas, who goes to Tarsus to find Saul and brings him back. For a whole year they teach there, “And the disciples were called Christians first in Antioch.” When a prophet named Agabus predicts a great famine, the Antioch believers send relief to the believers in Judea by the hands of Barnabas and Saul.

**Peter Is Rescued and Paul Begins His Journeys (Acts 12:1–14:28)** — The church endures persecution while the Holy Ghost sends Barnabas and Saul to preach across new regions, establishing communities despite fierce opposition.

King Herod Agrippa, a grandson of Herod the Great, kills James the brother of John with the sword. When he sees that this pleases the Jewish leaders, he arrests Peter too, planning to execute him after Passover. Peter is chained between two soldiers, with guards at the door, “but prayer was made without ceasing of the church unto God for him.” The night before his trial, an angel appears in the cell and strikes Peter on the side: “Arise up quickly.” The chains fall off his hands. They walk past the guards, and the iron gate to the city “opened to them of his own accord.” Peter goes to the house of Mary, John Mark's mother, where many are praying. A servant girl named Rhoda recognizes his voice and is so happy that she runs to tell the others without opening the door. They tell her, “Thou art mad.” But Peter keeps knocking, and they are amazed. Later, Herod gives a speech, and the crowd shouts, “It is the voice of a god, and not of a man.” Immediately an angel strikes him because he did not give God the glory, and he dies, eaten by worms. “But the word of God grew and multiplied.”

In Antioch, as the church worships and fasts, the Holy Ghost says, “Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them.” They lay hands on them and send them off on the first missionary journey, with young John Mark as their helper. On the island of Cyprus, a sorcerer opposes them, and Saul, “who also is called Paul,” strikes him blind for a time. The Roman governor believes. From here on, Luke uses the name Paul. John Mark leaves them and goes home.

In Antioch of Pisidia, Paul preaches in the synagogue. The next Sabbath almost the whole city comes, but jealous Jewish leaders argue against him. Paul and Barnabas say, “lo, we turn to the Gentiles,” for God has said, “I have set thee to be a light of the Gentiles.” The Gentiles rejoice, and many believe, but Paul and Barnabas are driven out. In Iconium a plot to stone them forces them to flee. In Lystra Paul heals a man crippled from birth. The crowd shouts that the gods have come down, calling Barnabas Jupiter and Paul Mercurius, and a priest brings oxen to sacrifice to them. The apostles tear their clothes: “Sirs, why do ye these things? We also are men of like passions with you.” Soon after, enemies from Antioch and Iconium turn the crowd against them, and they stone Paul and drag him out of the city, thinking he is dead. But as the disciples gather around him, he gets up and goes back into the city.

After preaching in Derbe, Paul and Barnabas return through the same towns, strengthening the believers: “we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God.” They appoint elders in every church. Back in Syrian Antioch, they report how God “had opened the door of faith unto the Gentiles.”

**The Church Resolves a Major Disagreement (Acts 15:1–35)** — Church leaders meet in Jerusalem to decide whether Gentile believers must follow the law of Moses to be saved.

Some men come down from Judea to Antioch teaching, “Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved.” In other words, Gentiles must become Jews first. Paul and Barnabas strongly disagree, so the church sends them to Jerusalem to settle the question with the apostles and elders. There, some believers who were Pharisees insist that Gentile converts must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses.

After much debate, Peter stands up. He reminds them how God gave the Holy Ghost to Cornelius's household, and “put no difference between us and them, purifying their hearts by faith.” “Now therefore why tempt ye God, to put a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear? But we believe that through the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ we shall be saved, even as they.” The crowd falls silent as Barnabas and Paul tell of the miracles God did among the Gentiles.

Then James, the brother of Jesus and a leader of the Jerusalem church, speaks. He quotes the prophet Amos: “After this I will return, and will build again the tabernacle of David, which is fallen down,” so that the Gentiles might seek the Lord. His judgment is “that we trouble not them, which from among the Gentiles are turned to God.” Gentile believers should only be asked to avoid food offered to idols, sexual immorality, and eating blood or strangled animals. These things would especially offend their Jewish neighbors.

The church sends Judas and Silas with Paul and Barnabas, carrying a letter: “For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things.” When the church in Antioch reads it, they rejoice. The church has decided that Gentiles are saved by grace through faith, just as Jews are, without having to become Jews first.

**Paul Carries the Gospel into Macedonia and Greece (Acts 15:36–18:22)** — Paul begins another journey with new companions, preaching across the Roman world and establishing churches among Jews and Gentiles.

Paul suggests to Barnabas that they visit the churches again. Barnabas wants to take John Mark, but Paul refuses, since Mark had left them before. “And the contention was so sharp between them, that they departed asunder one from the other.” Barnabas takes Mark to Cyprus, and Paul chooses Silas. In Lystra they add a young believer named Timothy.

The Holy Ghost blocks them from preaching in some regions. Then in Troas, Paul has a vision at night of a man pleading, “Come over into Macedonia, and help us.” At this point, Luke begins writing “we,” showing that he himself joined the journey. In Philippi, they find women praying by a river. One of them, Lydia, a seller of purple cloth, listens, and “the Lord opened” her heart. She and her household are baptized.

A slave girl with a spirit that tells fortunes follows them for days. Paul commands the spirit to come out of her. Her owners, angry about losing their income, drag Paul and Silas before the city officials. They are beaten with rods and locked in stocks in the inner prison. “And at midnight Paul and Silas prayed, and sang praises unto God: and the prisoners heard them.” Suddenly an earthquake shakes the prison, the doors fly open, and everyone's chains come loose. The jailer wakes, sees the open doors, and draws his sword to kill himself. But Paul shouts, “Do thyself no harm: for we are all here.” Trembling, the jailer falls before them: “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?” They answer, “Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved, and thy house.” That night he washes their wounds, and his whole household is baptized. The next day, Paul reveals that they are Roman citizens, who could not legally be beaten without a trial. The frightened officials come to apologize.

In Thessalonica, many believe, but a mob forms, complaining, “These that have turned the world upside down are come hither also.” In Berea, the Jews are “more noble” because they “searched the scriptures daily” to check what Paul taught. In Athens, Paul's “spirit was stirred in him, when he saw the city wholly given to idolatry.” Philosophers bring him to the Areopagus, also called Mars' hill. He tells them he found an altar inscribed “TO THE UNKNOWN GOD. Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you.” This God made the world and does not live in temples. “For in him we live, and move, and have our being.” He calls them to repent, because God will judge the world by the man He raised from the dead. At the mention of resurrection, some mock, but a few believe.

In Corinth, Paul stays with Aquila and Priscilla, Jewish believers who were tentmakers like him. The Lord tells him in a vision, “Be not afraid, but speak, and hold not thy peace: For I am with thee.” He stays a year and a half. When the Jews bring him before the Roman governor Gallio, Gallio refuses to hear a religious dispute and throws out the case. Paul then sails back to Antioch.

**Paul Strengthens the Churches and Faces New Conflict (Acts 18:23–21:16)** — Paul's continued ministry brings deeper teaching, powerful conversions, and growing opposition, while he prepares for danger awaiting him in Jerusalem.

Paul sets out again, strengthening the disciples. Meanwhile, an eloquent teacher named Apollos arrives in Ephesus. He knows the Scriptures well but only knows the baptism of John, so Priscilla and Aquila take him aside and explain the way of God more accurately. In Ephesus, Paul meets about twelve disciples who have never heard of the Holy Ghost. He baptizes them in the name of Jesus and lays hands on them, and the Holy Ghost comes on them. For two years Paul teaches daily in the lecture hall of Tyrannus, “so that all they which dwelt in Asia heard the word of the Lord Jesus.”

God does unusual miracles through Paul. Even handkerchiefs he touched are carried to the sick, and they are healed. Seven sons of a Jewish priest named Sceva try to use Jesus' name like a magic spell to cast out a demon. The evil spirit answers, “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?” The possessed man attacks them, and they run out naked and wounded. Afterward, many who practiced magic bring their books and burn them publicly, books worth fifty thousand pieces of silver.

A silversmith named Demetrius makes his living selling little silver shrines of the goddess Diana. He warns the other craftsmen that Paul's preaching is ruining their business. The city erupts in a riot. A mob rushes into the huge theater and shouts for about two hours, “Great is Diana of the Ephesians.” Finally the town clerk calms the crowd and sends them home.

On his way to Jerusalem, Paul preaches late into the night at Troas. A young man named Eutychus, sitting in a third-floor window, falls asleep and falls out. He is picked up dead. Paul goes down, embraces him, and says, “his life is in him,” and they take him home alive. At Miletus, Paul calls the elders of Ephesus for a tearful goodbye. He says the Holy Ghost warns him that chains and trouble await him. “But none of these things move me, neither count I my life dear unto myself, so that I might finish my course with joy.” He tells them, “Take heed therefore unto yourselves, and to all the flock,” and reminds them of Jesus' words, “It is more blessed to give than to receive.” They weep and kiss him, sad that they will not see his face again.

At Caesarea, a prophet named Agabus takes Paul's belt, ties his own hands and feet with it, and says the Jews in Jerusalem will bind its owner and hand him over to the Gentiles. Everyone begs Paul not to go. He answers, “I am ready not to be bound only, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus.” They finally say, “The will of the Lord be done.”

**Paul Is Arrested in Jerusalem (Acts 21:17–23:35)** — Paul's arrival in Jerusalem leads to unrest, arrest, and a plot against his life, but the Lord assures him that he will also testify in Rome.

In Jerusalem, James and the elders welcome Paul and praise God for what He has done among the Gentiles. But they warn that thousands of Jewish believers have heard rumors that Paul teaches Jews to abandon the law of Moses. To show that this is false, Paul joins four men in a temple purification ceremony. Near the end of the seven days, some Jews from Asia see him in the temple. They stir up the crowd, falsely claiming he brought a Greek into the temple. The whole city is in an uproar. They drag Paul out and try to kill him. The Roman commander rushes in with soldiers, arrests Paul, and the soldiers have to carry him up the stairs because the mob is so violent.

From the stairs, Paul asks permission to speak and addresses the crowd in Hebrew. He tells them his story: how he persecuted the followers of Jesus, how Jesus met him on the road to Damascus, and how the Lord told him, “Depart: for I will send thee far hence unto the Gentiles.” At the word Gentiles, the crowd erupts again. The commander orders Paul to be whipped and questioned. As they tie him up, Paul asks, “Is it lawful for you to scourge a man that is a Roman, and uncondemned?” The commander is alarmed. He says he paid a large sum for his own citizenship, but Paul answers, “But I was free born.”

The next day, Paul stands before the Jewish council. Seeing that part of the council are Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, and part are Pharisees, he cries out, “of the hope and resurrection of the dead I am called in question.” The council splits into a shouting match, and the commander has to rescue Paul again. That night the Lord stands by him: “Be of good cheer, Paul: for as thou hast testified of me in Jerusalem, so must thou bear witness also at Rome.”

More than forty men swear an oath not to eat or drink until they have killed Paul. But Paul's nephew hears of the plot and warns the commander. That night, the commander sends Paul away under a guard of nearly five hundred soldiers and horsemen to Caesarea, to the Roman governor Felix.

**Paul Defends His Faith Before Rulers (Acts 24:1–26:32)** — Paul remains in Roman custody and explains his faith before governors Felix and Festus and King Agrippa.

Five days later, the high priest arrives in Caesarea with a lawyer named Tertullus, who calls Paul “a pestilent fellow” and “a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes.” Paul answers calmly that he did nothing wrong in the temple: “after the way which they call heresy, so worship I the God of my fathers.” Felix delays the case. Later, he and his wife Drusilla send for Paul to hear about faith in Christ. “And as he reasoned of righteousness, temperance, and judgment to come, Felix trembled, and answered, Go thy way for this time; when I have a convenient season, I will call for thee.” Felix hopes Paul will pay him a bribe, so he keeps him in custody for two years.

The next governor, Festus, asks Paul if he is willing to be tried in Jerusalem. Paul knows a plot awaits him there. As a Roman citizen, he has the right to have his case heard by the emperor, so he says, “I appeal unto Caesar.” Festus answers, “hast thou appealed unto Caesar? unto Caesar shalt thou go.”

A few days later, King Agrippa and Bernice visit Festus. Agrippa wants to hear Paul, so they enter the hall with great pomp. Paul tells his story again, from his life as a strict Pharisee to the light on the Damascus road. He says, “I was not disobedient unto the heavenly vision.” As he speaks of Christ's death and resurrection, Festus interrupts loudly, “Paul, thou art beside thyself; much learning doth make thee mad.” Paul replies, “I am not mad, most noble Festus; but speak forth the words of truth and soberness.” Then he turns to the king: “King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest.” Agrippa answers, “Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian.” Paul says, “I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were both almost, and altogether such as I am, except these bonds.” Afterward the rulers agree that Paul has done nothing deserving death or prison. Agrippa says, “This man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar.”

**A Dangerous Voyage Ends in Shipwreck (Acts 27:1–28:10)** — Paul travels toward Rome under guard, survives a violent storm and shipwreck, and continues ministering after reaching the island of Melita.

Paul and other prisoners sail for Italy under a centurion named Julius, who treats Paul kindly. Luke goes with him. Winds slow the trip, and it becomes late in the sailing season. At a harbor called Fair Havens on Crete, Paul warns that continuing will bring disaster. But the centurion listens to the ship's owner and pilot instead. Soon a violent northeastern wind called Euroclydon hits. For days the crew throws cargo and gear overboard. “And when neither sun nor stars in many days appeared, and no small tempest lay on us, all hope that we should be saved was then taken away.”

Then Paul stands up and says, “be of good cheer: for there shall be no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship.” An angel of God had stood by him that night and said, “Fear not, Paul; thou must be brought before Caesar.” “Wherefore, sirs, be of good cheer: for I believe God, that it shall be even as it was told me.” On the fourteenth night, the sailors sense land and try to sneak away in the lifeboat. Paul warns the centurion, “Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved,” and the soldiers cut the lifeboat loose. Before dawn, Paul urges all 276 people on board to eat. He gives thanks to God in front of them all and eats, and they are encouraged.

At daylight the ship runs aground on a sandbar, and the back of the ship is broken apart by the waves. The soldiers plan to kill the prisoners so none can escape, but the centurion stops them to save Paul. Everyone swims or floats to shore on boards and broken pieces of the ship, “And so it came to pass, that they escaped all safe to land.”

The island is Melita, today called Malta. The local people show them unusual kindness and build a fire in the cold rain. As Paul puts sticks on the fire, a viper bites and hangs from his hand. The islanders say, “No doubt this man is a murderer,” whom justice will not let live. But Paul shakes the snake into the fire and is not harmed. When nothing happens to him, they change their minds and say he is a god. Paul heals the father of Publius, the island's chief man, and many other sick people come and are healed. After three months, the islanders load them with supplies for the journey.

**The Gospel Reaches Rome (Acts 28:11–31)** — Paul arrives in Rome, continues preaching despite imprisonment, and the account closes with God's kingdom being proclaimed freely in the empire's capital.

They sail on another ship and land in Italy. Believers from Rome walk out to meet them at the Forum of Appius and the Three Taverns, “whom when Paul saw, he thanked God, and took courage.” In Rome, Paul is allowed to live by himself with a soldier guarding him.

After three days, Paul calls together the local Jewish leaders. He explains that he has done nothing against his people, and that “for the hope of Israel I am bound with this chain.” They say they have heard nothing bad about him, but about this sect, “we know that every where it is spoken against.” On an appointed day, many come, and from morning until evening Paul explains the kingdom of God and tries to persuade them about Jesus from the law of Moses and the prophets. Some believe, and some do not. As they leave, Paul quotes Isaiah's warning about people who hear but do not understand, and says, “the salvation of God is sent unto the Gentiles, and that they will hear it.”

The book ends with Paul still waiting for his trial, but not silenced: “And Paul dwelt two whole years in his own hired house, and received all that came in unto him, Preaching the kingdom of God, and teaching those things which concern the Lord Jesus Christ, with all confidence, no man forbidding him.” The message that began in Jerusalem has reached the capital of the empire, just as Jesus promised in chapter 1.
