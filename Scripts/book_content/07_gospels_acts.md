# Matthew
When it happened: From Jesus' birth "in the days of Herod the king" (Matthew 2:1)—before Herod's death in 4 BC—through his ministry, death, and resurrection, around AD 27–30.
When it was written: The author is not named in the book. Early church tradition credits Matthew, the tax collector who became one of the twelve apostles (Matthew 9:9). Most scholars date it between the AD 50s and 80s.
Where: Bethlehem, Egypt, Nazareth, Galilee (especially Capernaum and the Sea of Galilee), Judea, and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Judea was under Roman rule, governed by Herod the Great and later his sons and Roman governors such as Pontius Pilate. Many Jews longed for the promised Messiah, and the Pharisees and Sadducees were leading religious groups.

**Matthew 1-2** — Matthew begins with "The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham." Mary, who was engaged to Joseph, was found with child of the Holy Ghost. An angel told Joseph in a dream to take her as his wife, "and thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins." Jesus was born in Bethlehem. Wise men from the east followed his star and worshipped him with gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh. Warned in a dream, Joseph fled with the child and his mother to Egypt, escaping King Herod, who killed the young children of Bethlehem. After Herod died, they settled in Nazareth.

**Matthew 3-4** — John the Baptist preached in the wilderness, "Repent ye: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand," and baptized Jesus in the Jordan. The Spirit of God descended like a dove, and a voice from heaven said, "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." Jesus fasted forty days in the wilderness and answered each of the devil's temptations with Scripture. He began preaching in Galilee and called the fishermen Peter, Andrew, James, and John: "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men."

**Matthew 5-7** — On a mountain Jesus taught what is called the Sermon on the Mount: "Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." He called his followers the salt of the earth and the light of the world, taught them to love their enemies, gave them the Lord's Prayer, and told them not to worry: "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness." He ended with the wise man who built his house upon a rock.

**Matthew 8-12** — Jesus healed a leper, a centurion's servant, Peter's mother-in-law, and many others; he calmed a storm and cast out devils. He called Matthew the tax collector, ate with sinners, and said, "I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance." He sent out the twelve apostles and invited, "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." Opposition grew as the Pharisees accused him of casting out devils by Beelzebub.

**Matthew 13-17** — Jesus taught in parables: the sower, the wheat and the tares, the mustard seed, the hidden treasure, and the pearl of great price. He fed five thousand men with five loaves and two fishes and walked on the water. When he asked his disciples who they said he was, Peter answered, "Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God." From then on Jesus began to tell them that he must suffer, be killed, and be raised again the third day. On a high mountain he was transfigured before Peter, James, and John, and his face shone like the sun.

**Matthew 18-25** — Jesus taught about humility and about forgiving "Until seventy times seven." He went up to Jerusalem riding on an ass while crowds cried, "Hosanna to the son of David," and drove the money changers out of the temple. He answered his opponents' questions and named the greatest commandments: to love God with all your heart and to love your neighbour as yourself. He pronounced woes on the scribes and Pharisees, foretold the destruction of the temple and his own return, and told the parables of the ten virgins, the talents, and the sheep and the goats: "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me."

**Matthew 26-27** — Judas agreed to betray Jesus for thirty pieces of silver. At the Passover meal Jesus gave his disciples bread and the cup: "For this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins." In Gethsemane he prayed, "not as I will, but as thou wilt." Judas betrayed him with a kiss, and the disciples fled. Peter denied him three times before the cock crew. The high priest's council condemned him, and Pontius Pilate, though his wife warned him and he washed his hands before the crowd, handed him over to be crucified. Jesus cried, "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" and yielded up the ghost. The veil of the temple was torn in two, and the centurion said, "Truly this was the Son of God." Joseph of Arimathaea laid the body in his own new tomb, which was sealed and guarded.

**Matthew 28** — At dawn on the first day of the week, an angel rolled back the stone and told the women, "He is not here: for he is risen, as he said." The risen Jesus met them on the way, and later met the eleven disciples on a mountain in Galilee. He sent them out: "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost... and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world."

# Mark
When it happened: Jesus' public ministry, from his baptism by John to his resurrection, around AD 27–30.
When it was written: The author is not named in the book. Early church tradition credits John Mark (Acts 12:12), who is said to have recorded the preaching of Peter. Many scholars consider it the earliest Gospel, written in the AD 50s or 60s.
Where: Galilee, especially Capernaum and the Sea of Galilee; the surrounding regions of the Decapolis, Tyre and Sidon, and Caesarea Philippi; Judea; and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas under Rome, and Judea by a Roman governor. Mark explains Jewish customs for his readers (Mark 7:3–4), suggesting many of them were not Jewish.

**Mark 1** — "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God." Mark's account moves quickly. John the Baptist preached in the wilderness and baptized Jesus in the Jordan. After forty days in the wilderness, Jesus came into Galilee preaching, "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe the gospel." He called fishermen to follow him, and straightway he taught in the synagogue at Capernaum, cast out an unclean spirit, and healed Peter's mother-in-law and a leper, and his fame spread throughout the region.

**Mark 2-3** — Four men lowered a paralyzed man through a roof, and Jesus forgave his sins and healed him. Jesus called Levi the tax collector, ate with sinners, and said, "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath." The Pharisees and Herodians began to plot against him. He chose twelve apostles. His own family thought, "He is beside himself," and scribes claimed he cast out devils by Beelzebub.

**Mark 4-5** — Jesus taught in parables, such as the sower and the mustard seed. On the sea he rebuked a storm—"Peace, be still"—and his disciples asked, "What manner of man is this?" Across the sea he freed a man possessed by a legion of devils. A woman who had suffered bleeding for twelve years touched his garment and was healed, and he raised the twelve-year-old daughter of Jairus, saying, "Talitha cumi."

**Mark 6-8** — Jesus was rejected in his hometown. John the Baptist was beheaded by Herod. Jesus fed five thousand, walked on the sea, healed the daughter of a Greek woman, a deaf man, and a blind man, and fed four thousand. At Caesarea Philippi Peter said, "Thou art the Christ," and Jesus began to teach that the Son of man must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and rise again after three days. "For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?"

**Mark 9-10** — Jesus was transfigured on a mountain. He healed a boy whose father cried, "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief," and taught that whoever wants to be first must be the servant of all. He blessed little children, told a rich young man to sell what he had and follow him, and declared, "For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many." Leaving Jericho, he healed blind Bartimaeus.

**Mark 11-13** — Jesus rode into Jerusalem on a colt to shouts of "Hosanna," cleansed the temple, and answered those who challenged his authority. He named the first commandment—to love God with all your heart, soul, mind, and strength—and the second, to love your neighbour as yourself. He praised a poor widow who gave two mites, and on the mount of Olives foretold the destruction of the temple and his coming in the clouds.

**Mark 14-15** — A woman anointed Jesus with costly ointment at Bethany. At the Passover he gave his disciples the bread and the cup. In Gethsemane he prayed, "Abba, Father... take away this cup from me: nevertheless not what I will, but what thou wilt." Judas betrayed him, and the disciples fled. The council condemned him, Peter denied him three times, and Pilate released Barabbas and delivered Jesus to be crucified. Simon of Cyrene was made to carry his cross. Jesus cried out with a loud voice and gave up the ghost, and the centurion said, "Truly this man was the Son of God." Joseph of Arimathaea laid him in a tomb.

**Mark 16** — When the sabbath was past, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome came with spices and found the stone rolled away. A young man in a white garment told them, "he is risen; he is not here." In the longer ending included in the KJV, Jesus appeared to Mary Magdalene and then to the eleven, sent them to "preach the gospel to every creature," and was received up into heaven.

# Luke
When it happened: From the announcement of John the Baptist's birth "in the days of Herod, the king of Judaea" (Luke 1:5) to Jesus' ascension. John's ministry began in the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar (Luke 3:1), around AD 28.
When it was written: The book is addressed to Theophilus (Luke 1:3). Early church tradition credits Luke, "the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14) and Paul's travelling companion; Luke and Acts are two volumes by the same writer (Acts 1:1). It is usually dated to the AD 60s–80s.
Where: The temple in Jerusalem, Nazareth, Bethlehem, Galilee, the long journey toward Jerusalem, Emmaus, and Bethany.
The world at the time: Luke ties his account to Roman history—the census of Caesar Augustus (Luke 2:1), the reign of Tiberius, the governor Pontius Pilate, and Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee.

**Luke 1-2** — Luke wrote to Theophilus an orderly account, having carefully followed "all things from the very first." In the temple, the angel Gabriel told the aged priest Zacharias that his wife Elisabeth would bear a son, John. Six months later Gabriel told Mary of Nazareth that she would conceive and bear the Son of the Highest. She answered, "be it unto me according to thy word," and praised God: "My soul doth magnify the Lord." Because of a decree from Caesar Augustus, Joseph and Mary went to Bethlehem, where Jesus was born and laid in a manger, "because there was no room for them in the inn." Angels announced his birth to shepherds: "Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men." When he was twelve, Jesus was found in the temple, listening to the teachers and asking them questions.

**Luke 3-4** — In the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar, John preached repentance and baptized Jesus. Jesus, about thirty years old, was tempted in the wilderness and then read from Isaiah in the synagogue at Nazareth: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor." His hometown tried to throw him off a cliff, but he went on to Capernaum, healing the sick and casting out devils.

**Luke 5-9** — After a miraculous catch of fish, Peter, James, and John left everything to follow Jesus. He healed the sick, chose twelve apostles, and taught, "Love your enemies, do good to them which hate you." He raised a widow's son at Nain, and a sinful woman washed his feet with her tears. He calmed a storm, fed five thousand, and Peter confessed him as "The Christ of God." After the transfiguration, "he stedfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem."

**Luke 10-19** — On the long journey, Jesus told many parables found only in Luke: the good Samaritan, who showed mercy to a wounded stranger; the rich fool; the lost sheep, the lost coin, and the prodigal son, whose father ran to meet him—"For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found"; the rich man and Lazarus; and the Pharisee and the publican. Jesus visited Martha and Mary, and healed ten lepers, of whom only one, a Samaritan, returned to give thanks. He called the tax collector Zacchaeus down from a sycomore tree: "For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost."

**Luke 19-21** — Jesus wept over Jerusalem, entered the city on a colt, cleansed the temple, answered his challengers, and foretold the city's destruction.

**Luke 22-23** — At the last supper Jesus took bread and said, "This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me." He prayed on the mount of Olives in such agony that "his sweat was as it were great drops of blood." Arrested, he was denied by Peter, tried before the council, Pilate, and Herod, and crucified between two criminals. He prayed, "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do," and told the repentant criminal, "To day shalt thou be with me in paradise." Then he cried, "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit," and died.

**Luke 24** — On the first day of the week, the women found the tomb empty, and two men in shining garments asked them, "Why seek ye the living among the dead?" The risen Jesus walked with two disciples on the road to Emmaus and was known to them in the breaking of bread. He appeared to the eleven, ate fish in front of them, opened their understanding of the Scriptures, and told them to wait in Jerusalem for power from on high. At Bethany he blessed them and was carried up into heaven.

# John
When it happened: Jesus' ministry, which John's Gospel spans across at least three Passovers (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55), around AD 27–30.
When it was written: The book was written by "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (John 21:20–24), traditionally identified as John the son of Zebedee. It is usually dated to around AD 85–95, and early tradition places John in Ephesus.
Where: Judea, Galilee (including Cana), Samaria (at Sychar), Jerusalem—where Jesus attended the feasts at the temple—and Bethany.
The world at the time: As in the other Gospels, Judea was under Roman rule. John pays special attention to the Jewish feasts—Passover, tabernacles, and dedication—and to the conflict between Jesus and the religious leaders in Jerusalem.

**John 1** — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." All things were made by him; in him was life, and the life was the light of men. "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." John the Baptist pointed to Jesus: "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world." Andrew, Simon Peter, Philip, and Nathanael began to follow him.

**John 2-4** — At a wedding in Cana, Jesus turned water into wine, the first of his miracles. He drove the money changers from the temple. The Pharisee Nicodemus came to him by night, and Jesus told him, "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God," and "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." At a well in Samaria, Jesus asked a Samaritan woman for a drink and offered her living water, and many in her town believed. He healed a nobleman's son without going to his house.

**John 5-10** — At the pool of Bethesda Jesus healed a man who had been sick for thirty-eight years. After feeding five thousand, he said, "I am the bread of life." At the feast of tabernacles he cried, "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." When the scribes and Pharisees brought a woman taken in adultery, he said, "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her." He declared, "I am the light of the world," and "Before Abraham was, I am." He gave sight to a man born blind, and called himself the good shepherd who "giveth his life for the sheep."

**John 11-12** — When his friend Lazarus died, Jesus came to Bethany and told Lazarus's sister Martha, "I am the resurrection, and the life." Jesus wept at the grave. Then he cried, "Lazarus, come forth," and the dead man came out. From that day the chief priests and Pharisees plotted to kill him. Mary anointed Jesus' feet, and crowds welcomed him into Jerusalem with palm branches.

**John 13-17** — At supper Jesus washed his disciples' feet and gave them a new commandment, "That ye love one another; as I have loved you." He comforted them: "Let not your heart be troubled... I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." He promised them the Comforter, the Holy Ghost, called himself the true vine, and prayed for his disciples and for all who would believe through their word, "That they all may be one."

**John 18-19** — Jesus was arrested in a garden, questioned by Annas and Caiaphas, and denied by Peter. Pilate asked him, "Art thou the King of the Jews?" and later, "What is truth?" Pilate found no fault in him, yet had him scourged and delivered him to be crucified. From the cross Jesus entrusted his mother to the disciple he loved. He said, "It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost." A soldier pierced his side, and Joseph of Arimathaea and Nicodemus buried him in a new tomb in a garden.

**John 20-21** — Early on the first day of the week, Mary Magdalene found the tomb empty, and the risen Jesus spoke her name. That evening he appeared to the disciples behind shut doors, and a week later he invited Thomas, who had doubted, to touch his wounds. Thomas said, "My Lord and my God." John wrote these things "that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name." By the sea of Tiberias Jesus gave the disciples a great catch of fish, and three times he asked Peter, "lovest thou me?" and told him, "Feed my sheep."

# Acts
When it happened: From Jesus' ascension (around AD 30) to the end of Paul's two years under house arrest in Rome (Acts 28:30), around AD 60–62.
When it was written: Acts is the second volume written to Theophilus (Acts 1:1), by the same author as Luke. Several passages say "we," suggesting the writer travelled with Paul (for example Acts 16:10). It is usually dated to the AD 60s–80s.
Where: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, Damascus, Antioch in Syria, Cyprus, the cities of Asia Minor (including Galatia and Ephesus), Macedonia (Philippi and Thessalonica), Greece (Athens and Corinth), Caesarea, the island of Melita, and Rome.
The world at the time: The Roman Empire's roads, sea routes, and relative peace made travel possible, and Jewish synagogues were found in cities across the empire. Roman officials such as the governors Felix and Festus, and King Agrippa, appear in the story.

**Acts 1-2** — Luke's second book begins where his Gospel ended. For forty days the risen Jesus showed himself alive and told the apostles, "ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth." Then he was taken up, and a cloud received him out of their sight. Matthias was chosen to replace Judas. On the day of Pentecost the Holy Ghost came with a sound like a rushing mighty wind and tongues like fire, and the apostles spoke in other languages. Peter preached that God had raised Jesus and made him "both Lord and Christ," and about three thousand were baptized. The believers shared what they had and met together daily.

**Acts 3-7** — Peter healed a man lame from birth at the temple gate. Arrested and warned, Peter and the apostles answered the council, "We ought to obey God rather than men." Ananias and Sapphira lied about their gift and died. Seven men, including Stephen, were chosen to care for the widows. Stephen was accused of blasphemy, retold Israel's history before the council, saw the heavens opened, and was stoned, praying, "Lord, lay not this sin to their charge." A young man named Saul consented to his death.

**Acts 8-12** — Persecution scattered the believers. Philip preached in Samaria and explained Isaiah to an Ethiopian official, who was baptized. On the road to Damascus, Saul, still breathing out threatenings against the disciples, was struck down by a light from heaven and heard Jesus say, "Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?" Blinded, he was healed by Ananias and began to preach Jesus. Through a vision, Peter learned that God accepts people from every nation, and he baptized the Roman centurion Cornelius and his household. At Antioch "the disciples were called Christians first." King Herod killed James and imprisoned Peter, but an angel led Peter out of prison; Herod later died, eaten of worms.

**Acts 13-14** — The church at Antioch sent out Barnabas and Saul, also called Paul. They preached on Cyprus and in Galatia—first in the synagogues, then to the Gentiles. At Lystra the crowds called them gods after a lame man was healed, and then stoned Paul and left him for dead; he got up and went on.

**Acts 15** — In Jerusalem the apostles and elders met to decide whether Gentile believers had to be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. Peter testified that God had purified the Gentiles' hearts by faith, James spoke, and the council sent a letter laying no greater burden on them than a few necessary things.

**Acts 15-18** — Paul set out again with Silas. At Troas he saw a vision of a man of Macedonia saying, "Come over into Macedonia, and help us." At Philippi Lydia believed, and Paul and Silas were beaten and jailed. At midnight they prayed and sang praises, an earthquake opened the prison doors, and the jailer asked, "what must I do to be saved?" They answered, "Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved." Paul preached in Thessalonica and Berea, spoke to philosophers in Athens about an altar inscribed "TO THE UNKNOWN GOD," and stayed a year and a half in Corinth.

**Acts 18-21** — On his third journey Paul spent about three years at Ephesus, where so many turned from magic that they burned books worth fifty thousand pieces of silver, and silversmiths who made shrines for the goddess Diana started a riot. At Troas a young man named Eutychus fell from a window during Paul's long sermon and was restored to life. Paul said farewell to the elders of Ephesus and went up to Jerusalem, though he was warned he would be bound there.

**Acts 21-26** — In Jerusalem a mob seized Paul in the temple, and Roman soldiers rescued him. Paul told the crowd and the council how he had come to believe. When more than forty men vowed to kill him, he was taken by night to Caesarea. He stood trial before the governors Felix and Festus, appealed to Caesar as a Roman citizen, and told his story to King Agrippa, who said, "Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian."

**Acts 27-28** — On the voyage to Rome, a storm drove the ship for fourteen days. Paul assured the 276 people aboard that none would be lost, and all reached land when the ship broke apart off the island of Melita. There a viper fastened on Paul's hand without harming him, and he healed many who were sick. In Rome Paul lived two whole years in his own rented house, receiving everyone who came to him, "Preaching the kingdom of God, and teaching those things which concern the Lord Jesus Christ, with all confidence, no man forbidding him."
