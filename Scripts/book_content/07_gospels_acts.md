# Matthew
When it happened: From Jesus' birth "in the days of Herod the king" (Matthew 2:1)—before Herod's death in 4 BC—through his ministry, death, and resurrection, around AD 27–30.
When it was written: Most scholars date it between the AD 50s and 80s. It was written for readers steeped in the Jewish Scriptures, often noting that events happened "that it might be fulfilled" (Matthew 1:22).
Where: Bethlehem, Egypt, Nazareth, Galilee (especially Capernaum and the Sea of Galilee), Judea, and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Judea was under Roman rule, governed by Herod the Great and later his sons and Roman governors such as Pontius Pilate. Many Jews longed for the promised Messiah, and the Pharisees and Sadducees were leading religious groups.

**The Promised King Is Born (Matthew 1:1–2:23)** — Matthew traces Jesus' family line to Abraham and David, then describes His birth and the events that bring His family to Nazareth.

Matthew begins with the ancestors of Jesus Christ, tracing His family line through Abraham, the forefather of Israel, and David, the king whose descendants had received God's promise of a lasting kingdom. The record passes through generations of kings, the Babylonian captivity, and finally Joseph, Mary's husband.

Before Joseph and Mary came together as husband and wife, Mary was found to be expecting a child through the Holy Ghost. An angel told Joseph that the child would save His people from their sins. Joseph obeyed, and Jesus was born in Bethlehem during the reign of King Herod.

Wise men from the east followed a star to find the child, bringing gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh. Herod, fearing a rival king, ordered the killing of young boys in Bethlehem. Warned by an angel, Joseph took Mary and Jesus to Egypt. After Herod's death, the family returned and settled in Nazareth.

**Jesus Begins His Public Ministry (Matthew 3:1–4:25)** — John prepares the people for repentance, Jesus is baptized and tested, and His ministry begins with preaching, healing, and the calling of disciples.

Years later, John the Baptist preached in the wilderness of Judea, calling people to repent because the kingdom of heaven was near. He baptized those who confessed their sins and warned religious leaders against relying upon their ancestry instead of producing good fruit.

Jesus came to John for baptism. When He emerged from the water, the Spirit of God descended like a dove, and a voice from heaven identified Him as God's beloved Son.

The Spirit then led Jesus into the wilderness, where He fasted forty days and nights. The devil tempted Him three times, but Jesus answered each temptation with Scripture.

After John's imprisonment, Jesus began preaching in Galilee. He called fishermen named Peter, Andrew, James, and John to follow Him. As He taught in synagogues and healed diseases, large crowds gathered from surrounding regions.

**Jesus Teaches the Way of God's Kingdom (Matthew 5:1–7:29)** — Jesus teaches His followers about righteousness, prayer, mercy, trust in God, and the obedience expected of those who enter His kingdom.

On a mountain, Jesus taught His disciples while crowds listened. He called the humble, merciful, pure in heart, and peacemakers blessed. He taught that His followers should be salt and light, influencing the world through good works.

Jesus explained that He had not come to destroy God's law but to fulfill it. He addressed anger, lust, marriage, honesty, revenge, and love for enemies, showing that righteousness involved the heart as well as outward actions.

He warned against giving, praying, or fasting merely to impress others. He taught His disciples how to pray, urged them to seek God's kingdom first, and encouraged them to trust their heavenly Father for daily needs.

Jesus also warned against hypocritical judgment and false prophets. He ended by comparing those who obeyed His words to a wise builder whose house stood upon rock. The crowds were astonished because He taught with authority.

**Jesus Demonstrates His Authority and Sends His Disciples (Matthew 8:1–10:42)** — Jesus heals the sick, commands nature and evil spirits, forgives sins, and sends twelve apostles to proclaim the kingdom.

After coming down from the mountain, Jesus healed a man with leprosy and a Roman officer's servant. He healed Peter's mother-in-law, calmed a storm, and drove evil spirits from two men.

Jesus also healed a paralyzed man after declaring his sins forgiven. He called Matthew, a tax collector, to follow Him, ate with sinners, and explained that He had come to call sinners to repentance.

He restored a ruler's daughter to life, healed a woman who had suffered bleeding, gave sight to blind men, and enabled a man who could not speak to speak.

Seeing crowds who were distressed and scattered like sheep without a shepherd, Jesus appointed twelve apostles. He gave them authority to heal and cast out evil spirits, sending them first to Israel to announce that the kingdom of heaven was near. He warned of persecution but told them not to fear, promising that God knew and valued them.

**The Kingdom Meets Doubt and Opposition (Matthew 11:1–12:50)** — Questions about Jesus increase as He responds to John, confronts unbelief, and challenges religious leaders who reject His works.

From prison, John sent disciples to ask whether Jesus was the expected one. Jesus pointed to His healings and the good news being preached to the poor. He honored John's role while warning towns that had witnessed mighty works without repenting.

Jesus invited the weary and burdened to come to Him for rest.

Religious leaders then challenged His disciples for picking grain on the Sabbath, the Jewish day of rest. Jesus taught that mercy mattered more than empty religious judgment and healed a man with a withered hand.

When Jesus delivered a man from evil spirits, some Pharisees accused Him of acting through Satan's power. Jesus rejected their accusation, warned about careless words and hardened hearts, and spoke of the sign of Jonah. He explained that those who did His Father's will belonged to His spiritual family.

**Jesus Reveals the Mysteries of the Kingdom (Matthew 13:1–14:36)** — Through parables and miracles, Jesus teaches how God's kingdom grows while showing compassion and power despite rejection.

Beside the sea, Jesus told parables about seeds falling on different soils, weeds growing among wheat, a mustard seed, leaven, hidden treasure, a valuable pearl, and a fishing net.

He explained that people responded differently to God's message and that the righteous and wicked would ultimately be separated.

When Jesus returned to His hometown, many rejected Him because they knew His family. Meanwhile, Herod had John the Baptist executed after a request made during a feast.

After hearing of John's death, Jesus withdrew, but crowds followed. He fed five thousand men, besides women and children, with five loaves and two fish. Later, He walked upon the sea toward His disciples. Peter briefly walked toward Him before becoming afraid and sinking. Jesus rescued him, and the disciples worshiped Jesus as the Son of God.

**Jesus Teaches True Faith and Reveals His Coming Suffering (Matthew 15:1–17:27)** — Jesus exposes false religious traditions, welcomes expressions of faith, and begins preparing His disciples for His death and resurrection.

Jesus taught that evil words and actions came from the heart, not merely from failing to follow ceremonial traditions. He then answered the persistent request of a Canaanite woman and healed her daughter.

After healing many others, He fed four thousand men, besides women and children. When religious leaders demanded another sign, He warned His disciples against their misleading teaching.

Near Caesarea Philippi, Peter declared that Jesus was the Christ, the Son of the living God. Jesus spoke of building His church, then began explaining that He must suffer, be killed, and rise on the third day.

Later, Peter, James, and John saw Jesus transformed in glory upon a mountain, with Moses and Elijah appearing beside Him. A voice from heaven commanded them to listen to Jesus. Afterward, Jesus healed a boy suffering from a demon and again predicted His death and resurrection. He also arranged payment of the temple tax.

**Jesus Teaches Humility, Forgiveness, and Service (Matthew 18:1–20:34)** — Jesus teaches His followers to care for the vulnerable, forgive others, honor marriage, and seek greatness through service rather than status.

When the disciples asked who was greatest in heaven's kingdom, Jesus placed a child among them and taught that true greatness required humility. He warned against causing others to stumble and described a shepherd searching for one lost sheep.

He instructed His followers about addressing wrongdoing within their community and taught forgiveness through a parable about a servant who received mercy but refused to show it to someone else.

Jesus then addressed marriage and divorce, welcomed little children, and challenged a wealthy young man who struggled to surrender his possessions.

Through a parable about workers receiving wages from a vineyard owner, He taught about generosity in the kingdom. He again predicted His death and resurrection and explained that leadership meant serving others. As He left Jericho, He restored sight to two blind men.

**The King Enters Jerusalem and Confronts Its Leaders (Matthew 21:1–23:39)** — Jesus enters Jerusalem, challenges corrupt worship, and warns religious leaders whose outward devotion hides injustice and hypocrisy.

Jesus entered Jerusalem riding a donkey, fulfilling a prophetic picture of a humble king. Crowds welcomed Him, but He soon drove merchants and money changers from the temple, declaring that God's house was meant for prayer.

After cursing a fruitless fig tree, Jesus faced questions about His authority. Through parables about two sons, wicked vineyard tenants, and a wedding feast, He warned of religious leaders who rejected God's call.

His opponents questioned Him about taxes, resurrection, and God's greatest commandment. Jesus answered that people must love God wholeheartedly and love their neighbors as themselves.

He then condemned the hypocrisy of scribes and Pharisees, religious teachers who emphasized outward appearances while neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness. Jesus mourned over Jerusalem's unwillingness to receive those God had sent.

**Jesus Warns of Future Trouble and Calls for Readiness (Matthew 24:1–25:46)** — Jesus foretells coming distress and judgment, urging His followers to remain watchful, faithful, and prepared for His return.

After leaving the temple, Jesus predicted its destruction. His disciples asked about the timing of these events and the sign of His coming and the end of the world.

Jesus warned of false messiahs, wars, persecution, betrayal, and growing wickedness. He described severe trouble, heavenly signs, and the coming of the Son of man in glory. He urged vigilance because the exact day and hour were unknown.

He compared readiness to faithful servants carrying out their duties and to ten virgins waiting for a bridegroom. Another parable described servants entrusted with their master's resources and held accountable upon his return.

Finally, Jesus pictured the Son of man judging the nations, separating people like a shepherd separating sheep from goats. Their treatment of the hungry, thirsty, strangers, sick, and imprisoned revealed the importance of mercy and compassion.

**Jesus Is Betrayed, Arrested, and Condemned (Matthew 26:1–27:26)** — As religious leaders plot His death, Jesus shares a final meal with His disciples, is betrayed, and stands before Jewish and Roman authorities.

Jesus warned His disciples that He would soon be crucified. Religious leaders plotted against Him, while a woman anointed Him with costly ointment. Judas Iscariot, one of the twelve disciples, agreed to betray Him for thirty pieces of silver.

During the Passover meal, Jesus identified His coming betrayal and gave His disciples bread and wine, speaking of His body and the blood of the new covenant, shed for the forgiveness of sins.

In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed in deep sorrow while His disciples struggled to remain awake. Judas arrived with an armed crowd, and Jesus was arrested.

Before the high priest and council, Jesus faced accusations and was condemned for blasphemy. Peter denied knowing Him three times.

Judas later regretted his betrayal and returned the money before taking his own life. Jesus was brought before Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor. Although Pilate questioned the accusations, the crowd demanded Jesus' crucifixion and the release of Barabbas, a prisoner.

**Jesus Dies and Rises from the Dead (Matthew 27:27–28:15)** — Jesus is crucified and buried, but on the third day His tomb is found empty, and His resurrection is announced.

Roman soldiers mocked Jesus, placed a crown of thorns upon Him, and led Him away to be crucified. At Golgotha, He was nailed to a cross between two criminals.

Darkness covered the land for three hours. Jesus cried out and died. The temple veil was torn from top to bottom, the earth shook, and tombs opened. A Roman centurion and those with him declared that Jesus was the Son of God.

Joseph of Arimathaea, a disciple of Jesus, placed His body in a new tomb, while women watched. Religious leaders arranged for guards to secure the burial place.

After the Sabbath, Mary Magdalene and the other Mary came to the tomb. An angel announced that Jesus had risen, and the women hurried to tell the disciples. Jesus met them on their way, and they worshiped Him.

Meanwhile, the guards reported what had happened. The religious leaders paid them to claim that the disciples had stolen Jesus' body while they slept.

**Jesus Sends His Disciples to All Nations (Matthew 28:16–20)** — The risen Jesus meets His disciples in Galilee and commands them to make disciples throughout the world, promising His continuing presence.

The eleven remaining disciples traveled to a mountain in Galilee where Jesus had directed them to meet Him. When they saw Him, they worshiped, although some doubted.

Jesus declared that all authority in heaven and on earth had been given to Him. He commanded His disciples to go to all nations, make disciples, baptize them in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, and teach them to obey everything He had commanded.

He ended with a promise that He would remain with them always, even to the end of the world.

---

Matthew begins by tracing Jesus to Abraham and David, placing Him within God's promises to Israel. It closes with the risen Jesus claiming authority over heaven and earth and extending His mission to every nation. The One introduced as Emmanuel, meaning God with us, sends His followers into the world with the assurance that His presence will continue with them.

# Mark
When it happened: Jesus' public ministry, from his baptism by John to his resurrection, around AD 27–30.
When it was written: Early church tradition says Mark recorded the preaching of Peter. Many scholars consider it the earliest Gospel, written in the AD 50s or 60s.
Where: Galilee, especially Capernaum and the Sea of Galilee; the surrounding regions of the Decapolis, Tyre and Sidon, and Caesarea Philippi; Judea; and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas under Rome, and Judea by a Roman governor. Mark explains Jewish customs for his readers (Mark 7:3–4), suggesting many of them were not Jewish.

**Jesus Begins His Ministry in Galilee (Mark 1:1–45)** — John the Baptist prepares the way for Jesus, who begins proclaiming God's kingdom and demonstrates His authority through teaching and healing.

Mark begins with John the Baptist preaching in the wilderness, calling people to repent and be baptized. He announces that someone greater is coming.

Jesus arrives from Nazareth and is baptized in the Jordan River. As He rises from the water, the Spirit descends like a dove, and a voice from heaven identifies Him as God's beloved Son.

The Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness, where Satan tempts Him for forty days. After John's imprisonment, Jesus begins preaching in Galilee, urging people to repent and believe the gospel, the good news of God's kingdom.

He calls four fishermen—Simon Peter, Andrew, James, and John—to follow Him. In Capernaum, Jesus teaches with authority, drives out an evil spirit, and heals Simon's mother-in-law. Crowds bring their sick, but Jesus also withdraws to pray. He continues preaching throughout Galilee and heals a man with leprosy who approaches Him for help.

**Jesus Confronts Opposition and Calls Twelve Apostles (Mark 2:1–3:35)** — Jesus challenges religious expectations, forgives sins, and appoints twelve apostles as opposition to His ministry grows.

When friends lower a paralyzed man through a roof, Jesus first declares his sins forgiven. Some religious teachers consider this blasphemy, but Jesus heals the man, demonstrating His authority to forgive.

He calls Levi, a tax collector, and eats with tax collectors and sinners. When questioned, Jesus explains that He came to call sinners to repentance. He also answers questions about fasting and the Sabbath, the Jewish day of rest.

After healing a man's withered hand on the Sabbath, Jesus faces growing hostility from Pharisees and supporters of Herod.

Crowds continue gathering, and Jesus appoints twelve apostles to accompany Him, preach, and cast out evil spirits.

Some religious teachers accuse Him of using Satan's power. Jesus rejects the accusation, explaining that Satan would not destroy his own kingdom. When His family seeks Him, Jesus teaches that those who do God's will belong to His spiritual family.

**Jesus Reveals the Kingdom Through Parables and Miracles (Mark 4:1–5:43)** — Jesus teaches how God's kingdom grows and demonstrates authority over storms, evil spirits, sickness, and death.

Beside the Sea of Galilee, Jesus teaches through parables, which are comparisons drawn from familiar life. He describes seed falling on different soils, a lamp meant to give light, seed growing without the farmer understanding how, and a mustard seed becoming a large plant.

Later, a violent storm threatens the disciples' boat. Jesus commands the wind and sea to become calm, leaving His disciples amazed.

Across the water, He frees a man possessed by many evil spirits. The spirits enter a herd of pigs, which rush into the sea. The restored man is sent to tell others what the Lord has done for him.

Returning across the sea, Jesus meets Jairus, a synagogue ruler whose daughter is dying. Along the way, a woman suffering from years of bleeding touches Jesus' garment and is healed.

When news arrives that Jairus's daughter has died, Jesus continues to the house and raises her to life.

**The Disciples Witness Jesus' Power (Mark 6:1–7:37)** — Jesus sends His disciples to preach, feeds thousands, walks upon the sea, and teaches that true uncleanness comes from within.

In Nazareth, many reject Jesus because they know His family. He then sends the twelve disciples in pairs to preach repentance, heal the sick, and confront evil spirits.

King Herod hears about Jesus and recalls how he had ordered John the Baptist's execution following a request made during a banquet.

When the apostles return, Jesus leads them toward a quiet place, but a crowd follows. Moved with compassion, He teaches the people and feeds five thousand men with five loaves and two fish.

Later, Jesus walks upon the sea toward His struggling disciples.

Religious leaders criticize His followers for eating without traditional handwashing. Jesus explains that evil thoughts and actions arising from the heart make a person unclean.

He then travels beyond Galilee, where He delivers a Syrophoenician woman's daughter from an evil spirit and heals a man who is deaf and has difficulty speaking.

**Peter Recognizes Jesus as the Christ (Mark 8:1–9:50)** — Jesus challenges His disciples' understanding, reveals His coming suffering, and teaches that following Him requires sacrifice.

After feeding four thousand people, Jesus refuses the Pharisees' demand for a heavenly sign. He warns His disciples against their misleading influence, though they initially misunderstand Him.

At Bethsaida, He restores a blind man's sight in stages.

Near Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks His disciples who they believe He is. Peter declares that He is the Christ, meaning God's Anointed One.

Jesus then begins teaching that He must suffer, be rejected, die, and rise again. Peter objects, but Jesus rebukes him and explains that following Him requires self-denial and taking up one's cross.

Later, Peter, James, and John see Jesus transformed in glory upon a mountain. Moses and Elijah appear, and a voice from heaven commands them to listen to Jesus.

Afterward, Jesus delivers a boy from an evil spirit, again predicts His death, and teaches His disciples to welcome the humble rather than compete for greatness. He warns them seriously against causing others to stumble into sin.

**Jesus Teaches About Marriage, Wealth, and Service (Mark 10:1–52)** — Jesus teaches about faithful relationships, welcomes children, challenges attachment to wealth, and shows that greatness comes through serving others.

As Jesus continues toward Jerusalem, religious leaders question Him about divorce. He directs them to God's design for marriage and teaches that husband and wife are joined together by God.

When people bring children to Him, His disciples try to stop them. Jesus welcomes the children and teaches that God's kingdom must be received with childlike trust.

A wealthy man asks about inheriting eternal life. Jesus tells him to sell his possessions, give to the poor, and follow Him. The man leaves sorrowful because he owns much.

Jesus teaches that salvation is possible through God, even when it seems impossible for people.

He again predicts His death and resurrection. When James and John seek honored positions, Jesus explains that true greatness comes through service. He declares that the Son of man came to serve and give His life as a ransom for many.

Near Jericho, He restores sight to Bartimaeus, a blind beggar who calls upon Him for mercy.

**Jesus Enters Jerusalem and Challenges Its Leaders (Mark 11:1–12:44)** — Jesus enters Jerusalem as the promised king, confronts corruption in the temple, and exposes the failures of religious leadership.

Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a young donkey while crowds spread garments and branches along the road. They welcome Him with praise.

The following day, Jesus curses a fruitless fig tree and drives merchants and money changers from the temple, declaring that God's house is meant for prayer.

The religious leaders challenge His authority. Jesus responds with a parable about vineyard tenants who mistreat their owner's servants and kill his beloved son.

His opponents then question Him about paying taxes to Caesar, the resurrection, and the greatest commandment. Jesus teaches that people must love God completely and love their neighbors as themselves.

He also questions their understanding of the Messiah's relationship to David, Israel's former king, and warns against religious teachers who seek honor while exploiting widows.

Watching people give offerings, Jesus praises a poor widow who contributes two small coins—everything she has to live on.

**Jesus Warns of Coming Trouble and His Return (Mark 13:1–37)** — Jesus predicts the temple's destruction, warns of persecution and deception, and commands His followers to remain watchful for His coming.

As Jesus leaves the temple, a disciple admires its impressive buildings. Jesus announces that the structures will be destroyed.

Later, on the Mount of Olives, Peter, James, John, and Andrew privately ask when these events will happen.

Jesus warns of false messiahs, wars, earthquakes, famines, and persecution. His followers will face opposition, but the gospel must first be preached among all nations.

He describes a time of severe distress, warns people to flee when danger appears, and cautions against those falsely claiming to be the Christ.

Jesus then describes heavenly signs and the Son of man's coming in clouds with great power and glory. He will gather His chosen people.

Using the fig tree as an example, Jesus teaches His followers to recognize approaching events. Yet He declares that no one knows the exact day or hour of His coming, so they must remain watchful.

**Jesus Is Betrayed and Arrested (Mark 14:1–72)** — Religious leaders arrange Jesus' death, Judas betrays Him, and Jesus is arrested after sharing a final meal with His disciples.

As Passover approaches, religious leaders seek a way to kill Jesus. At Bethany, a woman pours expensive ointment over His head. Jesus defends her action as preparation for His burial.

Judas Iscariot, one of the twelve, agrees to betray Jesus.

During the Passover meal, Jesus announces that one of His disciples will betray Him. He shares bread and wine, speaking of His body and the blood of the new testament, shed for many.

At Gethsemane, Jesus prays in deep distress, submitting Himself to His Father's will while His disciples repeatedly fall asleep.

Judas arrives with an armed crowd and identifies Jesus with a kiss. Jesus is arrested, and His disciples flee.

Before the high priest and council, Jesus faces conflicting testimony. When questioned about being the Christ, He affirms His identity and speaks of the Son of man's future glory. The council condemns Him.

Meanwhile, Peter denies knowing Jesus three times. When the rooster crows a second time, he remembers Jesus' warning and weeps.

**Jesus Is Condemned and Crucified (Mark 15:1–47)** — Jesus faces Roman judgment, suffers crucifixion, and is buried while His followers witness the events surrounding His death.

The religious leaders bring Jesus before Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor. Pilate questions Him but eventually releases Barabbas, a prisoner involved in rebellion, after the crowd demands it.

Jesus is handed over to be crucified.

Roman soldiers mock Him, place a crown of thorns upon His head, and dress Him in purple. They lead Him to Golgotha, where Simon of Cyrene is made to carry His cross.

Jesus is crucified between two criminals. Passersby and religious leaders mock Him, challenging Him to save Himself.

Darkness covers the land from the sixth hour until the ninth. Jesus cries out and dies.

At His death, the curtain of the temple is torn from top to bottom. A Roman centurion standing nearby declares that Jesus truly was the Son of God.

Women who had followed Jesus watch from a distance, including Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of James the less and Joses.

Joseph of Arimathaea, a respected council member waiting for God's kingdom, requests Jesus' body. He wraps it in linen and places it in a tomb cut from rock, closing the entrance with a large stone.

**The Tomb Is Empty and Jesus Is Risen (Mark 16:1–20)** — Women discover Jesus' empty tomb, and Mark's closing passage describes resurrection appearances and the disciples' mission to proclaim the gospel.

After the Sabbath, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome bring spices to anoint Jesus' body.

When they reach the tomb, they find the stone rolled away. Inside, a young man dressed in white tells them that Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified, has risen.

He directs them to tell the disciples and Peter that Jesus will go before them into Galilee. The women flee trembling and amazed, initially saying nothing to anyone because they are afraid.

The traditional longer ending of Mark, preserved in the King James Version, continues with accounts of the risen Jesus appearing first to Mary Magdalene, then to two followers traveling into the countryside, and later to the eleven disciples.

Jesus rebukes their unbelief and commands them to preach the gospel throughout the world. He speaks of signs accompanying believers and the importance of faith.

The passage then describes Jesus being received into heaven and sitting at God's right hand. The disciples go out preaching, with the Lord working through them.

---

Mark's account begins with Jesus announcing God's kingdom and demonstrating His authority through teaching, healing, and deliverance. It follows Him through growing opposition, His willing suffering, and His death upon the cross.

The final chapter announces that the crucified Jesus has risen. The mission that began in Galilee extends outward through His followers, who are sent to proclaim the good news.

# Luke
When it happened: From the announcement of John the Baptist's birth "in the days of Herod, the king of Judaea" (Luke 1:5) to Jesus' ascension. John's ministry began in the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar (Luke 3:1), around AD 28.
When it was written: The book is addressed to Theophilus, and its writer explains that he carefully followed the accounts of eyewitnesses (Luke 1:1–4). Luke and Acts are two volumes by the same writer (Acts 1:1). It is usually dated to the AD 60s–80s.
Where: The temple in Jerusalem, Nazareth, Bethlehem, Galilee, the long journey toward Jerusalem, Emmaus, and Bethany.
The world at the time: Luke ties his account to Roman history—the census of Caesar Augustus (Luke 2:1), the reign of Tiberius, the governor Pontius Pilate, and Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee.

**God Announces the Births of John and Jesus (Luke 1:1–80)** — Luke begins his orderly account with angelic announcements, miraculous pregnancies, and promises that God is preparing salvation for His people.

Luke writes to Theophilus, explaining that he has carefully investigated the events surrounding Jesus so his reader can know their certainty.

During the reign of Herod, king of Judea, an angel tells Zechariah, an elderly priest, that his wife Elizabeth will bear a son named John. John will prepare people for the Lord. Zechariah doubts the announcement and becomes unable to speak.

Six months later, the angel Gabriel visits Mary, a young woman engaged to Joseph, a descendant of King David. Gabriel announces that she will conceive Jesus through the Holy Ghost. Her son will be called the Son of God and inherit David's throne.

Mary visits Elizabeth, who recognizes the significance of Mary's child. Mary praises God for His mercy and faithfulness.

When John is born, Zechariah's speech returns. He praises God for remembering His promises and declares that John will prepare the Lord's way.

**Jesus Is Born and Grows in Wisdom (Luke 2:1–52)** — Jesus is born in Bethlehem, welcomed by shepherds, recognized at the temple, and later found discussing God's word with teachers.

A decree from Caesar Augustus leads Joseph and Mary from Nazareth to Bethlehem, David's city. There Mary gives birth to Jesus, wraps Him in swaddling clothes, and lays Him in a manger because there is no room in the lodging place.

Angels announce His birth to nearby shepherds, calling Him the Saviour, Christ the Lord. The shepherds visit and spread the news.

Joseph and Mary later bring Jesus to the temple in Jerusalem. Simeon, a righteous man awaiting Israel's comfort, recognizes Him as God's salvation. Anna, an elderly prophetess, also gives thanks and speaks about Him.

The family returns to Nazareth. At twelve years old, Jesus remains in Jerusalem after Passover. His parents find Him in the temple listening to teachers and asking questions. He returns home with them, growing in wisdom and favor.

**Jesus Prepares for His Ministry (Luke 3:1–4:44)** — John calls Israel to repentance, Jesus is baptized and tested, and His preaching and miracles begin attracting attention.

John the Baptist preaches near the Jordan River, calling people to repent and show changed lives through generosity, honesty, and justice. He announces that someone greater is coming.

Jesus is baptized, and the Holy Ghost descends upon Him while a voice from heaven identifies Him as God's beloved Son. Luke then traces Jesus' ancestry backward through David and Abraham to Adam.

After forty days of temptation in the wilderness, Jesus returns to Galilee. In Nazareth, He reads from Isaiah and announces that the Scripture concerning good news for the poor and freedom for the oppressed is fulfilled. The people reject Him.

In Capernaum, Jesus teaches with authority, drives out an evil spirit, heals Simon Peter's mother-in-law, and cures many others. He continues preaching because announcing God's kingdom is central to His mission.

**Jesus Calls Disciples and Teaches a New Way (Luke 5:1–6:49)** — Jesus gathers followers, forgives sins, confronts religious objections, and teaches a life marked by mercy, humility, and obedience.

After a remarkable catch of fish, Simon Peter, James, and John leave their work to follow Jesus.

Jesus heals a man with leprosy and a paralyzed man, declaring the latter's sins forgiven. He calls Levi, a tax collector, and eats with people whom religious leaders consider sinners.

Disputes arise over fasting and the Sabbath. Jesus teaches that doing good and saving life matter more than rigid interpretations of religious rules.

After praying through the night, He chooses twelve apostles. Speaking to His followers, He blesses the poor and suffering while warning those satisfied with wealth and praise.

He commands love for enemies, generosity, forgiveness, and freedom from hypocritical judgment. He compares obedient listeners to builders who lay deep foundations upon rock.

**Jesus Shows Compassion and Reveals His Identity (Luke 7:1–9:50)** — Jesus heals, forgives, teaches through parables, and reveals His power while His disciples gradually recognize Him as the Christ.

Jesus heals a Roman officer's servant and raises a widow's only son in Nain. When John the Baptist sends questions about Him, Jesus points to His works of healing and preaching.

At a Pharisee's house, a woman known as a sinner washes Jesus' feet with tears. Jesus teaches that her great love reflects the forgiveness she has received.

Women, including Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Susanna, help support His ministry. Jesus tells the parable of the sower and explains how people receive God's word.

He calms a storm, frees a man from many demons, heals a suffering woman, and raises Jairus's daughter.

Jesus sends the twelve to preach and heal, later feeding about five thousand men with five loaves and two fish.

Peter identifies Him as the Christ. Jesus then predicts His suffering and calls His followers to deny themselves. On a mountain, Peter, James, and John see Jesus in glory with Moses and Elijah. Afterward, Jesus heals a boy and teaches humility.

**Jesus Begins His Journey Toward Jerusalem (Luke 9:51–11:54)** — As Jesus travels toward Jerusalem, He teaches the cost of discipleship, compassion for neighbors, prayer, and the danger of religious hypocrisy.

Jesus sets His face toward Jerusalem, knowing what awaits Him. A Samaritan village refuses to receive Him, but He rebukes His disciples when they propose bringing judgment upon it.

He sends seventy followers ahead to announce God's kingdom. Returning with joy, they hear Him emphasize the greater blessing of belonging to God.

Jesus tells of a Samaritan who helps an injured stranger when others pass by, teaching what it means to love one's neighbor.

At the home of Martha and Mary, He commends Mary's attention to His teaching. He later teaches His disciples to pray and encourages them to ask their heavenly Father for what they need.

As opposition grows, Jesus answers accusations about His power over evil spirits. He warns against spiritual emptiness, demands for signs, outward religious show, and leaders who burden others while neglecting justice and God's love.

**Jesus Warns Against Greed and Calls for Readiness (Luke 12:1–13:35)** — Jesus urges trust in God, repentance, and watchfulness while warning that wealth and outward religion cannot provide lasting security.

Jesus warns against hypocrisy and teaches His followers not to fear those who can harm the body. He tells of a rich man who stores up possessions but dies before enjoying them.

He urges His followers to seek God's kingdom rather than be ruled by anxiety over food and clothing. Through examples of watchful servants, He calls them to remain ready.

Jesus also warns that allegiance to Him may divide households.

When people mention tragedies involving Galileans and a fallen tower, Jesus rejects the idea that the victims were uniquely sinful. Instead, He calls everyone to repentance.

He tells of a fruitless fig tree given more time and heals a woman bent over for eighteen years.

Comparing God's kingdom to a mustard seed and leaven, He describes its growth. He also warns that entering through the narrow door requires more than claiming familiarity with Him. The section closes with His sorrow over Jerusalem's resistance to God's messengers.

**God Seeks the Lost and Welcomes the Repentant (Luke 14:1–16:31)** — Through meals and parables, Jesus confronts pride and selfishness while revealing God's joy over repentance and the responsibility that comes with wealth.

At a Pharisee's house, Jesus heals a man on the Sabbath and teaches guests to choose humble places. He tells of a great supper whose invited guests make excuses, leading the host to welcome the poor and neglected.

Jesus warns large crowds to consider the cost of following Him.

When religious leaders criticize His welcome of sinners, Jesus tells three parables: a shepherd recovering a lost sheep, a woman finding a lost coin, and a father receiving his returning son.

He then teaches about responsible stewardship through the example of a dishonest manager. He warns that no one can serve both God and money.

Finally, Jesus tells of a wealthy man who ignores a poor beggar named Lazarus. After death, their conditions are reversed, and the wealthy man learns that the warnings already given through Scripture were sufficient.

**Faith, Humility, and Salvation on the Road (Luke 17:1–19:27)** — Jesus teaches forgiveness, gratitude, prayer, and humility while bringing restoration to people who are overlooked or rejected.

Jesus instructs His disciples to forgive repeatedly and serve faithfully. He heals ten men with leprosy, but only one, a Samaritan, returns to thank Him.

Asked about God's kingdom, Jesus explains that it is already among them and warns of a future day when the Son of man will be revealed.

He teaches persistence through a widow seeking justice and humility through a Pharisee and a tax collector praying at the temple.

Jesus welcomes children, then challenges a wealthy ruler whose possessions keep him from following fully.

Near Jericho, He heals a blind beggar and meets Zacchaeus, a wealthy tax collector who responds by promising restitution and generosity.

Jesus declares that the Son of man came to seek and save the lost. He then tells a parable about servants entrusted with money, emphasizing responsibility during their master's absence.

**Jesus Enters Jerusalem and Faces Opposition (Luke 19:28–21:38)** — Jesus enters Jerusalem, confronts corrupt worship, answers His opponents, and warns of coming destruction and the need for watchfulness.

Approaching Jerusalem, Jesus rides a young donkey while His followers praise God. He weeps over the city, foretelling destruction because it has not recognized the time of its visitation.

He drives sellers from the temple and begins teaching there daily.

Religious leaders challenge His authority. Jesus tells of vineyard tenants who reject their owner's servants and kill his son. He answers questions about Roman taxes and resurrection, then warns against religious teachers who exploit widows.

He notices a poor widow giving two small coins and honors her sacrifice.

When disciples admire the temple, Jesus predicts its destruction. He warns of wars, persecution, Jerusalem surrounded by armies, distress among nations, and the coming of the Son of man.

He calls His followers to remain alert and prayerful rather than becoming weighed down by life's cares.

**Jesus Shares His Final Meal and Is Arrested (Luke 22:1–71)** — As Passover approaches, Jesus prepares His disciples for His suffering, institutes the Lord's Supper, and is betrayed and arrested.

Religious leaders seek Jesus' death, and Judas Iscariot agrees to betray Him.

At the Passover meal, Jesus shares bread and a cup with His apostles, connecting them with His body and the new covenant in His blood. He teaches that greatness among His followers means serving others.

Jesus warns Peter that he will deny Him, then goes to the Mount of Olives to pray. He submits Himself to His Father's will while His disciples struggle with sorrow and sleep.

Judas arrives with a crowd and betrays Jesus with a kiss. When one disciple strikes the high priest's servant, Jesus heals the injured man's ear.

Jesus is taken away. Peter denies knowing Him three times and weeps after the rooster crows. Jesus is mocked and questioned by the religious council, where He affirms His identity as the Son of God.

**Jesus Is Condemned, Crucified, and Buried (Luke 23:1–56)** — Jesus is sentenced under Roman authority, forgives His enemies, promises paradise to a condemned man, and dies upon the cross.

Jesus stands before Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor, who finds no guilt deserving death. Pilate sends Him to Herod Antipas, ruler of Galilee, who mocks Him before returning Him.

Despite Pilate's efforts to release Jesus, the crowd demands the release of Barabbas and Jesus' crucifixion.

On the way to execution, Simon of Cyrene carries the cross behind Jesus. Jesus warns mourning women about coming suffering.

At the cross, Jesus prays for those responsible for His execution. One criminal mocks Him, while another asks to be remembered. Jesus promises the repentant man a place with Him in paradise.

Darkness covers the land, the temple veil is torn, and Jesus commits His spirit to the Father before dying.

Joseph of Arimathaea places His body in a tomb. Women observe the burial, prepare spices, and rest on the Sabbath.

**Jesus Rises and Commissions His Followers (Luke 24:1–53)** — The empty tomb, appearances of the risen Jesus, and His ascension bring Luke's account to its conclusion.

Early on the first day of the week, women visit Jesus' tomb and find the stone rolled away. Two men in shining garments announce that He has risen. The women tell the apostles, but their report is initially dismissed.

Two followers traveling to Emmaus encounter Jesus without recognizing Him. He explains the Scriptures concerning His suffering and glory. They finally recognize Him when He breaks bread, and He disappears from their sight.

Jesus then appears among the gathered disciples. He shows them His hands and feet and eats fish before them, confirming that He is alive.

He opens their understanding of the Scriptures, explaining that the Christ had to suffer and rise on the third day. Repentance and forgiveness of sins are to be preached in His name among all nations, beginning at Jerusalem.

Jesus promises the power of the Father and instructs His followers to remain in Jerusalem until they receive it. He leads them toward Bethany, blesses them, and is carried into heaven.

The disciples return to Jerusalem with great joy, continuing to praise God in the temple. Luke's account ends with the risen Jesus exalted and His followers awaiting the promised power that will carry His message beyond Jerusalem.

# John
When it happened: Jesus' ministry, which John's Gospel spans across at least three Passovers (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55), around AD 27–30.
When it was written: It is usually dated to around AD 85–95, likely the last of the four Gospels, and early tradition places its writing in Ephesus. It states its own purpose: "that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ" (John 20:31).
Where: Judea, Galilee (including Cana), Samaria (at Sychar), Jerusalem—where Jesus attended the feasts at the temple—and Bethany.
The world at the time: As in the other Gospels, Judea was under Roman rule. John pays special attention to the Jewish feasts—Passover, tabernacles, and dedication—and to the conflict between Jesus and the religious leaders in Jerusalem.

**The Word Becomes Flesh (John 1:1–51)** — John introduces Jesus as the eternal Word who became human, while John the Baptist identifies Him and His first disciples begin following Him.

Before creation, the Word already existed with God and was God. Through Him all things were made. He was the source of life and light, yet the world did not recognize Him when He came. The Word became flesh and lived among humanity, revealing God's glory, grace, and truth.

John the Baptist testified that Jesus was the Lamb of God who takes away the world's sin. John was not the promised Christ, or Messiah, but had come to prepare people for Him.

Andrew and another disciple began following Jesus. Andrew brought his brother Simon, whom Jesus called Cephas, or Peter. Philip and Nathanael also joined Him, beginning the gathering of His disciples.

**Jesus Reveals His Authority and Offers New Life (John 2:1–3:36)** — Jesus performs His first sign, confronts improper temple practices, and teaches that eternal life comes through believing in God's Son.

At a wedding in Cana, Jesus turned water into wine, revealing His glory to His disciples.

Later, during Passover in Jerusalem, He drove merchants and money changers from the temple. When challenged, He spoke of raising the temple in three days, referring to His body and future resurrection.

A Pharisee named Nicodemus, a Jewish religious leader, visited Jesus at night. Jesus explained that a person must be born again through the Spirit to enter God's kingdom.

He also taught that God loved the world and gave His only begotten Son so that those who believe in Him might have everlasting life.

Meanwhile, John the Baptist continued his ministry. When his followers noticed more people going to Jesus, John gladly affirmed that Jesus must increase while he himself must decrease.

**Jesus Offers Living Water and Reveals His Power (John 4:1–5:47)** — Jesus reveals God's salvation to a Samaritan woman, heals a child, and declares His authority to give life and execute judgment.

Traveling through Samaria, Jesus stopped at a well and spoke with a woman who came to draw water. Although Jews and Samaritans had longstanding divisions, Jesus offered her living water, describing the eternal life He gives.

He revealed that He was the promised Messiah. The woman told her neighbors, and many Samaritans came to believe in Him.

Returning to Galilee, Jesus healed a royal official's son from a distance.

Later, in Jerusalem, He healed a man who had been unable to walk for thirty-eight years. Because the healing occurred on the Sabbath, religious leaders challenged Him.

Jesus answered that He worked according to His Father's will. He declared that the Father had given Him authority to raise the dead and judge humanity, and that His works, the Scriptures, and other witnesses testified to His identity.

**Jesus Feeds Thousands and Calls Himself the Bread of Life (John 6:1–71)** — Jesus feeds a vast crowd, walks upon the sea, and teaches that He alone gives the lasting life people truly need.

Near the Sea of Galilee, Jesus fed about five thousand men using five barley loaves and two small fish. The people wanted to make Him king, but He withdrew.

That night, His disciples struggled across the sea in strong winds. Jesus came toward them walking on the water, and they received Him into their boat.

The next day, crowds searched for Him. Jesus challenged their focus on ordinary food and urged them to seek what lasts for eternal life.

Calling Himself the bread of life, He explained that those who come to Him and believe will receive life. He also spoke of giving His flesh for the life of the world, words that many listeners found difficult.

Several disciples stopped following Him. When Jesus asked the twelve whether they also would leave, Peter affirmed that Jesus had the words of eternal life.

**Jesus Confronts Unbelief and Offers Living Water (John 7:1–8:59)** — During a Jewish festival, Jesus teaches publicly in Jerusalem, faces divided opinions, and declares that He is the light of the world.

Jesus traveled to Jerusalem for the Feast of Tabernacles, a festival remembering God's care for Israel in the wilderness. His teaching divided the crowds, with some believing and others questioning His identity.

On the festival's final day, He invited the thirsty to come to Him and drink, speaking of the Spirit believers would receive.

Religious leaders sought to arrest Him, but their officers returned without doing so.

In the account of a woman accused of adultery, Jesus challenged her accusers to consider their own sin before condemning her. He then told the woman to leave her sinful conduct. This passage has a complex manuscript history, though it is included in the King James Version.

Jesus declared Himself the light of the world and taught that His truth brings freedom. His opponents appealed to their descent from Abraham, Israel's great ancestor, but Jesus explained that true belonging to God is shown through receiving His word.

He finally declared that He existed before Abraham. His listeners attempted to stone Him, but He departed.

**Jesus Opens Blind Eyes and Calls His Sheep (John 9:1–10:42)** — Jesus gives sight to a man born blind and teaches that He is the good shepherd who knows and protects His people.

Jesus healed a man blind from birth by placing clay on his eyes and sending him to wash in the pool of Siloam.

Religious leaders questioned the man repeatedly because the healing occurred on the Sabbath. When he defended Jesus, they expelled him from their assembly. Jesus later found him, and the man believed and worshiped Him.

Jesus then described Himself as the door of the sheep and the good shepherd. Unlike a hired worker who abandons the flock, He willingly lays down His life for His sheep. He also spoke of bringing other sheep into one flock.

During the Feast of Dedication in Jerusalem, opponents demanded that He state plainly whether He was the Christ.

Jesus pointed to His works and declared His unity with the Father. Some attempted to seize Him, but He escaped and continued His ministry beyond the Jordan.

**Jesus Raises Lazarus and Approaches His Final Hour (John 11:1–12:50)** — Jesus raises Lazarus from the dead, leading many to believe while religious leaders decide that Jesus must be killed.

Jesus received news that Lazarus, the brother of Mary and Martha, was sick. By the time Jesus reached Bethany, Lazarus had been dead four days.

Martha expressed confidence that God would hear Jesus. He told her that He was the resurrection and the life.

At the tomb, Jesus wept. He then called Lazarus out, and the dead man emerged alive.

Many believed, but the miracle increased opposition. The high priest Caiaphas and other leaders feared the consequences of Jesus' growing influence and planned His death.

Before Passover, Mary anointed Jesus' feet with costly ointment. Jesus defended her act in connection with His burial.

Jesus entered Jerusalem riding a young donkey while crowds welcomed Him. Some Greeks also sought Him, and He declared that His hour had come. Speaking of a grain of wheat dying to bear fruit, He pointed toward His approaching death.

**Jesus Serves His Disciples and Promises the Spirit (John 13:1–14:31)** — During His final evening with the disciples, Jesus demonstrates humble service, announces His betrayal, and promises continuing help from the Father.

Jesus washed His disciples' feet, showing them an example of humble service.

During the meal, He identified Judas Iscariot as the one who would betray Him. After Judas departed, Jesus gave His followers a new commandment: to love one another as He had loved them.

He also warned Peter that he would deny Him three times.

Jesus comforted His troubled disciples with the promise of His Father's house. He declared that He was the way, the truth, and the life, and that no one came to the Father except through Him.

He promised that the Father would send the Holy Ghost, the Comforter, to teach them and remind them of His words.

Although He was preparing to leave, Jesus assured them of His peace and called them to remain faithful.

**Jesus Prepares His Followers for What Comes Next (John 15:1–17:26)** — Jesus teaches His disciples to remain in Him, warns of persecution, and prays for their protection and unity.

Jesus compared Himself to the true vine and His followers to branches. Just as branches depend upon a vine for life, His disciples must remain in Him to bear fruit.

He commanded them to love one another and explained that the world might hate them because it had hated Him first.

Jesus promised that the Spirit of truth would testify about Him and guide the disciples. He prepared them for sorrow but assured them that their grief would turn to joy.

He also taught them to pray in His name and promised peace despite trouble in the world.

Jesus then prayed to the Father. He spoke of completing the work given to Him and asked that His disciples be protected from evil, made holy through God's truth, and united.

His prayer extended beyond those present to people who would later believe through their message.

**Jesus Is Arrested and Sentenced to Death (John 18:1–19:16)** — Judas betrays Jesus, Peter denies knowing Him, and Jewish leaders bring Jesus before the Roman governor to demand His execution.

Jesus crossed the brook Cedron with His disciples and entered a garden. Judas arrived with soldiers and officers to arrest Him.

Peter drew a sword and cut off the ear of Malchus, the high priest's servant, but Jesus commanded him to put the weapon away.

Jesus was taken first to Annas, the father-in-law of Caiaphas, the high priest. As Jesus was questioned, Peter denied knowing Him three times.

Religious leaders then brought Jesus before Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor.

Pilate questioned Jesus about His kingship. Jesus explained that His kingdom was not of this world and that He had come to bear witness to the truth.

Though Pilate repeatedly found no grounds for condemning Him, the crowd demanded Jesus' death. After soldiers mocked and struck Jesus, Pilate finally delivered Him to be crucified.

**Jesus Is Crucified and Buried (John 19:17–42)** — Jesus dies upon the cross, fulfilling the work He came to accomplish, and His body is placed in a nearby tomb.

Jesus carried His cross to Golgotha, where He was crucified between two others.

Pilate placed an inscription above Him identifying Him as Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews. Soldiers divided His garments and cast lots for His seamless coat.

Near the cross stood Jesus' mother and the disciple whom He loved. Jesus entrusted His mother to that disciple's care.

Knowing that His work was complete, Jesus spoke of His thirst. After receiving vinegar, He declared that it was finished and died.

A soldier pierced His side with a spear, and blood and water came out.

Joseph of Arimathaea obtained permission to take Jesus' body. Nicodemus brought spices, and together they wrapped Him in linen and laid Him in a new tomb in a nearby garden.

**Jesus Rises and Appears to His Disciples (John 20:1–31)** — Mary Magdalene discovers the empty tomb, the risen Jesus appears to His followers, and Thomas comes to believe after seeing Him.

Early on the first day of the week, Mary Magdalene found the tomb's stone removed. She told Peter and the disciple whom Jesus loved, who ran to investigate.

After they departed, Mary remained weeping. Jesus appeared to her, but she initially thought He was the gardener. When He called her by name, she recognized Him.

That evening, Jesus appeared among His disciples despite the locked doors. He showed them His hands and side, gave them peace, and spoke of sending them as the Father had sent Him.

Thomas was absent and later refused to believe without seeing Jesus' wounds.

Eight days afterward, Jesus appeared again and invited Thomas to examine His wounds. Thomas confessed Him as his Lord and God.

John explains that these signs were recorded so readers might believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life through His name.

**Jesus Restores Peter and Calls Him to Follow (John 21:1–25)** — The risen Jesus meets His disciples beside the Sea of Tiberias, provides a miraculous catch of fish, and renews Peter's responsibility to care for His followers.

Several disciples, including Peter, Thomas, Nathanael, and the sons of Zebedee, spent a night fishing without success.

At morning, Jesus stood on the shore, though they did not immediately recognize Him. He directed them to cast their net on the right side of the boat, and they caught 153 large fish.

Recognizing Jesus, Peter entered the water and went toward shore. Jesus had prepared bread and fish for them.

After they ate, Jesus asked Peter three times whether he loved Him. Each time Peter answered, Jesus instructed him to feed or care for His sheep.

Jesus also spoke of the manner in which Peter would one day die and called him to follow.

When Peter asked about the future of the disciple Jesus loved, Jesus directed him to focus on his own calling.

The account ends by identifying that beloved disciple as the one bearing witness to these events and acknowledging that Jesus did many other things beyond those recorded.

John began by declaring that the eternal Word became flesh and lived among humanity. Through His teachings, signs, death, and resurrection, Jesus revealed the Father and offered eternal life to those who believe. The closing encounter returns to the personal call with which many disciples began: following Jesus and caring for those who belong to Him.

# Acts
When it happened: From Jesus' ascension (around AD 30) to the end of Paul's two years under house arrest in Rome (Acts 28:30), around AD 60–62.
When it was written: Acts is the second volume written to Theophilus (Acts 1:1). Several passages say "we," suggesting the writer travelled with Paul (for example Acts 16:10). It is usually dated to the AD 60s–80s.
Where: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, Damascus, Antioch in Syria, Cyprus, the cities of Asia Minor (including Galatia and Ephesus), Macedonia (Philippi and Thessalonica), Greece (Athens and Corinth), Caesarea, the island of Melita, and Rome.
The world at the time: The Roman Empire's roads, sea routes, and relative peace made travel possible, and Jewish synagogues were found in cities across the empire. Roman officials such as the governors Felix and Festus, and King Agrippa, appear in the story.

**Jesus Promises Power to His Followers (Acts 1:1–26)** — Before ascending into heaven, Jesus promises the Holy Ghost, and His followers prepare to continue His work by choosing a replacement for Judas.

After His resurrection, Jesus appeared to His apostles over forty days, speaking about God's kingdom. He instructed them to remain in Jerusalem until they received the promised Holy Ghost, who would give them power to be His witnesses from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth.

Jesus then ascended into heaven. Two men in white assured the watching disciples that He would return.

The apostles gathered in prayer with other believers, including Mary, Jesus' mother. Peter, one of Jesus' leading disciples, explained that Judas Iscariot, who had betrayed Jesus, needed to be replaced among the twelve apostles. After prayer, Matthias was chosen.

**The Holy Ghost Comes at Pentecost (Acts 2:1–47)** — God pours out the Holy Ghost upon the believers, empowering their witness and bringing thousands into a new community centered on Jesus.

During Pentecost, a Jewish festival celebrated in Jerusalem, the believers heard a sound like rushing wind. Tongues resembling fire appeared, and they began speaking in languages they had not learned.

Visitors from many nations heard God's works proclaimed in their own languages. Peter explained that this fulfilled a promise spoken through the prophet Joel.

He declared that Jesus, whom the people had crucified, had risen from the dead and was both Lord and Christ.

About three thousand people received Peter's message and were baptized. The believers devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread. They shared possessions to meet needs and gathered regularly in worship.

**The Apostles Face Their First Opposition (Acts 3:1–5:42)** — Miraculous healing draws attention to Jesus, while religious authorities challenge the apostles and the growing community faces judgment within its own ranks.

Peter and John healed a man who had been unable to walk since birth. When a crowd gathered, Peter explained that the healing came through faith in Jesus.

Religious leaders arrested the apostles and commanded them to stop speaking about Him. Peter and John refused, declaring that they must obey God.

The believers continued sharing resources, but Ananias and Sapphira dishonestly claimed to have donated the entire proceeds from a property sale. Both died after their deception was exposed.

As more people believed and healings continued, the apostles were imprisoned again. An angel released them, and they returned to teaching. After being questioned and beaten, they continued proclaiming Jesus with joy.

**Stephen's Death Scatters the Believers (Acts 6:1–8:40)** — The church appoints servants to meet practical needs, Stephen is killed for his testimony, and persecution carries the message beyond Jerusalem.

As the community grew, complaints arose because some widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution of food. Seven men, including Stephen and Philip, were chosen to serve.

Stephen performed wonders and spoke boldly about Jesus. Accused before the Jewish council, he reviewed Israel's history and confronted its leaders for resisting God. The crowd stoned him to death, while a young man named Saul approved.

Severe persecution scattered believers beyond Jerusalem. Philip preached in Samaria, where many believed, including a man named Simon who had practiced sorcery. Peter and John later visited, and Samaritan believers received the Holy Ghost.

Philip then met an Ethiopian official reading the prophet Isaiah. He explained the passage's connection to Jesus, and the official believed and was baptized.

**Saul Encounters Jesus and Receives a New Calling (Acts 9:1–43)** — A fierce persecutor becomes a follower of Jesus, while Peter's ministry continues through remarkable healings and the raising of a woman from death.

Saul traveled toward Damascus intending to arrest believers. A heavenly light surrounded him, and Jesus confronted him about his persecution.

Blinded, Saul was led into the city. God sent a disciple named Ananias, who restored his sight and told him that he had been chosen for God's purposes.

Saul was baptized and began preaching that Jesus was the Son of God. His former allies soon plotted to kill him, forcing him to escape.

Meanwhile, Peter healed a paralyzed man named Aeneas in Lydda. In Joppa, he prayed over a deceased disciple named Tabitha, also called Dorcas, and she returned to life. These events brought many others to faith.

**God Opens the Door to the Gentiles (Acts 10:1–11:30)** — Peter learns that God's invitation extends beyond Israel, and the growing church recognizes that Gentiles can receive the Holy Ghost through faith.

Cornelius, a Roman officer who feared God, received a vision instructing him to send for Peter.

Peter also received a vision involving animals traditionally considered unclean under Jewish law. God used this experience to prepare him to visit Cornelius, who was not Jewish.

Peter proclaimed Jesus' life, death, and resurrection. While he spoke, the Holy Ghost came upon Cornelius and the other listeners. Peter then ordered their baptism.

Some believers in Jerusalem questioned Peter for entering a Gentile home, but after hearing his account, they recognized that God had granted repentance leading to life to Gentiles as well.

The message continued spreading to Antioch, where Barnabas and Saul taught many believers. The disciples were first called Christians there. When famine was predicted, the church organized relief for believers in Judea.

**Peter Is Rescued and Paul Begins His Journeys (Acts 12:1–14:28)** — The church endures persecution while the Holy Ghost sends Barnabas and Saul to preach across new regions, establishing communities despite fierce opposition.

King Herod killed the apostle James, John's brother, and imprisoned Peter. During the church's prayers, an angel freed Peter. Herod later died after accepting praise that belonged to God.

In Antioch, the Holy Ghost set apart Barnabas and Saul for missionary work. They traveled to Cyprus, where a Roman official believed despite opposition from a sorcerer.

Continuing into Asia Minor, Saul, also called Paul, preached in synagogues and explained that forgiveness came through Jesus.

Some welcomed the message; others resisted. In Lystra, after Paul healed a man unable to walk, a crowd mistakenly treated Barnabas and Paul as gods. Later, opponents stirred up the crowd, and Paul was stoned and left for dead.

He survived, continued preaching, and revisited the new churches, appointing elders and encouraging believers to remain faithful through hardship.

**The Church Resolves a Major Disagreement (Acts 15:1–35)** — Church leaders meet in Jerusalem to decide whether Gentile believers must follow the law of Moses to be saved.

Some teachers insisted that Gentile converts must be circumcised according to Moses' law. Paul and Barnabas strongly disagreed, and the matter was brought before the apostles and elders in Jerusalem.

Peter reminded them that God had already given the Holy Ghost to Gentiles through faith. Paul and Barnabas reported the signs God had worked among the nations.

James, a leader of the Jerusalem church, supported welcoming Gentiles without requiring them to become Jews.

The leaders sent a letter explaining that Gentile believers were not required to bear the full burden of Moses' law. They were instructed to avoid idol-related pollution, sexual immorality, blood, and meat from strangled animals.

The decision brought encouragement and unity to the churches.

**Paul Carries the Gospel into Macedonia and Greece (Acts 15:36–18:22)** — Paul begins another journey with new companions, preaching across the Roman world and establishing churches among Jews and Gentiles.

After disagreeing about John Mark, Barnabas and Paul traveled separately. Paul took Silas and later added Timothy, a young believer.

In Troas, Paul received a vision calling him to Macedonia. The travelers crossed into Europe and preached in Philippi, where Lydia believed.

After Paul freed a slave girl from an evil spirit, he and Silas were imprisoned. An earthquake opened the prison doors, and the jailer and his household came to believe.

Paul continued through Thessalonica and Berea, facing opposition but gaining followers.

In Athens, he spoke about the Creator to people surrounded by idols. Some mocked his teaching about resurrection, while others believed.

In Corinth, Paul worked with Aquila and Priscilla, preached for an extended period, and faced an unsuccessful accusation before the Roman governor Gallio. He then returned toward Antioch.

**Paul Strengthens the Churches and Faces New Conflict (Acts 18:23–21:16)** — Paul's continued ministry brings deeper teaching, powerful conversions, and growing opposition, while he prepares for danger awaiting him in Jerusalem.

Paul traveled again, strengthening disciples. Meanwhile, a gifted teacher named Apollos received fuller instruction from Priscilla and Aquila before continuing his ministry.

In Ephesus, Paul taught about Jesus, and believers who knew only John's baptism received the Holy Ghost. Extraordinary healings occurred, and many people abandoned magical practices.

The spread of Christianity threatened the business of craftsmen who made shrines for the goddess Diana, provoking a public uproar.

Paul later traveled through Macedonia and Greece. In Troas, he restored a young man named Eutychus after the youth fell from a window during a lengthy gathering.

At Miletus, Paul summoned the elders of the Ephesian church and urged them to care for God's people, warning that harmful teachers would arise.

Although believers repeatedly warned Paul of coming imprisonment, he continued toward Jerusalem, prepared to suffer for Jesus' name.

**Paul Is Arrested in Jerusalem (Acts 21:17–23:35)** — Paul's arrival in Jerusalem leads to unrest, arrest, and a plot against his life, but the Lord assures him that he will also testify in Rome.

In Jerusalem, Paul met James and the elders, who advised him to demonstrate respect for Jewish customs. Despite this, opponents falsely accused him of bringing Gentiles into restricted temple areas.

A crowd attacked Paul, and Roman soldiers intervened.

Paul addressed the people, recounting his encounter with Jesus and his calling to the Gentiles. The crowd erupted again, and he was taken into Roman custody.

When officials prepared to examine him by scourging, Paul revealed that he was a Roman citizen, a status carrying legal protections.

Before the Jewish council, a dispute arose between Pharisees and Sadducees concerning resurrection.

That night, the Lord assured Paul that he would testify in Rome. When more than forty men plotted to kill him, Paul's nephew exposed the plan. Roman soldiers then escorted Paul safely to Caesarea.

**Paul Defends His Faith Before Rulers (Acts 24:1–26:32)** — Paul remains in Roman custody and explains his faith before governors Felix and Festus and King Agrippa.

In Caesarea, Jewish accusers charged Paul with stirring up unrest and violating the temple. Before Governor Felix, Paul denied the accusations and explained his belief in the resurrection.

Felix kept him imprisoned for two years, occasionally speaking with him but hoping to receive money.

When Festus became governor, Paul's accusers renewed their charges. Paul appealed to Caesar, exercising his right as a Roman citizen to seek judgment before the emperor.

King Agrippa later heard Paul's defense. Paul described his former persecution of Christians, his encounter with Jesus, and his mission to preach repentance and faith.

Agrippa and the officials concluded that Paul had done nothing deserving death or imprisonment, but his appeal to Caesar meant that he would be sent to Rome.

**A Dangerous Voyage Ends in Shipwreck (Acts 27:1–28:10)** — Paul travels toward Rome under guard, survives a violent storm and shipwreck, and continues ministering after reaching the island of Melita.

Paul sailed toward Italy with other prisoners under the supervision of a Roman centurion named Julius.

As dangerous sailing conditions approached, Paul warned that continuing the voyage would bring serious loss. His warning was not followed, and a fierce storm overtook the ship.

After many days without sunlight or clear skies, the travelers feared for their lives. Paul encouraged them, explaining that an angel had assured him everyone aboard would survive, though the ship would be lost.

The vessel eventually ran aground near Melita, also known as Malta. All 276 people reached land safely.

On the island, a venomous snake fastened onto Paul's hand, but he suffered no harm. He later healed the father of Publius, the island's chief official, and other sick residents came for healing.

After three months, the travelers resumed their journey.

**The Gospel Reaches Rome (Acts 28:11–31)** — Paul arrives in Rome, continues preaching despite imprisonment, and the account closes with God's kingdom being proclaimed freely in the empire's capital.

After traveling through Sicily and southern Italy, Paul finally reached Rome. Believers came to meet him, encouraging him after the long and difficult journey.

Paul was permitted to live under guard rather than remain in a common prison.

He invited local Jewish leaders to hear why he had been arrested. Explaining that his imprisonment concerned the hope of Israel, he arranged a longer meeting and testified about God's kingdom and Jesus from the Scriptures.

Some believed his message, while others rejected it.

Paul warned that the message would also be sent to the Gentiles, who would hear it.

For two full years, Paul remained in his rented lodging, receiving visitors and openly teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ. He proclaimed God's kingdom with confidence, and no one prevented him from continuing his message.

Acts begins with a small gathering of disciples in Jerusalem, awaiting the Holy Ghost after Jesus' ascension. Through preaching, miracles, persecution, unexpected conversions, and journeys across the Roman world, the message reaches people of many nations.

Its final scene is not a record of Paul's release or death, which Acts does not describe. Instead, Paul remains in Rome, still proclaiming Jesus. The mission entrusted to the apostles has moved far beyond Jerusalem, and the good news continues to spread despite opposition and imprisonment.
