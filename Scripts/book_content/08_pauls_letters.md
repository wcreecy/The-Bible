# Romans
When it happened: Written as Paul was about to take a collection to the poor believers in Jerusalem (Romans 15:25–26), most likely from Corinth near the end of his third missionary journey (Acts 20:2–3), around AD 57.
When it was written: Paul's helper Tertius wrote it down (Romans 16:22), and Phebe, a servant of the church at Cenchrea near Corinth, likely carried it (Romans 16:1).
Where: Sent to the believers in Rome, a church Paul had not yet visited.
The world at the time: Rome was the capital of the empire. Its church included both Jewish and Gentile believers; the emperor Claudius had earlier expelled the Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2), and many had since returned.

**Romans 1** — Paul, "a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle," wrote this letter to all the believers in Rome. He had long wanted to visit them but had been hindered. He was "not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek."

**Romans 1-3** — Paul begins by showing that everyone needs that gospel. The Gentiles knew of God through creation but did not glorify him, and turned to idols and every kind of wrongdoing. The Jews had God's law but did not keep it either. His conclusion: "all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God"—yet all may be "justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus."

**Romans 4-5** — Abraham is his example: "Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness," before he was ever circumcised. Those justified by faith have peace with God. As sin and death entered the world through one man, Adam, so grace and life come through one man, Jesus Christ.

**Romans 6-8** — Paul then asks whether believers should keep sinning so that grace may abound, and answers, "God forbid." Believers have died to sin with Christ and are raised to walk in newness of life. He describes the struggle of wanting to do good under the law yet failing, and then the freedom of life in the Spirit: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus." Nothing, he says, "shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."

**Romans 9-11** — Paul turns to his deep sorrow that many of his own people, Israel, had not believed. He explains that God has kept a remnant chosen by grace, compares Gentile believers to wild branches grafted into an olive tree, warns them against pride, and declares that "all Israel shall be saved."

**Romans 12-15** — Because of God's mercies, believers are to present their bodies as "a living sacrifice." Paul urges humble service, sincere love, submission to governing authorities, and love of neighbour, which fulfils the law. He tells those strong in faith and those weak in faith not to judge one another over food and holy days.

**Romans 15-16** — Paul explains that he is about to take a gift from the churches of Macedonia and Achaia to the poor believers in Jerusalem, and then hopes to visit Rome on his way to Spain. He commends Phebe, a servant of the church at Cenchrea, greets many believers in Rome by name, and warns them against those who cause divisions. Tertius, who wrote the letter down, adds his own greeting.

# 1 Corinthians
When it happened: Written from Ephesus (1 Corinthians 16:8) during Paul's third missionary journey, around AD 53–55.
When it was written: Paul wrote with Sosthenes (1 Corinthians 1:1), responding to reports from Chloe's household (1:11) and to a letter the Corinthians had sent him (7:1).
Where: Corinth, a wealthy Greek port city and capital of the Roman province of Achaia, where Paul had founded the church on his second journey (Acts 18).
The world at the time: Corinth was a busy center of trade, full of temples to many gods and known for its immorality. Meat sold in its markets had often been offered to idols first.

**1 Corinthians 1-4** — Paul, with Sosthenes, wrote to "the church of God which is at Corinth," which he had founded. Members of Chloe's household had told him of divisions: one said "I am of Paul," another "I of Apollos," others "I of Cephas" or "I of Christ." "Is Christ divided?" Paul asked. The preaching of the cross is foolishness to those who perish, "but unto us which are saved it is the power of God." He had come to them not with impressive speech but determined to know nothing among them except "Jesus Christ, and him crucified." He and Apollos were only servants: "I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase."

**1 Corinthians 5-7** — Paul rebuked them for tolerating a man living with his father's wife, for taking one another to court before unbelievers, and for sexual immorality: "know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost... ye are bought with a price." Then he answered the questions they had written to him about marriage and singleness.

**1 Corinthians 8-10** — On meat offered to idols, Paul said knowledge puffs up but love builds up; they must not let their freedom become a stumbling block to a weaker brother. He had given up his own rights to win others—"I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some"—and he warned them from Israel's wilderness history to flee from idolatry.

**1 Corinthians 11-14** — He corrected their disorder at the Lord's supper, where some went hungry while others got drunk, and repeated what he had received from the Lord: "this do in remembrance of me." On spiritual gifts, he taught that there are many gifts but one Spirit, just as there are many members in one body. Then he showed them a more excellent way: "Charity suffereth long, and is kind... And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity." He urged that in worship "all things be done decently and in order."

**1 Corinthians 15-16** — Some among them denied the resurrection of the dead. Paul reminded them of the gospel: "that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; And that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day," and that he was seen by Peter, by the twelve, by more than five hundred brethren at once, by James, and last of all by Paul himself. "If Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain." But Christ is risen, the firstfruits, and the dead will be raised incorruptible: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?" Paul closed with instructions for the collection for the believers in Jerusalem, his travel plans, and greetings.

# 2 Corinthians
When it happened: Written from Macedonia (2 Corinthians 2:13; 7:5), some months after 1 Corinthians, around AD 55–56.
When it was written: Paul wrote with Timothy (2 Corinthians 1:1). He mentions an earlier, painful letter that he wrote "with many tears" (2 Corinthians 2:4).
Where: Sent to the church at Corinth and to all the believers throughout Achaia.
The world at the time: Paul's relationship with the Corinthians had been strained, and he had written them a painful letter "with many tears" (2:4). Rival teachers, whom he sarcastically calls "the very chiefest apostles" (11:5), were challenging his authority.

**2 Corinthians 1-2** — Paul and Timothy wrote to the church at Corinth, blessing "the God of all comfort; Who comforteth us in all our tribulation." Paul had faced such trouble in Asia that he despaired even of life. He explained why he had changed his plans to visit them: instead he had written to them "with many tears," not to grieve them but so that they would know his love. The man who had been disciplined should now be forgiven and comforted.

**2 Corinthians 3-7** — Paul described his ministry of the new covenant, "not of the letter, but of the spirit." "But we have this treasure in earthen vessels": troubled on every side, yet not distressed; perplexed, but not in despair. "For we walk by faith, not by sight." "If any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." God has given to us "the ministry of reconciliation." Titus had brought news that the Corinthians responded to Paul's letter with godly sorrow, and Paul rejoiced.

**2 Corinthians 8-9** — Paul urged them to finish their collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem, pointing to the generous churches of Macedonia and to Christ, "that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor." "For God loveth a cheerful giver."

**2 Corinthians 10-13** — Paul then defended his apostleship against false apostles who boasted of their credentials. Forced to boast "as a fool," he listed his sufferings: imprisonments; five times receiving thirty-nine stripes; three times beaten with rods; once stoned; three shipwrecks; dangers, hunger, and cold; and the daily care of all the churches. He told of a man caught up to the third heaven, and of his own thorn in the flesh, which the Lord would not remove: "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness." He warned that he was coming a third time, urged them to examine themselves, and ended with the blessing, "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost, be with you all."

# Galatians
When it happened: Probably around AD 48–55. Whether it was written before or after the Jerusalem council of Acts 15 is debated.
When it was written: Paul wrote its closing lines in large letters with his own hand (Galatians 6:11).
Where: Sent to "the churches of Galatia" (Galatians 1:2) in central Asia Minor—possibly the churches at Antioch in Pisidia, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe that Paul founded in Acts 13–14.
The world at the time: Some teachers were insisting that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to belong fully to God's people. Even Peter, at Antioch, had withdrawn from eating with Gentile believers (Galatians 2:11–12).

**Galatians 1-2** — Paul wrote to the churches of Galatia with unusual urgency: "I marvel that ye are so soon removed from him that called you into the grace of Christ unto another gospel." If anyone—even an angel—preached another gospel, "let him be accursed." He explained that his gospel came "by the revelation of Jesus Christ," not from men. He had once persecuted the church, but God called him by his grace. Years later, the leaders in Jerusalem—James, Cephas, and John—gave him the right hand of fellowship. At Antioch Paul opposed Peter to his face for withdrawing from eating with Gentiles. "A man is not justified by the works of the law, but by the faith of Jesus Christ." "I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me."

**Galatians 3-4** — "O foolish Galatians, who hath bewitched you?" Had they received the Spirit by keeping the law, or by hearing with faith? Abraham "believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness," and those who believe are his children. The law was a schoolmaster to bring us to Christ. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus." In the fulness of time God sent his Son so that we might be adopted as sons and cry, "Abba, Father." Paul used Hagar and Sarah as an allegory of bondage and freedom.

**Galatians 5-6** — "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free." That freedom is not an excuse for the flesh but a call to serve one another in love. "Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh." "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance." They should bear one another's burdens, for "whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap." In large letters written with his own hand, Paul said he would glory only "in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ."

# Ephesians
When it happened: Written while Paul was a prisoner (Ephesians 3:1; 4:1; 6:20), most likely in Rome around AD 60–62.
When it was written: Tychicus carried it (Ephesians 6:21). Some early manuscripts lack the words "at Ephesus," suggesting it may have circulated among several churches.
Where: Ephesus, the leading city of the Roman province of Asia, where Paul had spent about three years (Acts 19–20).
The world at the time: Ephesus was famous for its great temple of Diana. Its church included both Jewish and Gentile believers, and the letter stresses that Christ has made them one.

**Ephesians 1-3** — Writing as "the prisoner of Jesus Christ," Paul blessed God, "who hath blessed us with all spiritual blessings in heavenly places in Christ," who chose us in him before the foundation of the world, and in whom "we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins." He prayed that they would know the hope of God's calling and the greatness of his power, which raised Christ from the dead. Though they had been "dead in trespasses and sins," God, rich in mercy, made them alive with Christ: "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast." Christ had broken down the middle wall of partition between Jew and Gentile, making the two into one new man. Paul prayed that they would know "the love of Christ, which passeth knowledge."

**Ephesians 4-6** — Therefore they should "walk worthy of the vocation wherewith ye are called," keeping "the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace": "One Lord, one faith, one baptism." Christ gave apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers to build up his body. They were to put off the old man and put on the new, speak the truth in love, "be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another," and walk in love and as children of light. Paul gave instructions to wives and husbands—"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church"—to children and parents, and to servants and masters. Finally, "be strong in the Lord," and "Put on the whole armour of God": truth, righteousness, the gospel of peace, the shield of faith, the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God.

# Philippians
When it happened: Written while Paul was in bonds (Philippians 1:7, 13), likely in Rome around AD 60–62; he sends greetings from "Caesar's household" (Philippians 4:22).
When it was written: Epaphroditus, who had brought the Philippians' gift to Paul, carried it home (Philippians 2:25).
Where: Philippi, a Roman colony in Macedonia and the first city in Europe where Paul founded a church (Acts 16).
The world at the time: As a Roman colony, Philippi was proud of its Roman citizenship. The church there had supported Paul with gifts more than once (Philippians 4:15–18).

**Philippians 1** — Paul and Timothy wrote to the believers at Philippi, thanking God for their fellowship in the gospel from the first day, "Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it." Though he was in bonds, Paul rejoiced that his imprisonment had made the gospel known throughout the palace. "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain."

**Philippians 2** — He urged them to be of one mind, each esteeming others better than themselves: "Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus," who took the form of a servant "and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross. Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him... That at the name of Jesus every knee should bow." Paul planned to send Timothy and was sending back Epaphroditus, who had nearly died while bringing their gift.

**Philippians 3** — Paul warned against those who put confidence in the flesh. He himself had every reason to boast—circumcised the eighth day, a Hebrew of the Hebrews, a Pharisee, blameless in the law—"But what things were gain to me, those I counted loss for Christ." "I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus," for "our conversation is in heaven."

**Philippians 4** — He urged Euodias and Syntyche to be of the same mind in the Lord. "Rejoice in the Lord alway: and again I say, Rejoice." "Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus." He thanked them for their gift: "I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content... I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me."

# Colossians
When it happened: Written while Paul was in prison (Colossians 4:3, 18), likely in Rome around AD 60–62, and sent with Tychicus and Onesimus (Colossians 4:7–9).
When it was written: Paul had not visited Colossae himself; the church learned the gospel from Epaphras (Colossians 1:7; 2:1).
Where: Colosse, a small city in the Lycus valley of Asia Minor, near Laodicea and Hierapolis. The church had been founded by Epaphras (Colossians 1:7), and Paul had never visited it (Colossians 2:1).
The world at the time: A false teaching was spreading that mixed human philosophy with rules about food, holy days, new moons, and sabbaths, and the worship of angels (Colossians 2:8, 16, 18).

**Colossians 1** — Paul and Timothy wrote to the believers at Colosse, a church Paul had never visited, which had heard the gospel from Epaphras. Paul prayed that they would be filled with the knowledge of God's will. Christ is "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature: For by him were all things created... and by him all things consist." He is the head of the body, the church, and through the blood of his cross God reconciled all things to himself. The mystery hidden for ages is now revealed: "Christ in you, the hope of glory."

**Colossians 2** — "As ye have therefore received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk ye in him." Paul warned them not to be led astray "through philosophy and vain deceit," for "in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily." God had forgiven all their trespasses, "Blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us... nailing it to his cross." So they should let no one judge them about food, holy days, new moons, or sabbaths, or lead them into worshipping angels.

**Colossians 3-4** — "If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above." They were to put to death earthly sins, put off anger and lying, and put on mercy, kindness, humility, and forgiveness, "And above all these things put on charity." "And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus." Paul gave instructions for households, asked them to pray that God would open a door for the gospel, and sent greetings from Aristarchus, Mark, Luke "the beloved physician," and others. He asked that the letter also be read in the church at Laodicea.

# 1 Thessalonians
When it happened: Written from Corinth around AD 50–51, shortly after Paul founded the church in Thessalonica (Acts 17–18; 1 Thessalonians 3:6). It is likely one of Paul's earliest letters.
When it was written: Paul had been forced to leave Thessalonica after a short stay (Acts 17:1–10), and he wrote partly to explain why he had not been able to return (1 Thessalonians 2:17–18).
Where: Thessalonica, the capital of Macedonia, on the great Roman road across northern Greece.
The world at the time: Opposition had forced Paul to leave Thessalonica after only a short stay (Acts 17:1–10). The new believers were facing persecution, and some were grieving over believers who had died before Christ's return.

**1 Thessalonians 1-3** — Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy wrote to the young church at Thessalonica, remembering their "work of faith, and labour of love, and patience of hope." The Thessalonians had "turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God," and the word of the Lord had sounded out from them everywhere. Paul recalled how gently he had lived among them, working night and day so as not to be a burden to anyone. Forced to leave, he had longed to see them, and he sent Timothy, who returned with good news of their faith and love.

**1 Thessalonians 4-5** — Paul urged them to live in holiness, to love one another more and more, and to "study to be quiet, and to do your own business, and to work with your own hands." Then he spoke to their grief over believers who had died: they should not sorrow "even as others which have no hope." "For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout... and the dead in Christ shall rise first: Then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds... and so shall we ever be with the Lord." The day of the Lord would come "as a thief in the night," so they should watch and be sober. He closed with short commands: "Rejoice evermore. Pray without ceasing. In every thing give thanks."

# 2 Thessalonians
When it happened: Written soon after 1 Thessalonians, probably from Corinth around AD 51.
When it was written: Paul signed it with his own hand as a mark of every genuine letter from him (2 Thessalonians 3:17). He wrote partly because of a message, possibly a forged letter, claiming that "the day of Christ is at hand" (2 Thessalonians 2:2).
Where: Thessalonica in Macedonia.
The world at the time: Persecution continued. Some were teaching that the day of Christ had already come—possibly using a letter falsely claimed to be from Paul (2 Thessalonians 2:2)—and some believers had stopped working.

**2 Thessalonians 1** — Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy thanked God that the Thessalonians' faith was growing and their love abounding, even under persecution. God would give rest to those who were troubled and judge their persecutors "when the Lord Jesus shall be revealed from heaven with his mighty angels."

**2 Thessalonians 2** — Some were being shaken by claims—perhaps even by a letter said to be from Paul—that the day of Christ had already come. Paul told them it would not come until there was first a falling away "and that man of sin be revealed," whom the Lord would destroy "with the brightness of his coming." He urged them to stand fast and hold to the teaching they had received.

**2 Thessalonians 3** — Paul asked them to pray "that the word of the Lord may have free course." He warned against those who walked disorderly and refused to work, reminding them of his own example and his rule "that if any would not work, neither should he eat." "Be not weary in well doing." He signed the letter with his own hand, as he did in every letter.

# 1 Timothy
When it happened: Written after Paul left Timothy in Ephesus and went on to Macedonia (1 Timothy 1:3), often placed after Paul's first imprisonment in Rome, around AD 62–64.
When it was written: Paul writes to Timothy, his "own son in the faith" (1 Timothy 1:2), with instructions for leading the church in Ephesus.
Where: Timothy was leading the church in Ephesus.
The world at the time: False teachers were spreading fables and endless genealogies (1 Timothy 1:4) and forbidding marriage and certain foods (1 Timothy 4:3). Churches were organizing their leadership with bishops and deacons.

**1 Timothy 1** — Paul wrote to Timothy, "my own son in the faith," whom he had left at Ephesus to stop certain men from teaching false doctrine, fables, and endless genealogies. Paul gave thanks that Christ had shown mercy to him, once a blasphemer and persecutor: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief."

**1 Timothy 2-3** — He urged prayers "For kings, and for all that are in authority," because God "will have all men to be saved," and "there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus." He gave instructions for worship and set out the qualifications for bishops and deacons, who must be blameless, self-controlled, and faithful in their own households. He spoke of "the mystery of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh."

**1 Timothy 4-6** — Paul warned that some would depart from the faith, forbidding marriage and certain foods. He told Timothy, "Let no man despise thy youth; but be thou an example of the believers," and to give attention to reading, exhortation, and doctrine. He gave guidance for caring for widows, honoring elders, and for servants. He warned that "the love of money is the root of all evil," and charged Timothy, "Fight the good fight of faith, lay hold on eternal life," and to guard what had been entrusted to him.

# 2 Timothy
When it happened: Written while Paul was imprisoned in Rome and expected to be executed soon (2 Timothy 1:8, 17; 4:6)—traditionally under the emperor Nero, around AD 64–67.
When it was written: It is presented as Paul's last letter, written as he expected to die: "the time of my departure is at hand" (2 Timothy 4:6).
Where: Timothy was most likely at Ephesus (2 Timothy 1:18; 4:19).
The world at the time: Many had deserted Paul (2 Timothy 1:15; 4:10, 16), and only Luke remained with him (2 Timothy 4:11).

**2 Timothy 1-2** — From prison in Rome, Paul wrote to Timothy, "my dearly beloved son," remembering his tears and his sincere faith, which first lived in his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice. "For God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind." Paul was not ashamed of his chains, "for I know whom I have believed." Many in Asia had turned away from him, but Onesiphorus had searched him out in Rome. Timothy was to be strong in grace, endure hardness "as a good soldier of Jesus Christ," and "Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth."

**2 Timothy 3-4** — Paul warned that "in the last days perilous times shall come," but Timothy had seen Paul's sufferings and had known the holy scriptures from childhood: "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness." "Preach the word; be instant in season, out of season." Paul knew his death was near: "I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith." Demas had forsaken him, and only Luke was with him. He asked Timothy to come quickly and to bring Mark, his cloke, and the books, "but especially the parchments."

# Titus
When it happened: Written after Paul left Titus on the island of Crete (Titus 1:5), likely around AD 62–64. Paul planned to spend the winter at Nicopolis (Titus 3:12).
When it was written: Paul writes to Titus, "mine own son after the common faith" (Titus 1:4), with instructions for appointing elders in Crete (Titus 1:5).
Where: The island of Crete in the Mediterranean.
The world at the time: Cretans had a poor reputation, which Paul illustrates by quoting one of their own poets (Titus 1:12). False teachers, especially "they of the circumcision," were upsetting whole households (Titus 1:10–11).

**Titus 1** — Paul wrote to Titus, "mine own son after the common faith," whom he had left in Crete to set things in order and to ordain elders in every city. Elders must be blameless, hospitable, self-controlled, and holding fast the faithful word, because many unruly talkers were overturning whole households for gain. Paul quoted one of the Cretans' own prophets: "The Cretians are alway liars."

**Titus 2** — Titus was to teach sound doctrine to older men and women, younger women and men, and servants, and to be a pattern of good works himself. "For the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men," teaching us to live soberly, righteously, and godly, "Looking for that blessed hope, and the glorious appearing of the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ."

**Titus 3** — Believers were to be subject to rulers, ready for every good work, and gentle toward everyone, remembering what they themselves once were. "Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us." Paul told Titus to avoid foolish questions and to come to him at Nicopolis for the winter.

# Philemon
When it happened: Written while Paul was a prisoner (Philemon 1:1, 9), likely in Rome around AD 60–62, and sent at the same time as Colossians, which mentions Onesimus (Colossians 4:9).
When it was written: Paul wrote at least part of it with his own hand (Philemon 1:19). Though personal, it is also addressed to Apphia, Archippus, and "the church in thy house" (Philemon 1:2).
Where: Sent to Philemon and the church that met in his house, apparently at Colosse.
The world at the time: Slavery was common throughout the Roman Empire. Onesimus, a servant of Philemon, had left his master and met Paul, through whom he became a believer.

**Philemon 1:1-7** — Paul, "a prisoner of Jesus Christ," and Timothy wrote to Philemon, to Apphia and Archippus, and to the church that met in Philemon's house. Paul thanked God for Philemon's love and faith toward all the saints, which had refreshed their hearts.

**Philemon 1:8-21** — Paul could have commanded him, but instead appealed out of love on behalf of Onesimus, Philemon's servant, who had departed from him for a season and had become Paul's son in the faith while Paul was in bonds. In time past Onesimus had been unprofitable, "but now profitable to thee and to me." Paul was sending him back, "Not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved." "If thou count me therefore a partner, receive him as myself. If he hath wronged thee, or oweth thee ought, put that on mine account... I will repay it." Paul was confident Philemon would do even more than he asked.

**Philemon 1:22-25** — Paul asked Philemon to prepare a lodging for him, hoping to be released through their prayers, and sent greetings from Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke.
