# Genesis
When it happened: From creation to the death of Joseph. Genesis gives no calendar dates; the lives of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are often placed in the early second millennium BC (roughly 2000–1700 BC), though dates are uncertain.
When it was written: Jewish and Christian tradition credits Moses as the author of Genesis and the rest of the Pentateuch. Many scholars think the book reached its final form over a long period, possibly as late as the time of the exile.
Where: Mesopotamia (Ur of the Chaldees and Haran), the land of Canaan (Shechem, Bethel, Hebron, Beer-sheba), and Egypt.
The world at the time: Powerful cities rose in Mesopotamia and Egypt was ruled by pharaohs. Abraham's family lived as herdsmen moving among the Canaanite towns, owning no land except a burial cave they bought at Hebron (Genesis 23).

**Genesis 1-3** — "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." Over six days God spoke light, sky, land and plants, the sun, moon and stars, the creatures of the sea and air, and the animals of the land into being. Last of all he made man in his own image, male and female, and on the seventh day he rested. God formed the man from the dust of the ground, placed him in the garden of Eden, and commanded him not to eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. From the man's rib he made the woman. The serpent persuaded the woman to eat the forbidden fruit, and she gave some to her husband, who ate as well. God pronounced judgment on the serpent, the woman, and the man, declared that the woman's seed would bruise the serpent's head, and sent them out of the garden.

**Genesis 4-5** — Outside Eden, Adam and Eve's son Cain killed his brother Abel and was sent away as a wanderer. The generations passed from Adam to Noah. Methuselah lived 969 years, and Enoch "walked with God: and he was not; for God took him."

**Genesis 6-9** — Wickedness filled the earth, and God determined to destroy it with a flood—but "Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD." At God's command Noah built an ark and entered it with his family and the animals. The waters prevailed on the earth for 150 days. When the ark came to rest on the mountains of Ararat and the ground dried, Noah built an altar. God promised never again to destroy the earth with a flood and set his bow in the cloud as the sign of that covenant.

**Genesis 10-11** — Noah's descendants spread out into the nations. At Babel, where everyone still spoke one language, people began building a city and a tower to reach heaven. God confused their language and scattered them across the earth. The family line narrows from Noah's son Shem down to Terah and his son Abram.

**Genesis 12-25** — God told Abram to leave his country and go to a land He would show him, promising to make him a great nation and that "in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed." Abram travelled to Canaan with his wife Sarai and his nephew Lot. When God promised him descendants as countless as the stars, Abram "believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness." Still childless, Sarai gave her servant Hagar to Abram, and Hagar bore Ishmael. God renamed them Abraham and Sarah, gave circumcision as the sign of his covenant, and promised Sarah a son. God destroyed the wicked cities of Sodom and Gomorrah, but rescued Lot; Lot's wife looked back and became a pillar of salt. When Abraham was a hundred years old, Sarah bore Isaac. Later God tested Abraham by commanding him to offer Isaac as a burnt offering; the angel of the LORD stopped his hand, and Abraham offered a ram in his son's place. After Sarah died, Abraham's servant found Rebekah as a wife for Isaac, and Abraham died at 175.

**Genesis 25-36** — Rebekah bore twins, Esau and Jacob. Esau sold his birthright to Jacob for a meal of pottage, and later, with Rebekah's help, Jacob deceived his blind father Isaac into giving him the blessing meant for Esau. Fleeing Esau's anger, Jacob dreamed at Bethel of a ladder reaching to heaven, and God renewed Abraham's promise to him. In Haran he worked seven years for his uncle Laban to marry Rachel, was given her sister Leah instead, and worked seven more years for Rachel. Eleven sons and a daughter were born to him there. On the way home Jacob wrestled with a man until daybreak and was renamed Israel. He was reconciled with Esau, and Rachel died giving birth to his twelfth son, Benjamin.

**Genesis 37-50** — Jacob loved Joseph more than his other sons and gave him a coat of many colours. Joseph's brothers, jealous of him and his dreams, sold him to traders bound for Egypt and told their father a wild beast had devoured him. In Egypt Joseph served Potiphar, was falsely accused by Potiphar's wife, and was thrown into prison, where he interpreted the dreams of Pharaoh's butler and baker. Two years later he interpreted Pharaoh's own dreams—seven years of plenty followed by seven years of famine—and Pharaoh set him over all the land of Egypt. When the famine came, Joseph's brothers arrived to buy grain. Joseph tested them, and when Judah offered himself as a slave in Benjamin's place, Joseph revealed who he was. Jacob brought his whole family to live in Goshen, blessed his sons before he died, and was buried in Canaan. Joseph told his brothers, "ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good," and before he died at 110 he made them promise to carry his bones out of Egypt when God brought them back to the land.

# Exodus
When it happened: Israel's slavery in Egypt, the exodus, and the first year in the wilderness. 1 Kings 6:1 places the exodus 480 years before Solomon began the temple, pointing to the mid-1400s BC; many scholars prefer a date in the 1200s BC.
When it was written: Traditionally written by Moses, and the book itself records Moses writing down the LORD's words (Exodus 17:14; 24:4; 34:27). Many scholars see a longer process of composition and editing.
Where: Egypt (the land of Goshen and the store cities of Pithom and Raamses), Midian, the wilderness, and Mount Sinai, also called Horeb.
The world at the time: Egypt was the great power of the region, and its king, who "knew not Joseph," had enslaved the Hebrews. Exodus never names the pharaoh, so the event cannot be tied to a specific Egyptian reign with certainty.

**Exodus 1-2** — After Joseph died, the Israelites multiplied in Egypt until a new king arose "which knew not Joseph." He made them slaves, building the treasure cities Pithom and Raamses, and ordered every Hebrew baby boy thrown into the river. One Levite mother hid her son and then laid him in an ark of bulrushes among the reeds. Pharaoh's daughter found him, raised him as her son, and named him Moses. When he was grown, Moses killed an Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew, fled to Midian, married Zipporah, and kept the flocks of her father Jethro.

**Exodus 3-4** — At Horeb, the mountain of God, the angel of the LORD appeared to Moses in a flame of fire out of a bush that burned but was not consumed. God said he had seen the affliction of his people and would send Moses to bring them out. When Moses asked his name, God answered, "I AM THAT I AM." Moses protested that he was slow of speech, so God appointed his brother Aaron to speak for him.

**Exodus 5-11** — Pharaoh refused: "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?" He made the slaves' work harder, forcing them to make bricks without straw. Then God sent plagues on Egypt: water turned to blood, frogs, lice, flies, disease on the livestock, boils, hail, locusts, and darkness. Each time Pharaoh hardened his heart and would not let the people go.

**Exodus 12-13** — God instituted the Passover. Each household killed a lamb and put its blood on the doorposts, and at midnight the LORD struck every firstborn in Egypt but passed over the houses marked with blood. Pharaoh finally sent Israel away, and about six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children, left Egypt after 430 years. The LORD led them by a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night.

**Exodus 14-15** — Pharaoh changed his mind and chased them to the Red Sea. Moses stretched out his hand, the LORD divided the waters, and Israel walked through on dry ground. When the Egyptians followed, the waters returned and covered them. Moses and the people sang a song of praise, and Miriam led the women with timbrels.

**Exodus 15-18** — In the wilderness the people complained of thirst and hunger. God made the bitter waters of Marah sweet, sent quails, and gave manna—bread from heaven gathered every morning except on the sabbath—and brought water out of a rock at Horeb. Israel defeated Amalek while Aaron and Hur held up Moses' hands. Moses' father-in-law Jethro advised him to appoint able men to judge the people with him.

**Exodus 19-24** — At Mount Sinai the LORD descended in fire, thunder, and thick cloud, and spoke the Ten Commandments. He gave laws for worship, justice, and daily life, and the people answered, "All that the LORD hath spoken we will do." Moses sprinkled the blood of the covenant on the people and went up the mountain, where he stayed forty days and forty nights.

**Exodus 25-31** — There God gave detailed instructions for the tabernacle, the ark of the covenant, the priests' garments, and the consecration of Aaron and his sons. He gave Moses two tables of testimony, "tables of stone, written with the finger of God."

**Exodus 32-34** — Meanwhile the people had Aaron make a golden calf and worshipped it. Moses came down, broke the tables, and burned the calf. He pleaded for the people, and the LORD did not destroy them. The LORD passed by Moses proclaiming, "The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious," and Moses cut two new tables of stone. When he came down from the mountain, his face shone.

**Exodus 35-40** — The people brought so many offerings that they had to be told to stop. Bezaleel, Aholiab, and the skilled workers made the tabernacle and everything in it just as the LORD had commanded. On the first day of the first month of the second year, the tabernacle was set up; the cloud covered the tent, and the glory of the LORD filled it.

# Leviticus
When it happened: While Israel was camped at Mount Sinai, in the weeks after the tabernacle was set up on the first day of the second year (Exodus 40:17; Numbers 1:1).
When it was written: The book is almost entirely speeches introduced with "the LORD spake unto Moses," and tradition credits Moses as its author. Many scholars connect its present form to Israel's priests over a longer period.
Where: At the tabernacle, the tent of meeting, in the camp at the foot of Mount Sinai.
The world at the time: Israel was a newly freed people between Egypt and Canaan. God told them, "After the doings of the land of Egypt, wherein ye dwelt, shall ye not do: and after the doings of the land of Canaan... shall ye not do" (Leviticus 18:3).

**Leviticus 1-7** — With the tabernacle standing, the LORD called to Moses from the tent of meeting and gave the laws of the offerings: the burnt offering, the meat offering of grain, the peace offering, the sin offering, and the trespass offering. He explained what each was for, what could be offered, and how the priests were to handle it.

**Leviticus 8-10** — Moses ordained Aaron and his sons as priests in a seven-day ceremony. When Aaron offered the first sacrifices, fire came out from before the LORD and consumed the offering on the altar, and the people shouted and fell on their faces. But Aaron's sons Nadab and Abihu offered "strange fire" that the LORD had not commanded, and fire went out from the LORD and devoured them.

**Leviticus 11-15** — God taught Israel to distinguish between clean and unclean: which animals could be eaten, purification after childbirth, how the priests were to examine leprosy in people, clothing, and houses, and how to be cleansed from bodily discharges.

**Leviticus 16** — Once a year, on the Day of Atonement, the high priest alone was to go behind the veil into the most holy place and sprinkle blood on the mercy seat. A second goat, bearing the people's iniquities, was sent away into the wilderness. This was "to make an atonement for the children of Israel for all their sins once a year."

**Leviticus 17-22** — The LORD called Israel to holiness in every part of life: "Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy." These chapters forbid eating blood, set rules for marriage and sexual conduct, and command care for the poor and the stranger, honest weights, and "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself." Further standards were given for the priests.

**Leviticus 23-25** — God set Israel's yearly calendar of holy days: the sabbath, Passover and unleavened bread, firstfruits, the feast of weeks, the blowing of trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and the feast of tabernacles. A man who blasphemed the name of the LORD was stoned. Every seventh year the land was to rest, and in the fiftieth year, the jubile, land returned to its families and Israelite servants went free.

**Leviticus 26-27** — The LORD promised blessing for obedience—rain, harvest, peace, and his presence among them—and warned of growing judgment and exile for disobedience. Yet if they confessed their sin, he would remember his covenant. The book closes with laws about vows and things dedicated to the LORD.

# Numbers
When it happened: From the second month of the second year after the exodus (Numbers 1:1) to the fortieth year (Numbers 33:38)—the nearly forty years Israel spent in the wilderness.
When it was written: Traditionally written by Moses; the book records that "Moses wrote their goings out according to their journeys" (Numbers 33:2). Many scholars see later editing.
Where: The wilderness of Sinai, the wilderness of Paran, Kadesh, the borders of Edom, and finally the plains of Moab by the Jordan, across from Jericho.
The world at the time: Canaan was full of walled cities. East of the Jordan lay Edom, Moab, and Ammon, the Amorite kingdoms of Sihon and Og, and the Midianites, several of whom opposed Israel's passage.

**Numbers 1-10** — In the wilderness of Sinai, Moses counted the men of Israel able to go to war—603,550—and arranged the tribes in camp around the tabernacle, with the Levites set apart to carry and serve it. The priests were given a blessing for the people: "The LORD bless thee, and keep thee." Then the cloud lifted from the tabernacle, and Israel set out from Sinai.

**Numbers 11-12** — Almost at once the people complained. Tired of manna, they cried for meat; God sent quails, and a plague with them. The LORD put his Spirit on seventy elders to share Moses' burden. When Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses, Miriam was struck with leprosy and shut out of the camp for seven days. The text notes that Moses was "very meek, above all the men which were upon the face of the earth."

**Numbers 13-14** — From the wilderness of Paran, Moses sent twelve men to spy out Canaan. They came back carrying a cluster of grapes so large that two men bore it on a staff, but ten of them reported giants and walled cities. Only Caleb and Joshua urged the people to go up. The congregation talked of stoning them and choosing a captain to lead them back to Egypt. God declared that they would wander forty years—a year for each day of spying—until that generation died in the wilderness; only Caleb and Joshua would enter the land.

**Numbers 15-19** — Korah, Dathan, and Abiram led a rebellion against Moses and Aaron. The earth opened and swallowed them, and fire consumed the 250 men offering incense. Aaron's rod budded, blossomed, and yielded almonds, confirming his priesthood. Laws followed for the priests and Levites and for purification with the ashes of a red heifer.

**Numbers 20-21** — Miriam died at Kadesh. When the people had no water, God told Moses to speak to the rock; instead Moses struck it twice, and God told Moses and Aaron that they would not bring the people into the land. Edom refused Israel passage, and Aaron died on Mount Hor. When the people spoke against God, fiery serpents bit them; Moses made a serpent of brass and set it on a pole, and whoever looked at it lived. Israel defeated Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan.

**Numbers 22-25** — On the plains of Moab, King Balak hired the prophet Balaam to curse Israel. On the way, Balaam's donkey saw the angel of the LORD standing in the road and spoke to him. Each time Balak took him to a high place to curse Israel, Balaam blessed them instead, foretelling that "there shall come a Star out of Jacob." Afterward many Israelites joined the Moabites in worshipping Baal-peor, and a plague killed 24,000 until Phinehas the priest acted.

**Numbers 26-36** — A second census counted the new generation; of those numbered at Sinai, only Caleb and Joshua remained. The daughters of Zelophehad were granted their father's inheritance, and Joshua was appointed to succeed Moses. Israel fought against Midian, and the tribes of Reuben and Gad and half the tribe of Manasseh received land east of the Jordan on condition that they help conquer Canaan. The book closes by listing Israel's journeys, setting the borders of the land, and appointing cities for the Levites and cities of refuge.

# Deuteronomy
When it happened: In the last weeks of Moses' life, beginning on the first day of the eleventh month of the fortieth year after the exodus (Deuteronomy 1:3).
When it was written: The book presents Moses' farewell speeches and says that "Moses wrote this law" (Deuteronomy 31:9); the account of his death in chapter 34 was added by another hand. Many scholars connect the book's final form with the scroll found in the temple in King Josiah's day in the 600s BC (2 Kings 22).
Where: The plains of Moab, east of the Jordan River across from Jericho, ending on Mount Nebo.
The world at the time: A new generation, born in the wilderness, was about to cross the Jordan into a land held by the Hittites, Girgashites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites (Deuteronomy 7:1).

**Deuteronomy 1-4** — In the fortieth year after leaving Egypt, on the plains of Moab, Moses spoke to the new generation of Israel. He retold their journey from Horeb: the spies, their refusal to enter the land, the long years of wandering, and the victories over Sihon and Og. He reminded them that at Horeb they had heard God's voice out of the fire but saw no form, and warned them never to make idols.

**Deuteronomy 5-11** — Moses repeated the Ten Commandments and called Israel to wholehearted love: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD: And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." They were to teach these words to their children. God had humbled them in the wilderness to teach them that "man doth not live by bread only," and they were not to think they received the land because of their own righteousness. Moses recalled the golden calf and his forty days of pleading for them.

**Deuteronomy 12-26** — Moses then set out the laws for life in the land: worship at the one place the LORD would choose, rejecting the gods of Canaan, testing prophets, clean and unclean food, tithes, the release of debts every seventh year, the yearly feasts, justice through judges, kings, and priests, cities of refuge, war, family life, and care for the widow, the fatherless, and the stranger.

**Deuteronomy 27-30** — Once across the Jordan, the tribes were to proclaim blessings from Mount Gerizim and curses from Mount Ebal. Moses laid out the blessings of obedience and the curses of disobedience, including being scattered among the nations, and promised that if they returned to the LORD, he would gather them again. "I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life."

**Deuteronomy 31-34** — At 120 years old, Moses commissioned Joshua to lead the people, wrote down the law, and gave it to the priests to be read aloud every seven years. He taught Israel a song recalling God's faithfulness and their unfaithfulness, and he blessed each tribe. Then he climbed Mount Nebo, and the LORD showed him the whole land. Moses died there in Moab, and the LORD buried him, "but no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day." Israel mourned for thirty days, and Joshua, "full of the spirit of wisdom," led them. "There arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face."
