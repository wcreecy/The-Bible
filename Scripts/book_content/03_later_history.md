# 1 Kings
When it happened: From the last days of King David (about 970 BC) through Solomon's reign and the division of the kingdom, to the reigns of Ahaziah in Israel and Jehoshaphat in Judah (about 850 BC).
When it was written: 1 and 2 Kings were originally one book, and Jewish tradition credits Jeremiah. The writer cites earlier sources such as "the book of the acts of Solomon" (1 Kings 11:41). Because 2 Kings ends in the Babylonian exile, the finished work dates to about 560 BC or later.
Where: Jerusalem, Samaria, the shrines at Bethel and Dan, Mount Carmel, Mount Horeb, Jezreel, and the Phoenician cities of Tyre, Sidon, and Zarephath.
The world at the time: Solomon traded with Hiram king of Tyre and married Pharaoh's daughter. After the kingdom split, Egypt's Shishak invaded Judah (1 Kings 14:25), Syria rose at Damascus, and Baal worship spread from Sidon through Ahab's wife Jezebel.

**Solomon Becomes King After David (1 Kings 1:1–2:46)** — As David approaches death, a struggle over the throne ends with Solomon becoming king and securing his position.

King David is now very old and cannot keep warm. His servants find a beautiful young woman, Abishag, to care for him. Meanwhile his son Adonijah, the oldest surviving son and very handsome, decides to make himself king. “Then Adonijah the son of Haggith exalted himself, saying, I will be king.” His father had never corrected him. Joab the commander and Abiathar the priest support him, and he throws a feast to celebrate. But he does not invite Nathan the prophet, Zadok the priest, Benaiah, the mighty men, or his brother Solomon.

Nathan goes to Bathsheba and tells her to remind David of his promise that Solomon would be king. While she is speaking with the king, Nathan comes in and confirms her story. David says, “Assuredly Solomon thy son shall reign after me.” He orders Zadok and Nathan to put Solomon on David's own mule, take him down to the spring of Gihon, and anoint him king. They do it, blow the trumpet, and all the people shout, “God save king Solomon.” The celebration is so loud that “the earth rent with the sound of them.” Adonijah's guests hear the noise, learn what has happened, and scatter in fear. Adonijah runs to the altar and grabs its horns for protection. Solomon spares him on the condition that he behave.

Before David dies, he charges Solomon: “I go the way of all the earth: be thou strong therefore, and shew thyself a man; And keep the charge of the LORD thy God, to walk in his ways.” He also tells Solomon to deal with Joab, who murdered Abner and Amasa, and with Shimei, who cursed him. “So David slept with his fathers, and was buried in the city of David,” after reigning forty years. Soon Adonijah asks Bathsheba to request Abishag as his wife, which would strengthen his claim to the throne. Solomon sees the danger and has him executed. He removes Abiathar from the priesthood, fulfilling God's word against Eli's family. Joab flees to the altar, and Benaiah kills him there. Shimei is ordered never to leave Jerusalem. When he breaks that order three years later, he too is executed. “And the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon.”

**God Gives Solomon Wisdom (1 Kings 3:1–4:34)** — God grants Solomon extraordinary wisdom after he asks for understanding to govern Israel.

Solomon makes an alliance with Pharaoh by marrying his daughter. He loves the LORD, but he still offers sacrifices at the high places, the hilltop shrines. At Gibeon, he offers a thousand burnt offerings. That night, God appears to him in a dream: “Ask what I shall give thee.” Solomon answers that he feels like “but a little child: I know not how to go out or come in.” He asks, “Give therefore thy servant an understanding heart to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad.” God is pleased that he did not ask for long life, riches, or the death of his enemies. He promises him “a wise and an understanding heart; so that there was none like thee before thee.” And he adds what Solomon did not ask for: riches and honor. If Solomon walks in God's ways, God will also give him a long life.

Soon after, two prostitutes come before the king. They live in the same house, and each had a baby. One woman's baby died in the night, because she lay on it. Now both claim the living baby. One says the other switched the babies while she slept. The other says the living child is hers. Solomon says, “Bring me a sword.” He orders, “Divide the living child in two, and give half to the one, and half to the other.” The real mother cries out, “O my lord, give her the living child, and in no wise slay it.” But the other woman says, “Let it be neither mine nor thine, but divide it.” Solomon says, “Give her the living child... she is the mother thereof.” When all Israel hears about it, they fear the king, “for they saw that the wisdom of God was in him, to do judgment.”

Israel is at peace and enjoys great plenty. “Judah and Israel were many, as the sand which is by the sea in multitude, eating and drinking, and making merry.” Every man lives “under his vine and under his fig tree.” Solomon organizes officials to supply his court. His wisdom is “exceeding much,” greater than the wisest men of the east and of Egypt. He speaks three thousand proverbs and writes 1,005 songs. He knows about trees, animals, birds, reptiles, and fish. People come from every nation to hear his wisdom.

**Solomon Begins Building God's Temple (1 Kings 5:1–6:38)** — Solomon prepares materials and workers for a permanent temple in Jerusalem.

Hiram, king of Tyre, had been a friend of David. Solomon sends him a message: God has given Israel peace, so now he will build the temple his father David had longed to build. He asks Hiram to have his skilled workers cut cedar trees from Lebanon. Hiram is delighted and agrees to float the logs down the coast on rafts, in exchange for wheat and olive oil. Solomon drafts thousands of workers: thirty thousand Israelites who take turns in Lebanon, seventy thousand carriers, and eighty thousand stonecutters in the hills.

“And it came to pass in the four hundred and eightieth year after the children of Israel were come out of the land of Egypt... that he began to build the house of the LORD.” The temple is about ninety feet long, thirty feet wide, and forty-five feet high, with side rooms and a porch in front. Amazingly, the stones are cut at the quarry, so that “there was neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron heard in the house, while it was in building.”

While it is being built, God speaks to Solomon: if he walks in God's statutes, God will keep his promise to David, “And I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will not forsake my people Israel.” The inside walls are lined with cedar carved with flowers and gourds, and the whole interior is covered with gold. In the most holy place, Solomon sets two huge cherubim made of olive wood covered with gold, each fifteen feet tall, with their wings touching the walls and each other. The doors are carved with cherubim, palm trees, and flowers and covered with gold. The temple takes seven years to build.

**Solomon Completes the Royal Buildings and Temple Furnishings (1 Kings 7:1–51)** — Solomon builds his palace and completes bronze and gold temple furnishings.

Solomon spends thirteen years building his own palace complex. It includes the House of the Forest of Lebanon, named for its many cedar pillars, a hall of judgment where he sits on his throne, and a palace for Pharaoh's daughter. All of it is built with huge, expensive stones.

Then Solomon brings a skilled bronze worker from Tyre, also named Hiram, to make the temple's bronze furnishings. Hiram casts two enormous bronze pillars for the temple porch, each about twenty-seven feet tall, topped with decorations of lilies and pomegranates. He names the right pillar Jachin, meaning he establishes, and the left pillar Boaz, meaning in him is strength. He casts a huge bronze basin called the Sea, about fifteen feet across, holding thousands of gallons of water. It rests on twelve bronze oxen, three facing each direction. He also makes ten bronze stands on wheels with basins for washing, along with pots, shovels, and bowls. There is so much bronze that it is never weighed.

Solomon also has the furnishings for the inside of the temple made of gold: the altar, the table for the holy bread, ten lampstands, and all the bowls, snuffers, and dishes. When the work is finished, Solomon brings in the silver, gold, and vessels his father David had dedicated and puts them in the temple treasury.

**God's Glory Fills the Temple (1 Kings 8:1–66)** — Solomon brings the ark into the temple, dedicates it through prayer, and celebrates God's faithfulness.

Solomon gathers all the leaders of Israel to Jerusalem to bring the ark of the covenant into the new temple. So many sheep and oxen are sacrificed that no one can count them. The priests carry the ark into the most holy place and set it under the wings of the cherubim. Inside the ark are only the two stone tablets Moses placed there at Mount Sinai. As the priests come out, “the cloud filled the house of the LORD, So that the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud: for the glory of the LORD had filled the house of the LORD.”

Solomon turns and blesses the people. Then he stands before the altar, spreads out his hands toward heaven, and prays. “LORD God of Israel, there is no God like thee, in heaven above, or on earth beneath.” He knows the temple cannot contain God: “But will God indeed dwell on the earth? behold, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee; how much less this house that I have builded?” Still, he asks God to watch over this place and listen when his people pray toward it.

He lists many situations. When someone sins against a neighbor, when Israel is defeated because of sin, when there is no rain, famine, or plague, when a foreigner comes to pray, or when Israel goes to war, “then hear thou in heaven thy dwelling place.” Solomon even looks ahead to a day when Israel sins and is carried away captive to a distant land. If they repent there and pray toward this land and this house, he asks God to forgive them. “For there is no man that sinneth not.”

When he finishes, Solomon stands and blesses the assembly: “Blessed be the LORD, that hath given rest unto his people Israel... there hath not failed one word of all his good promise.” He urges them, “Let your heart therefore be perfect with the LORD our God.” Then Solomon and the people offer 22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep and celebrate the feast for fourteen days. The people go home “joyful and glad of heart for all the goodness that the LORD had done.”

**God Warns Solomon to Remain Faithful (1 Kings 9:1–28)** — God accepts the temple but warns that disobedience and idolatry will bring judgment.

After Solomon finishes the temple and the palace, God appears to him a second time, as he did at Gibeon. God says he has heard Solomon's prayer and has made the temple holy: “mine eyes and mine heart shall be there perpetually.” If Solomon walks before him with integrity, as David did, God will establish his throne forever. But if Solomon or his sons turn away and worship other gods, “Then will I cut off Israel out of the land which I have given them; and this house... will I cast out of my sight.” People passing by will be astonished at its ruins and ask why. The answer will be, “Because they forsook the LORD their God.”

Solomon gives Hiram twenty towns in Galilee in return for the cedar and gold, but Hiram does not like them and calls them Cabul, meaning displeasing. Solomon builds and fortifies many cities, including Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer, which Pharaoh had captured and given as a wedding gift. He uses the remaining Canaanite peoples as forced laborers, but Israelites serve as soldiers and officials. Three times a year Solomon offers sacrifices at the temple. He also builds a fleet of ships at Eziongeber on the Red Sea, and with Hiram's experienced sailors they bring back about sixteen tons of gold from Ophir.

**The Queen of Sheba Visits Solomon (1 Kings 10:1–29)** — Solomon's wisdom and wealth draw international attention.

The queen of Sheba, from a land probably in southern Arabia or East Africa, hears about Solomon's fame and comes “to prove him with hard questions.” She arrives in Jerusalem with a huge caravan of camels carrying spices, gold, and precious stones. She asks Solomon everything on her mind, “and Solomon told her all her questions: there was not any thing hid from the king.”

When she sees his wisdom, his palace, the food on his table, his officials, his servants in their uniforms, and the burnt offerings he offers at the temple, “there was no more spirit in her.” She tells him, “It was a true report that I heard in mine own land of thy acts and of thy wisdom. Howbeit I believed not the words, until I came, and mine eyes had seen it: and, behold, the half was not told me.” She praises God for putting Solomon on the throne of Israel. She gives him about four and a half tons of gold, more spices than had ever been seen, and precious stones. Solomon gives her everything she asks for, and she goes home.

Solomon's wealth becomes legendary. He receives about twenty-five tons of gold every year. He makes two hundred large shields of hammered gold and a great ivory throne covered with gold, with six steps and twelve carved lions, “there was not the like made in any kingdom.” All his drinking cups are gold, because “it was nothing accounted of in the days of Solomon.” “So king Solomon exceeded all the kings of the earth for riches and for wisdom.” He gathers fourteen hundred chariots and twelve thousand horsemen and trades horses from Egypt. He makes silver as common in Jerusalem as stones. But the law of Moses had warned kings not to multiply horses from Egypt, silver, or gold.

**Solomon Turns Away from God (1 Kings 11:1–43)** — Solomon's foreign wives turn him to idolatry, and God announces the kingdom will be divided.

“But king Solomon loved many strange women,” women from foreign nations, along with Pharaoh's daughter. They come from Moab, Ammon, Edom, Sidon, and the Hittites, nations God had warned Israel not to marry. “He had seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines: and his wives turned away his heart.” When Solomon is old, they lead him to worship other gods, and “his heart was not perfect with the LORD his God, as was the heart of David his father.” He builds shrines on a hill east of Jerusalem for Chemosh, “the abomination of Moab,” and Molech, “the abomination of the children of Ammon,” and for all his foreign wives' gods.

God is angry with Solomon, who had seen God twice. He tells him, “I will surely rend the kingdom from thee, and will give it to thy servant.” But for David's sake, God will not do it during Solomon's lifetime, and he will leave one tribe for Solomon's son, for the sake of David and of Jerusalem.

God raises up enemies against Solomon: Hadad the Edomite, Rezon of Damascus, and one of Solomon's own officials, Jeroboam, a capable young man in charge of the labor force. One day the prophet Ahijah meets Jeroboam on the road outside Jerusalem. Ahijah takes off his new cloak and tears it into twelve pieces. He gives Jeroboam ten pieces, saying God will tear the kingdom from Solomon and give ten tribes to him. If Jeroboam obeys God as David did, God will build him a lasting dynasty. When Solomon hears this, he tries to kill Jeroboam, but Jeroboam flees to Egypt. Solomon dies after reigning forty years, and his son Rehoboam becomes king.

**Israel Divides into Two Kingdoms (1 Kings 12:1–33)** — Rehoboam rejects the people's request for relief, and ten tribes establish Jeroboam as king.

All Israel gathers at Shechem to make Rehoboam king. Jeroboam has come back from Egypt, and he and the people make a request: “Thy father made our yoke grievous: now therefore make thou the grievous service of thy father, and his heavy yoke which he put upon us, lighter, and we will serve thee.” Rehoboam tells them to come back in three days.

He asks the old men who had advised Solomon. They tell him, “If thou wilt be a servant unto this people this day... and speak good words to them, then they will be thy servants for ever.” But he rejects their advice and asks the young men he grew up with. They tell him to answer, “My little finger shall be thicker than my father's loins.” “My father hath chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions.” On the third day, Rehoboam gives that harsh answer.

The people of Israel reply, “What portion have we in David?... to your tents, O Israel: now see to thine own house, David.” The ten northern tribes break away. When Rehoboam sends his official in charge of forced labor, they stone him to death, and Rehoboam barely escapes in his chariot. The northern tribes make Jeroboam their king. Only Judah and Benjamin stay loyal to David's family. Rehoboam gathers an army to win back the north, but a prophet brings God's word: “ye shall not go up, nor fight against your brethren... for this thing is from me.” They obey and go home.

Now Jeroboam worries that if his people keep going to Jerusalem to worship at the temple, their hearts will return to Rehoboam. So he makes two golden calves. He puts one in Bethel, in the south of his kingdom, and the other in Dan, in the far north. He tells the people, “It is too much for you to go up to Jerusalem: behold thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.” These are almost exactly Aaron's words at the golden calf. Jeroboam builds shrines, appoints priests who are not Levites, and invents his own feast. “And this thing became a sin.” For the rest of the book, the kings of Israel are measured by whether they followed “the sins of Jeroboam.”

**A Prophet Warns Jeroboam About his Altar (1 Kings 13:1–34)** — A man of God condemns Jeroboam's altar, but his own disobedience leads to his death.

As Jeroboam stands by his new altar at Bethel to burn incense, a man of God arrives from Judah. He cries out against the altar and prophesies that a king from David's family named Josiah will one day burn the bones of these false priests on it. As a sign, the altar will split apart and its ashes will pour out. Jeroboam stretches out his hand and shouts, “Lay hold on him.” Instantly his hand withers and he cannot pull it back. The altar splits, and the ashes pour out. Jeroboam begs the man of God to pray for him, and his hand is restored. He invites the man home for a meal and a reward, but the man refuses. God had told him not to eat or drink there and to go home a different way.

An old prophet who lives in Bethel hears about it. He rides after the man of God and finds him sitting under an oak. He invites him home, and when the man repeats God's command, the old prophet lies: “I am a prophet also as thou art; and an angel spake unto me by the word of the LORD, saying, Bring him back.” So the man of God goes back and eats with him. While they are at the table, the word of the LORD comes to the old prophet, announcing that because the man disobeyed, his body will not be buried in his family's tomb. As the man of God rides away, a lion meets him on the road and kills him. The lion stands beside the body, with the donkey also standing there, not eating either one. The old prophet buries him in his own grave and mourns, “Alas, my brother!” He tells his sons to bury him beside the man of God, because his prophecy will surely come true.

Even after all this, “Jeroboam returned not from his evil way.” He keeps appointing anyone who wants to be a priest. “And this thing became sin unto the house of Jeroboam, even to cut it off.”

**Judgment Falls Upon Jeroboam and Judah (1 Kings 14:1–31)** — Judgment comes on Jeroboam's house, while Judah falls into idolatry under Rehoboam.

Jeroboam's son Abijah becomes sick. Jeroboam tells his wife to disguise herself and go to the prophet Ahijah, who first told him he would be king. Ahijah is now old and blind, but God warns him she is coming. When he hears her footsteps at the door, he says, “Come in, thou wife of Jeroboam; why feignest thou thyself to be another?” He has heavy news. Because Jeroboam has done more evil than all before him, making idols and casting God “behind thy back,” God will wipe out his family completely, “as a man taketh away dung, till it be all gone.” The boy will die as soon as she enters the city, but he will be the only one in the family to receive an honorable burial, “because in him there is found some good thing toward the LORD God of Israel.” As she steps across the threshold of her house, the child dies. Jeroboam reigns twenty-two years.

Meanwhile in Judah, Rehoboam's people also do evil. They build high places, sacred pillars, and idol poles “on every high hill, and under every green tree,” and there are male prostitutes at the shrines. In Rehoboam's fifth year, Shishak, king of Egypt, attacks Jerusalem and carries off the treasures of the temple and the palace, including all the gold shields Solomon had made. Rehoboam replaces them with bronze shields. There is constant war between Rehoboam and Jeroboam. Rehoboam dies and his son Abijam becomes king.

**Judah and Israel Follow Different Paths (1 Kings 15:1–16:34)** — Asa reforms Judah, while Israel sees violence, idolatry, and unstable leadership.

These chapters move quickly through many kings of both kingdoms. In Judah, Abijam follows his father's sins, but God keeps a lamp burning for David's sake in Jerusalem. Then comes Asa, who reigns forty-one years and “did that which was right in the eyes of the LORD, as did David his father.” He removes the shrine prostitutes and idols. He even removes his own grandmother from her position as queen mother because she made a disgusting idol, and he burns it. When the northern king attacks, Asa pays the king of Syria with temple treasures to attack Israel from the north.

In Israel, the kings are a troubled line. Jeroboam's son Nadab reigns two years before Baasha assassinates him and kills Jeroboam's whole family, fulfilling Ahijah's prophecy. But Baasha also walks in “the way of Jeroboam,” and a prophet announces the same fate for his family. His son Elah is murdered while drunk by Zimri, a chariot commander. Zimri reigns only seven days. When the army makes their general Omri king, Zimri goes into the palace, sets it on fire, and dies in the flames.

Omri builds a new capital city, Samaria, on a hill he buys, and “did worse than all that were before him.” His son Ahab becomes king, and Ahab “did evil in the sight of the LORD above all that were before him.” He marries Jezebel, daughter of the king of Sidon, and begins to worship Baal. He builds a temple and altar for Baal in Samaria and sets up an idol pole. “Ahab did more to provoke the LORD God of Israel to anger than all the kings of Israel that were before him.” In his days, a man named Hiel rebuilds Jericho, and he loses his oldest son when he lays its foundation and his youngest when he sets up its gates, just as Joshua had warned centuries before.

**Elijah Announces a Drought and Receives God's Provision (1 Kings 17:1–24)** — God sustains Elijah during the drought he announced and raises a widow's son through his prayer.

Suddenly a prophet appears: “Elijah the Tishbite.” He tells King Ahab, “As the LORD God of Israel liveth, before whom I stand, there shall not be dew nor rain these years, but according to my word.” Baal was supposed to be the god of rain and storms, so this is a direct challenge. God tells Elijah to hide by the brook Cherith, east of the Jordan. There he drinks from the brook, “And the ravens brought him bread and flesh in the morning, and bread and flesh in the evening.”

When the brook dries up, God sends Elijah to Zarephath, a town in Sidon, Jezebel's homeland, where a widow will feed him. At the town gate, he finds a widow gathering sticks and asks her for water and a piece of bread. She answers, “As the LORD thy God liveth, I have not a cake, but an handful of meal in a barrel, and a little oil in a cruse.” She is gathering sticks to make one last meal for herself and her son, “that we may eat it, and die.” Elijah says, “Fear not.” He tells her to make him a small cake first, and then make food for herself and her son, for God promises, “The barrel of meal shall not waste, neither shall the cruse of oil fail, until the day that the LORD sendeth rain upon the earth.” She does as he says, and the flour and oil never run out.

Later the widow's son becomes so sick that he stops breathing. She cries to Elijah, “art thou come unto me to call my sin to remembrance, and to slay my son?” Elijah carries the boy up to his room, lays him on his bed, and cries out to God. He stretches himself on the child three times and prays, “O LORD my God, I pray thee, let this child's soul come into him again.” God hears him, and the boy comes back to life. Elijah carries him down and says, “See, thy son liveth.” The woman says, “Now by this I know that thou art a man of God, and that the word of the LORD in thy mouth is truth.”

**Elijah Confronts the Prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18:1–46)** — At Mount Carmel, God answers Elijah with fire and ends the drought.

In the third year of the drought, God sends Elijah back to Ahab. The famine is severe. Ahab and his palace manager, Obadiah, have split up to search the whole land for grass for the horses. Obadiah fears the LORD, and when Jezebel was killing the LORD's prophets, he hid a hundred of them in two caves and fed them. Elijah meets him and tells him to announce that Elijah is here. When Ahab sees Elijah, he says, “Art thou he that troubleth Israel?” Elijah answers, “I have not troubled Israel; but thou, and thy father's house, in that ye have forsaken the commandments of the LORD, and thou hast followed Baalim.”

Elijah calls all Israel, along with 450 prophets of Baal and 400 prophets of Asherah, to Mount Carmel. He says to the people, “How long halt ye between two opinions? if the LORD be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him.” The people say nothing. So Elijah sets a test. Each side will prepare a bull on an altar but light no fire. “And the God that answereth by fire, let him be God.” The people agree.

The prophets of Baal go first. From morning until noon they cry, “O Baal, hear us,” and dance around their altar. Nothing happens. At noon Elijah mocks them: “Cry aloud: for he is a god; either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is in a journey, or peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked.” They shout louder and cut themselves with swords and knives until blood flows. They rave until evening, but “there was neither voice, nor any to answer, nor any that regarded.”

Then Elijah calls the people near. He repairs the LORD's broken-down altar with twelve stones, one for each tribe, and digs a trench around it. He lays the bull on the wood and has them pour four large jars of water over it, then again, and a third time, until the water fills the trench. At the time of the evening sacrifice, Elijah prays, “LORD God of Abraham, Isaac, and of Israel, let it be known this day that thou art God in Israel.” “Hear me, O LORD, hear me, that this people may know that thou art the LORD God.” “Then the fire of the LORD fell, and consumed the burnt sacrifice, and the wood, and the stones, and the dust, and licked up the water that was in the trench.” The people fall on their faces and cry, “The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God.” Elijah has the prophets of Baal seized, and he kills them at the brook Kishon.

Then Elijah tells Ahab, “there is a sound of abundance of rain.” He climbs to the top of Carmel, bows down with his face between his knees, and sends his servant to look toward the sea seven times. The seventh time, the servant says, “Behold, there ariseth a little cloud out of the sea, like a man's hand.” Soon the sky turns black with clouds and wind, and a great rain falls. Ahab rides his chariot toward his palace at Jezreel. But “the hand of the LORD was on Elijah,” and he tucks up his robe and runs ahead of Ahab's chariot all the way to Jezreel.

**God Encourages Elijah at Horeb (1 Kings 19:1–21)** — God strengthens a discouraged Elijah and gives him new work to do.

When Ahab tells Jezebel what Elijah has done, she sends him a message: by this time tomorrow, she will make his life like the lives of the dead prophets of Baal. Elijah, who stood alone against 850 prophets, runs for his life. He goes south to Beersheba, leaves his servant there, and travels a day into the wilderness. He sits under a juniper tree and prays to die: “It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life; for I am not better than my fathers.” Then he falls asleep. An angel touches him and says, “Arise and eat.” He finds bread baked on hot stones and a jar of water by his head. He eats and sleeps again. The angel comes a second time: “Arise and eat; because the journey is too great for thee.” Strengthened by that food, he travels forty days and nights to Horeb, the mountain of God, where God had appeared to Moses.

There, in a cave, God asks, “What doest thou here, Elijah?” Elijah answers that he has been zealous for the LORD, but Israel has broken God's covenant, torn down his altars, and killed his prophets, “and I, even I only, am left; and they seek my life.” God tells him to stand on the mountain. “And, behold, the LORD passed by, and a great and strong wind rent the mountains, and brake in pieces the rocks before the LORD; but the LORD was not in the wind: and after the wind an earthquake; but the LORD was not in the earthquake: And after the earthquake a fire; but the LORD was not in the fire: and after the fire a still small voice.” When Elijah hears it, he wraps his face in his cloak and stands at the cave's entrance.

God asks the same question again, and Elijah gives the same answer. God gives him new work: anoint Hazael as king of Syria, Jehu as king of Israel, and Elisha as the prophet who will take his place. And Elijah is not alone after all: “Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto Baal.”

Elijah finds Elisha plowing with twelve pairs of oxen. He walks by and throws his cloak over Elisha's shoulders, a sign of calling him. Elisha runs after him and asks to say goodbye to his parents first. Then he kills his oxen, cooks them over a fire made from the plow, and gives a farewell feast to the people. Then he follows Elijah and becomes his servant.

**Ahab Battles the Syrians (1 Kings 20:1–43)** — God gives Ahab victories over Syria, but Ahab spares the king God had condemned.

Benhadad, king of Syria, surrounds Samaria with a huge army and thirty-two allied kings. He demands Ahab's silver, gold, wives, and children. Ahab agrees at first, but when Benhadad demands even more, Ahab refuses. Benhadad boasts that he will reduce Samaria to dust. Ahab sends back a proverb: “Let not him that girdeth on his harness boast himself as he that putteth it off.” In other words, do not brag before the battle is won.

A prophet comes to Ahab and says God will give the huge army into his hand, “and thou shalt know that I am the LORD.” Ahab attacks with a small force while Benhadad is getting drunk with his kings, and the Syrians flee. The next year, the Syrians decide that Israel's God is a god of the hills, so they will fight in the plains. God sends another prophet to say that because the Syrians called him a god of the hills only, he will give them into Ahab's hand again. Israel kills a hundred thousand Syrian soldiers in one day, and a wall falls on twenty-seven thousand more.

Benhadad hides, then sends his servants to Ahab in sackcloth with ropes on their heads, begging for mercy. Ahab says, “he is my brother,” makes a treaty with him, and lets him go, even though God had devoted him to destruction. A prophet then disguises himself as a wounded soldier and tells Ahab a story about letting a prisoner escape. Ahab says the soldier deserves his punishment. The prophet pulls the bandage off his eyes and says, because Ahab let go a man God had devoted to destruction, “thy life shall go for his life, and thy people for his people.” Ahab goes home to Samaria “heavy and displeased.”

**Ahab Takes Naboth's Vineyard (1 Kings 21:1–29)** — Jezebel has Naboth killed so Ahab can seize his vineyard, and Elijah announces judgment.

Next to Ahab's palace in Jezreel is a vineyard that belongs to a man named Naboth. Ahab wants it for a vegetable garden and offers to buy it or trade for it. Naboth refuses: “The LORD forbid it me, that I should give the inheritance of my fathers unto thee.” Under God's law, family land was not supposed to be sold permanently. Ahab goes home sulking, lies on his bed, turns his face to the wall, and will not eat.

Jezebel says, “Dost thou now govern the kingdom of Israel? arise, and eat bread, and let thine heart be merry: I will give thee the vineyard.” She writes letters in Ahab's name, seals them with his seal, and sends them to the leaders of Naboth's town. She orders them to call a fast, seat Naboth in a place of honor, and bring two worthless men to accuse him falsely of cursing God and the king. They do exactly as she says. Naboth is taken outside the city and stoned to death, and his sons are killed with him (2 Kings 9:26). When Jezebel hears, she tells Ahab to go take the vineyard.

As Ahab goes down to claim it, Elijah meets him there. Ahab says, “Hast thou found me, O mine enemy?” Elijah answers, “I have found thee: because thou hast sold thyself to work evil in the sight of the LORD.” God's judgment is coming. “In the place where dogs licked the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick thy blood, even thine.” Ahab's whole family will be wiped out, and “The dogs shall eat Jezebel by the wall of Jezreel.” The book adds, “But there was none like unto Ahab, which did sell himself to work wickedness in the sight of the LORD, whom Jezebel his wife stirred up.”

Yet when Ahab hears Elijah's words, he tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth, fasts, and walks around quietly in sorrow. God tells Elijah, “Seest thou how Ahab humbleth himself before me?” Because he has humbled himself, God will not bring the disaster on his family during his lifetime, but during his son's.

**Ahab Dies in Battle and Ahaziah Succeeds Him (1 Kings 22:1–53)** — Ahab rejects Micaiah's warning and dies at Ramothgilead.

After three years of peace with Syria, Ahab wants to take back the city of Ramoth-gilead. Jehoshaphat, the good king of Judah, agrees to join him but asks him to seek the LORD's guidance first. Ahab gathers about four hundred prophets, who all tell him, “Go up; for the Lord shall deliver it into the hand of the king.” But Jehoshaphat asks, “Is there not here a prophet of the LORD besides, that we might enquire of him?” Ahab says there is one, Micaiah, “but I hate him; for he doth not prophesy good concerning me, but evil.”

They send for Micaiah, and the messenger urges him to agree with the others. Micaiah says, “As the LORD liveth, what the LORD saith unto me, that will I speak.” At first he mockingly tells Ahab to go and succeed. When Ahab demands the truth, he says, “I saw all Israel scattered upon the hills, as sheep that have not a shepherd.” Then he describes a vision of God's heavenly court, where a spirit offers to be “a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets” to lure Ahab to his death. One of the false prophets slaps Micaiah in the face. Ahab orders Micaiah thrown in prison and fed only bread and water until he returns safely. Micaiah answers, “If thou return at all in peace, the LORD hath not spoken by me.”

Ahab goes into battle disguised as an ordinary soldier, while Jehoshaphat wears his royal robes. The Syrian king orders his chariot commanders to fight only against the king of Israel. They chase Jehoshaphat until he cries out and they realize he is not Ahab. Then “a certain man drew a bow at a venture,” shooting at random, and the arrow strikes Ahab between the joints of his armor. Ahab tells his driver to get him out of the battle. He stays propped up in his chariot, facing the Syrians, until evening, while his blood runs into the chariot floor. At sunset he dies. They bring his body to Samaria and bury him, and when they wash his chariot by the pool of Samaria, the dogs lick up his blood, just as the LORD had said.

The book ends with summaries of two kings. Jehoshaphat of Judah reigns twenty-five years and does what is right, though he does not remove the high places. Ahab's son Ahaziah becomes king of Israel and walks in the ways of his father and mother, serving Baal.

# 2 Kings
When it happened: From about 850 BC to the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, ending with King Jehoiachin's release in Babylon in the thirty-seventh year of his exile, about 561 BC (2 Kings 25:27).
When it was written: The continuation of 1 Kings, with which it was originally one book. Jewish tradition credits Jeremiah; the finished work dates from the exile, about 560 BC or later.
Where: Samaria, Jerusalem, the Jordan valley, Damascus in Syria, Assyria, and Babylon.
The world at the time: The Assyrian Empire rose to dominate the region and destroyed Samaria and the northern kingdom in 722 BC. A century later Babylon replaced Assyria, and Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Jerusalem and the temple.

**Elijah Confronts King Ahaziah (2 Kings 1:1–18)** — After an injury, Ahaziah seeks a foreign god, and Elijah announces his death.

King Ahaziah of Israel falls through a lattice in an upper room of his palace and is badly hurt. He sends messengers to ask Baalzebub, the god of the Philistine city of Ekron, whether he will recover. But the angel of the LORD sends Elijah to meet the messengers with a question: “Is it not because there is not a God in Israel, that ye go to enquire of Baalzebub the god of Ekron?” Elijah tells them the king will not get up from his bed, “but shalt surely die.” The messengers go back and describe the man who met them: “He was an hairy man, and girt with a girdle of leather about his loins.” Ahaziah says, “It is Elijah the Tishbite.”

The king sends a captain with fifty soldiers to arrest Elijah, who is sitting on a hilltop. The captain says, “Thou man of God, the king hath said, Come down.” Elijah answers, “If I be a man of God, then let fire come down from heaven, and consume thee and thy fifty.” Fire falls and burns them up. A second captain with fifty men comes and gets the same result. The third captain falls on his knees and begs for his life and the lives of his men. The angel tells Elijah to go with him without fear. Elijah goes to the king and repeats God's message to his face. Ahaziah dies, and since he has no son, his brother Jehoram becomes king.

**Elijah Is Taken to Heaven (2 Kings 2:1–25)** — Elijah is carried into heaven, and Elisha receives his mantle and continues his work.

The time has come for God to take Elijah up to heaven in a whirlwind. Elijah and Elisha are walking from Gilgal. Three times Elijah tells Elisha to stay behind while he goes on to Bethel, then Jericho, then the Jordan. Each time Elisha answers, “As the LORD liveth, and as thy soul liveth, I will not leave thee.” At each town, groups of young prophets tell Elisha that God will take his master today. Elisha says, “Yea, I know it; hold ye your peace.”

At the Jordan, Elijah rolls up his cloak and strikes the water. The river divides, and the two of them cross on dry ground. Elijah asks Elisha what he can do for him before he is taken. Elisha says, “I pray thee, let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me.” Elijah says it is a hard thing, but if Elisha sees him taken, it will be so. As they walk and talk, “behold, there appeared a chariot of fire, and horses of fire, and parted them both asunder; and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven.” Elisha cries out, “My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof.” Then he sees him no more. He tears his own clothes in grief.

Elisha picks up Elijah's cloak, which has fallen, and goes back to the Jordan. He strikes the water and asks, “Where is the LORD God of Elijah?” The river parts again, and he crosses. The prophets at Jericho say, “The spirit of Elijah doth rest on Elisha,” and they bow to him. They insist on sending fifty men to search for Elijah's body for three days, but they find nothing.

The men of Jericho tell Elisha that their water is bad. He throws salt into the spring, and the water is healed. Then, on the road to Bethel, a gang of young men comes out of the city and mocks him: “Go up, thou bald head; go up, thou bald head.” They are mocking him to go up to heaven as Elijah did. Elisha turns and curses them in the name of the LORD. Two female bears come out of the woods and maul forty-two of them.

**God Delivers Israel and Judah from Moab (2 Kings 3:1–27)** — Elisha promises water and victory when three kings march against Moab.

Jehoram, Ahab's son, reigns over Israel. He gets rid of his father's Baal pillar but still follows the sins of Jeroboam. When the king of Moab rebels and stops paying his yearly tribute of sheep and wool, Jehoram asks Jehoshaphat, king of Judah, and the king of Edom to join him in war. They take a long route through the desert of Edom, and after seven days there is no water for the army or the animals.

Jehoshaphat asks, “Is there not here a prophet of the LORD?” They find Elisha. He tells the king of Israel that he would not even look at him if it were not for Jehoshaphat. He calls for a musician, and as the music plays, God's power comes on him. He tells them to dig ditches all over the dry valley. They will see no wind or rain, yet the valley will be filled with water. And God will also give them victory over Moab.

The next morning water flows in from the direction of Edom and fills the ditches. When the Moabites look across at sunrise, the sun shining on the water makes it look red like blood. They think the three kings have fought and killed each other, so they rush in to collect the loot, and Israel attacks and drives them back. In desperation, the king of Moab takes his oldest son, who would have been king after him, and offers him as a burnt offering on the city wall. There is great anger against Israel, and the Israelite army withdraws and goes home.

**Elisha Provides for Those in Need (2 Kings 4:1–44)** — God works through Elisha to provide for a widow, a Shunammite family, and the prophets.

Elisha performs many miracles to help ordinary people. A widow of one of the prophets comes to him crying. Her husband has died in debt, and the man he owed is coming to take her two sons as slaves. All she has is a small jar of olive oil. Elisha tells her to borrow empty jars from all her neighbors, “borrow not a few.” She shuts the door and pours oil from her little jar into the empty ones. The oil keeps flowing until every jar is full. Then it stops. Elisha tells her to sell the oil, pay her debt, and live on the rest.

In the town of Shunem, a wealthy woman often feeds Elisha when he passes through. She and her husband build a small room on their roof for him. Elisha wants to repay her, and his servant Gehazi points out that she has no son and her husband is old. Elisha tells her that next year she will hold a son in her arms. She says, “Nay, my lord, thou man of God, do not lie unto thine handmaid.” But the next year she has a son.

When the boy is older, he goes out to his father in the fields and cries, “My head, my head.” He is carried to his mother and dies in her lap at noon. She lays him on Elisha's bed, shuts the door, and rides quickly to find the prophet at Mount Carmel. She tells Gehazi, “It is well,” but at Elisha's feet she pours out her grief. Elisha sends Gehazi ahead with his staff to lay on the boy's face, but nothing happens. Then Elisha arrives, shuts the door, and prays. He lies on the child, mouth to mouth, eyes to eyes, hands to hands, and the boy's body grows warm. He walks back and forth in the house, then stretches himself on the boy again. The child sneezes seven times and opens his eyes. Elisha calls the mother and says, “Take up thy son.”

During a famine at Gilgal, someone puts wild poisonous gourds in a stew for the prophets. They cry, “O thou man of God, there is death in the pot.” Elisha throws in some flour, and the stew becomes safe to eat. Then a man brings twenty barley loaves, and Elisha tells his servant to feed a hundred men with them. The servant doubts, but Elisha says, “They shall eat, and shall leave thereof.” They eat, and there is food left over, as the LORD had said.

**Naaman Is Healed of his Leprosy (2 Kings 5:1–27)** — A Syrian commander is healed at Elisha's word, while Elisha's servant is struck for his greed.

Naaman is the commander of the Syrian army, a great man and a mighty warrior, “but he was a leper.” In his house is a young Israelite girl who was captured in a raid and now serves his wife. She says, “Would God my lord were with the prophet that is in Samaria! for he would recover him of his leprosy.” The king of Syria sends Naaman to the king of Israel with a letter and a fortune in silver, gold, and clothing. The king of Israel tears his clothes in panic: “Am I God, to kill and to make alive?” He thinks the Syrians are picking a fight. Elisha sends word: “let him come now to me, and he shall know that there is a prophet in Israel.”

Naaman arrives at Elisha's door with his horses and chariots. Elisha does not even come out. He sends a messenger: “Go and wash in Jordan seven times, and thy flesh shall come again to thee, and thou shalt be clean.” Naaman is furious. He expected the prophet to come out, call on his God, and wave his hand over the spot. “Are not Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel?” He storms off in a rage. But his servants reason with him: if the prophet had asked him to do something great, he would have done it. “How much rather then, when he saith to thee, Wash, and be clean?” So Naaman goes down and dips himself seven times in the Jordan, “and his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child, and he was clean.”

Naaman goes back to Elisha and says, “Behold, now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel.” He offers a gift, but Elisha refuses to take anything. Naaman asks for two mule loads of Israel's soil to take home so he can worship the LORD on it. Elisha tells him, “Go in peace.”

But Elisha's servant Gehazi thinks, “my master hath spared Naaman.” He runs after Naaman, lies that two young prophets have arrived and need silver and clothes, and Naaman gladly gives him even more than he asks. Gehazi hides the gifts in his house. When he returns, Elisha asks where he has been. Gehazi says, “Thy servant went no whither.” Elisha says, “Went not mine heart with thee?” Because of his greed and lies, Naaman's leprosy will cling to Gehazi and his descendants forever. “And he went out from his presence a leper as white as snow.”

**God Protects Elisha from the Syrians (2 Kings 6:1–23)** — God shows Elisha's servant his heavenly army and turns enemies into guests.

The group of prophets with Elisha is outgrowing their meeting place, so they go to the Jordan to cut beams for a bigger one. As one man is chopping, the iron axe head flies off and falls into the water. He cries out, “Alas, master! for it was borrowed.” Elisha asks where it fell, cuts a stick, and throws it in at that spot, “and the iron did swim.” The man reaches out and picks it up.

The king of Syria is at war with Israel. Every time he plans an ambush, Elisha warns the king of Israel where the Syrians will be. The Syrian king thinks there is a traitor in his camp. His servants tell him, “Elisha, the prophet that is in Israel, telleth the king of Israel the words that thou speakest in thy bedchamber.” So the king sends a large army with horses and chariots by night to surround the town of Dothan, where Elisha is staying.

Early the next morning, Elisha's servant goes out and sees the army all around the city. “Alas, my master! how shall we do?” Elisha answers, “Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them.” Then he prays, “LORD, I pray thee, open his eyes, that he may see.” God opens the young man's eyes, “and, behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha.”

As the Syrians come down toward him, Elisha prays for God to strike them with blindness, and he does. Elisha tells them, “This is not the way, neither is this the city: follow me,” and he leads them straight into Samaria, Israel's capital. Then he prays for their eyes to be opened, and they find themselves surrounded. The king of Israel asks, “My father, shall I smite them?” Elisha says no. Instead, the king should give them food and water and send them home. The king prepares a great feast for them and sends them back to their master. After that, the Syrian raiding bands stop coming into Israel.

**God Ends the Famine in Samaria (2 Kings 6:24–7:20)** — During a terrible siege, God ends the famine overnight, as Elisha had promised.

Later, Benhadad king of Syria surrounds Samaria with his whole army, and the city suffers a terrible famine. Food becomes so scarce that a donkey's head sells for eighty pieces of silver. As the king walks on the wall, a woman cries out to him. She and another woman had agreed to eat their sons. They ate hers one day, and now the other woman has hidden her son. When the king hears this, he tears his clothes, and the people see that he is wearing sackcloth underneath. In his anger, he swears to cut off Elisha's head.

Elisha announces that by this time tomorrow, food will be sold cheaply at the city gate. The king's officer scoffs: even if God made windows in heaven, could this happen? Elisha answers, “Behold, thou shalt see it with thine eyes, but shalt not eat thereof.”

Outside the city gate sit four men with leprosy. They say to each other, “Why sit we here until we die?” If they go into the city, they will starve. If they stay, they will die. So they decide to go to the Syrian camp. If the Syrians spare them, they live; if not, they die. At twilight they walk into the camp and find it completely empty. God had made the Syrians hear the sound of chariots, horses, and a great army, and they thought Israel had hired the Hittites and Egyptians to attack. They ran for their lives, leaving their tents, horses, donkeys, food, and treasure.

The four lepers eat and drink and hide silver and gold. Then they say, “We do not well: this day is a day of good tidings, and we hold our peace.” They go and tell the city gatekeepers. The king fears a trap, but he sends scouts, who find a trail of clothing and equipment the Syrians had thrown away as they fled. The people rush out and plunder the camp. Flour and barley sell cheaply at the gate, just as Elisha said. The king puts the officer who doubted in charge of the gate, and the crowd tramples him to death. He saw the food but never ate it.

**Elisha's Influence Reaches Two Kingdoms (2 Kings 8:1–29)** — Elisha's words shape events in Israel, Syria, and Judah.

Elisha had warned the Shunammite woman, whose son he raised from the dead, that a seven-year famine was coming and she should leave the land. She lived among the Philistines for seven years. When she comes back, she goes to the king to ask for her house and land. At that very moment, the king is asking Gehazi to tell him about Elisha's great deeds, and Gehazi is telling the story of the boy raised to life. Just then the woman walks in. Gehazi says, “My lord, O king, this is the woman, and this is her son, whom Elisha restored to life.” The king gives her back everything, including all the crops her field produced while she was gone.

Elisha goes to Damascus, where Benhadad, king of Syria, is sick. The king sends his official Hazael with forty camel loads of gifts to ask whether he will recover. Elisha tells Hazael that the king could recover from the illness but will surely die. Then Elisha stares at Hazael until Hazael is embarrassed, and Elisha begins to weep. Hazael asks why. Elisha says he knows the evil Hazael will do to Israel. He will burn their fortresses and kill their young men and children. Hazael says, “But what, is thy servant a dog, that he should do this great thing?” Elisha answers that God has shown him Hazael will be king of Syria. Hazael goes back and tells Benhadad he will recover. The next day he takes a thick cloth, soaks it in water, and spreads it over the king's face until he dies. Then Hazael becomes king.

In Judah, Jehoram, the son of good King Jehoshaphat, marries Ahab's daughter and walks in the ways of the kings of Israel. Edom rebels against Judah's rule. Then his son Ahaziah becomes king, also following Ahab's ways. Ahaziah joins his uncle, King Jehoram of Israel, in war against Hazael. When Jehoram is wounded, Ahaziah goes to visit him at Jezreel.

**Jehu Destroys the House of Ahab (2 Kings 9:1–10:36)** — Jehu carries out God's judgment on Ahab's house and Baal worship.

Elisha sends a young prophet to Ramoth-gilead to anoint Jehu, an army commander, as king of Israel. The young man takes Jehu into a private room, pours oil on his head, and gives God's command: Jehu must strike down the house of Ahab to avenge the blood of the prophets Jezebel killed. Then the prophet runs away. Jehu's fellow officers ask, “wherefore came this mad fellow to thee?” When he tells them, they spread their cloaks on the stairs, blow a trumpet, and shout, “Jehu is king.”

Jehu races his chariot toward Jezreel, where King Jehoram is recovering. The watchman sees him coming: “the driving is like the driving of Jehu the son of Nimshi; for he driveth furiously.” Jehoram and King Ahaziah of Judah ride out to meet him. Jehoram asks, “Is it peace, Jehu?” Jehu answers, “What peace, so long as the whoredoms of thy mother Jezebel and her witchcrafts are so many?” Jehoram turns to flee, shouting “Treachery,” but Jehu draws his bow and shoots him through the heart. His body is thrown on Naboth's field, just as Elijah prophesied. Ahaziah flees, but he is also shot and dies.

When Jehu rides into Jezreel, Jezebel paints her eyes, does her hair, and looks out of an upper window. She calls down to him with a sneer. Jehu looks up and shouts, “Who is on my side? who?” Two or three servants look out. He says, “Throw her down.” They throw her out the window, and her blood splatters on the wall and on the horses, and Jehu drives over her. After he eats, he tells them to bury her, “for she is a king's daughter.” But they find only her skull, feet, and hands. Jehu says this fulfills Elijah's word: “In the portion of Jezreel shall dogs eat the flesh of Jezebel.”

Jehu writes to the leaders of Samaria, who then kill Ahab's seventy sons and send their heads to him in baskets. He kills everyone left in Ahab's family and all his officials and priests. Then he announces that he will serve Baal even more than Ahab did, and he calls all of Baal's worshipers to a great sacrifice in Baal's temple. Once they are all inside, he has his soldiers kill every one of them. They tear down the temple and turn it into a latrine. “Thus Jehu destroyed Baal out of Israel.” But Jehu still keeps Jeroboam's golden calves at Bethel and Dan. God tells him that because he has done what was right in destroying Ahab's house, his descendants will rule to the fourth generation. “But Jehu took no heed to walk in the law of the LORD God of Israel with all his heart.”

**Joash Becomes King and Repairs the Temple (2 Kings 11:1–12:21)** — A hidden prince is crowned, and covenant worship is restored in Judah.

When Athaliah, Ahab's daughter and the mother of King Ahaziah of Judah, sees that her son is dead, she seizes the throne and murders the entire royal family. But Ahaziah's sister Jehosheba rescues his baby son Joash and hides him with his nurse in a bedroom of the temple. For six years, Joash stays hidden in God's house while Athaliah rules Judah.

In the seventh year, Jehoiada the priest gathers the captains of the guard and shows them the young prince. He arranges for the guards to surround the temple on the Sabbath, each with his weapon in hand. Then he brings out the boy, puts the crown on his head, and gives him a copy of God's law. They anoint him, clap their hands, and shout, “God save the king.” Athaliah hears the noise and rushes into the temple. She sees the boy king standing by the pillar, surrounded by captains and trumpeters, with all the people rejoicing. She tears her clothes and cries, “Treason, Treason.” Jehoiada orders the guards to take her out of the temple, and she is executed at the horse gate of the palace. Jehoiada leads the people in a covenant to be the LORD's people. They tear down the temple of Baal and kill its priest.

Joash is only seven when he becomes king. He reigns forty years and “did that which was right in the sight of the LORD all his days wherein Jehoiada the priest instructed him.” He orders the priests to collect money to repair the temple, but years later the repairs still have not been done. So Jehoiada drills a hole in the lid of a chest and sets it beside the altar, and the people drop their offerings into it. The money is counted and paid directly to the carpenters and builders, “for they dealt faithfully.” Later, when Hazael of Syria threatens Jerusalem, Joash buys him off with temple treasures. In the end, Joash is assassinated by his own servants.

**Elisha Dies as Israel Faces Continued Conflict (2 Kings 13:1–25)** — Even at his death, Elisha points Israel to God's deliverance.

Jehu's son Jehoahaz becomes king of Israel and does evil, so God lets Hazael of Syria oppress Israel. Jehoahaz prays, and God sends a deliverer, but the people keep worshiping at Jeroboam's shrines. By the end, Israel's army is down to fifty horsemen, ten chariots, and ten thousand foot soldiers. His son Jehoash, also called Joash, becomes king next.

Elisha becomes deathly ill. King Jehoash comes to him and weeps over him, crying out, “O my father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof,” the same words Elisha had cried when Elijah was taken. Elisha tells the king to take a bow and arrows. Elisha puts his hands over the king's hands and tells him to shoot an arrow out the east window. “The arrow of the LORD'S deliverance,” he says, “and the arrow of deliverance from Syria.” Then he tells the king to strike the ground with the arrows. The king strikes three times and stops. Elisha is angry: “Thou shouldest have smitten five or six times; then hadst thou smitten Syria till thou hadst consumed it.” Now he will defeat Syria only three times.

Elisha dies and is buried. Some time later, men are burying a dead man when they see a band of Moabite raiders. In a panic, they throw the body into Elisha's tomb. “And when the man was let down, and touched the bones of Elisha, he revived, and stood up on his feet.” After Hazael dies, Jehoash defeats Syria three times and recovers the towns his father had lost, just as Elisha said.

**Judah and Israel Experience Changing Fortunes (2 Kings 14:1–29)** — Amaziah's pride brings defeat, while Jeroboam II restores Israel's borders.

Amaziah becomes king of Judah and does what is right, though not like David. He executes the officials who murdered his father, but following the law of Moses, he does not kill their children. He defeats the Edomites in battle and becomes proud. He sends a challenge to King Jehoash of Israel: “Come, let us look one another in the face.” Jehoash answers with a parable about a thistle in Lebanon that demanded a cedar's daughter for its son, only to be trampled by a wild animal. “Thou hast indeed smitten Edom, and thine heart hath lifted thee up: glory of this, and tarry at home.” Amaziah will not listen. In the battle, Judah is defeated, and Jehoash captures Amaziah, breaks down part of Jerusalem's wall, and takes the gold, silver, and vessels from the temple and palace. Later, Amaziah is assassinated.

In Israel, Jeroboam II becomes king and reigns forty-one years. He does evil, but God uses him to restore Israel's borders, as God had promised through the prophet Jonah, the same Jonah who was sent to Nineveh. “For the LORD saw the affliction of Israel, that it was very bitter.” Israel becomes strong and prosperous again for a time. The prophets Amos and Hosea preached during this period.

**Israel's Kings Lead the Nation Toward Destruction (2 Kings 15:1–38)** — Judah has steadier kings while Israel falls into a cycle of assassination.

In Judah, Azariah, also called Uzziah, reigns fifty-two years and does what is right. But God strikes him with leprosy, and he lives in a separate house until he dies, while his son Jotham governs the people. Jotham also does what is right.

In Israel, things fall apart quickly. Jeroboam's son Zachariah reigns only six months before Shallum assassinates him in public. This ends Jehu's family after four generations, just as God promised. Shallum reigns only one month before Menahem kills him. Menahem is cruel. When a town refuses to open its gates, he attacks it and rips open all the pregnant women. Then Pul, king of Assyria, invades, and Menahem pays him about thirty-seven tons of silver, taxing every wealthy man in Israel, to keep his throne. Menahem's son Pekahiah reigns two years before his officer Pekah assassinates him.

During Pekah's reign, Tiglathpileser, king of Assyria, invades and captures much of northern Israel, including Galilee, and carries the people away to Assyria. This is the first deportation of Israel. Then Hoshea assassinates Pekah and becomes king. Each of these kings “did that which was evil in the sight of the LORD: he departed not from the sins of Jeroboam.”

**King Ahaz Turns Judah Toward False Worship (2 Kings 16:1–20)** — Ahaz turns to idols and to Assyria instead of to God.

Ahaz becomes king of Judah at twenty and reigns sixteen years. He does not do what is right like David. He even burns his own son as a sacrifice, following the disgusting practices of the nations God drove out of the land. He offers sacrifices on the high places, on the hills, “and under every green tree.”

When the kings of Syria and Israel attack Jerusalem, Ahaz sends messengers to Tiglathpileser of Assyria: “I am thy servant and thy son: come up, and save me.” He sends him silver and gold from the temple and the palace treasuries. The Assyrian king attacks Damascus, captures it, and kills its king.

Ahaz goes to Damascus to meet Tiglathpileser. There he sees a pagan altar he likes and sends its design to Uriah the priest, who builds a copy in Jerusalem. When Ahaz returns, he offers sacrifices on the new altar and moves God's bronze altar aside for his own private use. He also takes apart some of the temple furnishings and changes the temple to please the king of Assyria. Ahaz dies, and his son Hezekiah becomes king.

**Assyria Conquers the Northern Kingdom of Israel (2 Kings 17:1–41)** — Israel is carried into exile because of persistent idolatry and rejection of God's prophets.

Hoshea becomes the last king of Israel. At first he serves Shalmaneser, king of Assyria, and pays tribute. But then he secretly asks Egypt for help and stops paying. The king of Assyria throws Hoshea in prison and invades the whole land. He besieges Samaria for three years. “In the ninth year of Hoshea the king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria.” This happened in 722 BC. The northern kingdom of Israel is gone.

The book stops to explain why. It happened because “the children of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, which had brought them up out of the land of Egypt.” They worshiped other gods and followed the customs of the nations. They built high places in all their towns, set up idols “on every high hill, and under every green tree,” and burned incense like the pagans. God had warned them again and again through his prophets, “Turn ye from your evil ways,” but they would not listen. They became stubborn like their ancestors. They made the two golden calves, worshiped the stars, served Baal, burned their sons and daughters in the fire, and practiced witchcraft. “Therefore the LORD was very angry with Israel, and removed them out of his sight: there was none left but the tribe of Judah only.”

The king of Assyria brings people from Babylon and other conquered lands to live in Samaria in place of the Israelites. When lions attack them, they think it is because they do not know how to worship the god of the land. So the king sends back one of Israel's priests to teach them. But they mix the worship of the LORD with the worship of their own gods. “They feared the LORD, and served their own gods.” These mixed people became the ancestors of the Samaritans in Jesus' day.

**Hezekiah Restores Worship and Trusts God (2 Kings 18:1–19:37)** — Hezekiah trusts God when Assyria threatens Jerusalem, and God delivers the city.

Hezekiah becomes king of Judah at twenty-five. “He did that which was right in the sight of the LORD, according to all that David his father did.” He tears down the high places, smashes the sacred pillars, and cuts down the idol poles. He even breaks into pieces the bronze serpent Moses made in the wilderness, because the people have been burning incense to it. “He trusted in the LORD God of Israel; so that after him was none like him among all the kings of Judah, nor any that were before him.” He rebels against the king of Assyria and refuses to serve him.

In Hezekiah's fourteenth year, Sennacherib, king of Assyria, invades Judah and captures its fortified cities. Hezekiah pays a huge tribute, even stripping the gold from the temple doors. But Sennacherib sends his commander, the Rabshakeh, with a great army to Jerusalem anyway. Standing outside the city wall, the Rabshakeh shouts in Hebrew so that all the people can hear: “What confidence is this wherein thou trustest?” He mocks their trust in Egypt and in the LORD. He says no god of any nation has been able to save its people from Assyria. “Let not Hezekiah deceive you.” The people stay silent, as Hezekiah ordered.

Hezekiah tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth, and goes into the temple. He sends officials to the prophet Isaiah, who answers, “Be not afraid of the words which thou hast heard.” God will make Sennacherib hear a rumor and return home, where he will die by the sword. Then Sennacherib sends Hezekiah a threatening letter. Hezekiah takes it into the temple and spreads it out before the LORD. He prays, “O LORD God of Israel, which dwellest between the cherubims, thou art the God, even thou alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth.” He admits that Assyria has destroyed other nations and their gods, “for they were no gods, but the work of men's hands.” “Now therefore, O LORD our God, I beseech thee, save thou us out of his hand, that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that thou art the LORD God, even thou only.”

Isaiah sends God's answer: Sennacherib has mocked the Holy One of Israel. “I will put my hook in thy nose, and my bridle in thy lips, and I will turn thee back by the way by which thou camest.” He will not shoot a single arrow into Jerusalem. “For I will defend this city, to save it, for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake.” That night, “the angel of the LORD went out, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians an hundred fourscore and five thousand,” 185,000 soldiers, and in the morning they are all dead. Sennacherib goes home to Nineveh. Later, while he is worshiping in the temple of his god, two of his sons kill him with the sword.

**God Extends Hezekiah's Life (2 Kings 20:1–21)** — God answers Hezekiah's prayer for healing, but his pride toward Babylon brings a warning.

Hezekiah becomes sick and is about to die. Isaiah comes to him and says, “Set thine house in order; for thou shalt die, and not live.” Hezekiah turns his face to the wall and prays, “Remember now how I have walked before thee in truth and with a perfect heart,” and he weeps bitterly. Before Isaiah has even left the palace courtyard, God sends him back with a new message: “I have heard thy prayer, I have seen thy tears: behold, I will heal thee.” God will add fifteen years to his life and defend Jerusalem from Assyria. Isaiah has them put a lump of figs on Hezekiah's boil, and he recovers.

Hezekiah asks for a sign that he will really be healed. Isaiah offers to make the shadow on the stairway of Ahaz go forward or backward ten steps. Hezekiah says it would be easy for the shadow to go forward, so let it go back. Isaiah prays, and the shadow moves backward ten steps.

Then messengers come from Merodachbaladan, the king of Babylon, with letters and a gift, because they have heard that Hezekiah was sick. Hezekiah is flattered and shows them everything in his treasure house: his silver, gold, spices, oil, armory, and everything in his storehouses. Isaiah asks, “What have they seen in thine house?” Hezekiah answers, “All the things that are in mine house have they seen.” Isaiah tells him that a day is coming when everything in the palace “shall be carried into Babylon: nothing shall be left,” and some of his own descendants will be taken away to serve in the palace of the king of Babylon. Hezekiah says, “Good is the word of the LORD which thou hast spoken,” thinking, “Is it not good, if peace and truth be in my days?” The chapter also mentions that Hezekiah built a tunnel to bring water into the city. That tunnel can still be walked through in Jerusalem today.

**Manasseh Leads Judah into Great Wickedness (2 Kings 21:1–26)** — Manasseh reverses Hezekiah's reforms and fills Jerusalem with idolatry and bloodshed.

Hezekiah's son Manasseh becomes king at only twelve years old and reigns fifty-five years, longer than any other king of Judah. He is also the worst. He rebuilds the high places his father destroyed, sets up altars to Baal, and makes an idol pole as Ahab did. He worships the stars and builds altars to them right inside the courts of the temple. He burns his own son in the fire, practices witchcraft, consults mediums, and places a carved idol in the temple. He leads Judah to do more evil than the nations God destroyed before Israel. “Moreover Manasseh shed innocent blood very much, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another.” Jewish tradition says he had the prophet Isaiah sawn in two.

God speaks through his prophets: because of Manasseh's sins, he will bring such disaster on Jerusalem and Judah that whoever hears of it will have both ears tingle. “I will wipe Jerusalem as a man wipeth a dish, wiping it, and turning it upside down.” God will abandon them to their enemies.

Manasseh's son Amon becomes king and is just as wicked. After two years, his own servants assassinate him in his palace. The people of the land execute the assassins and make Amon's eight-year-old son, Josiah, king.

**Josiah Rediscovers God's Law and Begins Reform (2 Kings 22:1–23:30)** — Josiah responds to the rediscovered law with humility, covenant renewal, and sweeping reform.

Josiah is only eight years old when he becomes king, and he reigns thirty-one years. “And he did that which was right in the sight of the LORD, and walked in all the way of David his father, and turned not aside to the right hand or to the left.” In his eighteenth year, he sends his secretary Shaphan to the temple to pay the workers repairing it. There, Hilkiah the high priest says, “I have found the book of the law in the house of the LORD.” It had been lost and forgotten during the years of idolatry.

Shaphan reads it to the king. When Josiah hears the words of the law, he tears his clothes. He realizes how far the nation has fallen and how angry God must be. He sends officials to ask God what to do. They go to Huldah the prophetess, who says disaster will indeed come on Jerusalem. But because Josiah's heart was tender and he humbled himself, he will die in peace before it comes.

Josiah gathers all the people, from the least to the greatest, and reads the whole book of the covenant to them. He stands by a pillar and makes a covenant to follow the LORD with all his heart and soul, and all the people join in. Then he cleans out the land. He removes from the temple all the objects made for Baal, Asherah, and the stars, and burns them. He tears down the houses of the male shrine prostitutes inside the temple. He destroys the valley of Topheth, where children were burned to Molech. He tears down the altars Manasseh built and the high places Solomon built for foreign gods. He even goes to Bethel and destroys Jeroboam's altar there. He digs up the bones of the false priests and burns them on the altar, just as the man of God from Judah had prophesied three hundred years earlier, naming Josiah. Then Josiah celebrates a Passover like none since the days of the judges.

“And like unto him was there no king before him, that turned to the LORD with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might, according to all the law of Moses.” Yet God's anger over Manasseh's sins is not turned away. When Pharaoh Necho of Egypt marches north, Josiah goes out to stop him at Megiddo, and Necho kills him there. His servants carry his body home in a chariot and bury him in Jerusalem.

**Judah's Final Kings Lead the Nation Toward Exile (2 Kings 23:31–24:20)** — Judah's last kings fall under Egypt and then Babylon.

After Josiah's death, Judah's last kings do evil one after another. The people make his son Jehoahaz king, but after three months Pharaoh Necho takes him prisoner to Egypt, where he dies. Necho puts another son of Josiah on the throne, renames him Jehoiakim, and makes him pay heavy tribute.

During Jehoiakim's reign, Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, defeats Egypt and becomes the great power of the world. Jehoiakim serves him for three years and then rebels. God sends raiding bands of Babylonians, Syrians, Moabites, and Ammonites against Judah, “to destroy it, according to the word of the LORD, which he spake by his servants the prophets,” because of the sins of Manasseh and the innocent blood he shed, “which the LORD would not pardon.”

Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin becomes king at eighteen and reigns only three months. Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem, and Jehoiachin surrenders. Nebuchadnezzar takes all the treasures of the temple and palace and carries away the king, his mother, his officials, the army, and ten thousand of the leading citizens, craftsmen, and skilled workers. The prophet Ezekiel was among this group. Only the poorest people are left. Nebuchadnezzar makes Jehoiachin's uncle Mattaniah king and renames him Zedekiah. Zedekiah also does evil, and finally, “Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon.”

**Jerusalem Falls and the Temple Is Destroyed (2 Kings 25:1–21)** — Babylon destroys Jerusalem and the temple and carries Judah into captivity.

In Zedekiah's ninth year, Nebuchadnezzar comes with his whole army and builds siege walls around Jerusalem. The siege lasts about a year and a half. The famine inside the city becomes so severe that there is no food at all. Then the Babylonians break through the wall. Zedekiah and his soldiers flee by night through a gate near the king's garden and run toward the Jordan valley. But the Babylonian army chases them and catches Zedekiah on the plains of Jericho, and his army scatters.

They bring Zedekiah to Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah. There “they slew the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes, and put out the eyes of Zedekiah, and bound him with fetters of brass, and carried him to Babylon.” The last thing he ever sees is the death of his sons.

A month later, Nebuzaradan, captain of Nebuchadnezzar's guard, comes to Jerusalem. “And he burnt the house of the LORD, and the king's house, and all the houses of Jerusalem.” His army breaks down the walls around the city. He carries away into exile the rest of the people, leaving only some of the poorest to work the vineyards and fields. The Babylonians break up the great bronze pillars, the bronze Sea, and the stands that Solomon made, and carry the bronze to Babylon, along with every gold and silver vessel. They take the chief priest and other leaders to Riblah, where they are executed. “So Judah was carried away out of their land.” This happened in 586 BC.

**The Survivors Scatter and Jehoiachin Receives Mercy (2 Kings 25:22–30)** — Gedaliah is murdered, but the book closes with mercy for David's line in Babylon.

Nebuchadnezzar appoints Gedaliah, a respected Judean official, as governor over the poor people left in the land. Gedaliah tells them not to be afraid to serve the Babylonians: “dwell in the land, and serve the king of Babylon; and it shall be well with you.” But a man of the royal family named Ishmael comes with ten men and murders Gedaliah, along with the Jews and Babylonians with him at Mizpah. Then, afraid of what Babylon will do, all the remaining people, from the least to the greatest, flee to Egypt.

The book ends with a small spark of hope. Thirty-seven years after King Jehoiachin was taken into exile, a new king of Babylon, Evilmerodach, releases him from prison. “And he spake kindly to him, and set his throne above the throne of the kings that were with him in Babylon.” Jehoiachin takes off his prison clothes and eats at the king's table every day for the rest of his life, with a regular allowance from the king. David's family line is still alive, even in exile, and God's promise to David has not been forgotten.

# 1 Chronicles
When it happened: Through genealogies it reaches from Adam to the return from exile, but its story covers the death of Saul and the reign of David, ending about 970 BC.
When it was written: 1 and 2 Chronicles were originally one book, and its writer is often called the Chronicler. The genealogies run past the exile (1 Chronicles 3:17–24; 9:1–3), so it was written after the return—likely in the 400s BC.
Where: Centered on Jerusalem, with Hebron and the tribal lands of Israel.
The world at the time: When Chronicles was written, Judah was a small province of the Persian Empire, with a rebuilt temple but no king. Retelling David's story reminded the community of God's promises to David's house and the importance of the temple.

**Israel's Family History Begins with Adam (1 Chronicles 1:1–2:55)** — Israel's ancestry is traced from Adam through Noah, Abraham, Jacob, Judah, Jesse, and David.

Chronicles was written for the Jews who had returned from exile in Babylon. They needed to know who they were and that God's promises still stood. So the book begins with a family tree that stretches all the way back to the first man: “Adam, Sheth, Enosh.” In a few short lists, it moves from Adam to Noah and his sons, then to the nations that came from them, including Nimrod, who “began to be mighty upon the earth.” Then it narrows to Abraham, Isaac, and Esau's descendants, the Edomites.

Chapter 2 begins Israel's own story with the twelve sons of Jacob. It focuses first on Judah, the tribe of kings. The list includes painful reminders, like Judah's son Er, who “was evil in the sight of the LORD,” and Achar, also called Achan, “the troubler of Israel,” who stole from Jericho. But the main line runs through Boaz to Jesse and to Jesse's seventh son, David. Chronicles is preparing the reader for the story of David's family.

**The Descendants of David and Israel's Tribes (1 Chronicles 3:1–5:26)** — The records follow David's royal descendants and the tribes of Judah, Simeon, and the east.

Chapter 3 lists David's sons. Six were born in Hebron, including Amnon and Absalom. Others were born in Jerusalem, including Solomon, whose mother is called Bathshua here. Then it traces the kings of Judah from Solomon through Josiah and on past the exile, all the way to Zerubbabel and his descendants. The readers would have known Zerubbabel as the leader who brought the first exiles home. David's line has survived.

Chapter 4 continues with the families of Judah and Simeon. In the middle of the list of names comes a short story. “And Jabez was more honourable than his brethren: and his mother called his name Jabez, saying, Because I bare him with sorrow.” his name sounds like the Hebrew word for pain. But Jabez prays, “Oh that thou wouldest bless me indeed, and enlarge my coast, and that thine hand might be with me, and that thou wouldest keep me from evil, that it may not grieve me! And God granted him that which he requested.”

Chapter 5 lists the tribes east of the Jordan: Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh. Reuben was Jacob's firstborn, but he lost his birthright because he slept with his father's concubine, and it was given to Joseph's sons. These tribes once won a great battle, because “they cried to God in the battle, and he was intreated of them; because they put their trust in him.” But later they worshiped the gods of the land, so God stirred up the king of Assyria, who carried them away into exile.

**The Levites and Their Sacred Responsibilities (1 Chronicles 6:1–81)** — Levi's descendants are traced through priestly families and worship leaders.

The tribe of Levi receives special attention, because Chronicles cares deeply about worship. The chapter traces the line of high priests from Aaron through Zadok, who served David and Solomon, all the way to Jehozadak, who “went into captivity, when the LORD carried away Judah and Jerusalem by the hand of Nebuchadnezzar.”

It also lists the musicians David appointed to lead worship “after that the ark had rest.” The three chief singers were Heman, Asaph, and Ethan, from the three branches of the Levites. Several of the psalms are credited to them. Aaron's descendants offered the sacrifices and burned incense, while the other Levites served in the tabernacle. Finally, the chapter lists the towns and pasturelands given to the priests and Levites in every tribe, including the cities of refuge.

**The Remaining Tribes and Israel's Returned Families (1 Chronicles 7:1–9:34)** — Genealogies preserve the other tribes and the community that returned to Jerusalem after captivity.

Chapters 7 and 8 list the families of the other tribes: Issachar, Benjamin, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, and Asher. Many are counted as “mighty men of valour.” In Ephraim's line comes Joshua, the son of Nun. Benjamin's family is traced carefully, because it leads to King Saul.

Then chapter 9 explains why these records matter: “So all Israel were reckoned by genealogies... and Judah, who were carried away to Babylon for their transgression.” The rest of the chapter lists the people who came back to live in Jerusalem after the exile: families from Judah, Benjamin, Ephraim, and Manasseh, along with priests, Levites, and gatekeepers. The gatekeepers were trusted to guard the temple day and night, to keep the keys, and to open the doors every morning. Others cared for the holy vessels, baked the bread, and led the singing, “for they were employed in that work day and night.” The returned exiles are connected to the faithful servants of God from long before.

**Saul's Reign Ends in Defeat (1 Chronicles 9:35–10:14)** — Saul and his sons die at Mount Gilboa, and the kingdom passes to David.

The family of Saul is listed again, and then the story begins with his death. The Philistines defeat Israel on Mount Gilboa and kill Saul's sons, including Jonathan. Saul is badly wounded by archers. His armor-bearer refuses to kill him, so Saul falls on his own sword. The Philistines strip his body and fasten his head in the temple of their god Dagon. The brave men of Jabeshgilead rescue the bodies and bury them.

Chronicles skips most of Saul's life and gives a short explanation instead: “So Saul died for his transgression which he committed against the LORD, even against the word of the LORD, which he kept not, and also for asking counsel of one that had a familiar spirit, to enquire of it; And enquired not of the LORD: therefore he slew him, and turned the kingdom unto David the son of Jesse.”

**David Becomes King Over Israel (1 Chronicles 11:1–12:40)** — All Israel anoints David king at Hebron, and mighty men from every tribe support him.

All Israel gathers to David at Hebron and says, “Behold, we are thy bone and thy flesh.” They anoint him king over Israel, “according to the word of the LORD by Samuel.” David marches on Jerusalem, held by the Jebusites, and promises that whoever leads the attack will become commander of the army. Joab goes up first and becomes chief. David captures the stronghold of Zion, and it becomes the city of David. “So David waxed greater and greater: for the LORD of hosts was with him.”

Chronicles lists David's mighty men and their deeds, including the three warriors who broke through the Philistine lines to bring him water from the well of Bethlehem. Then chapter 12 lists the men who came to join David even before he was king, while he was hiding from Saul at Ziklag. Some were relatives of Saul from Benjamin, skilled archers and slingers who could use either hand. Others were from Gad, “whose faces were like the faces of lions, and were as swift as the roes upon the mountains.” When some of Benjamin and Judah came, David was cautious. But Amasai, filled with the Spirit, said, “Thine are we, David, and on thy side, thou son of Jesse: peace, peace be unto thee.”

At Hebron, more than three hundred thousand soldiers from every tribe come to make David king. From Issachar come men “that had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do.” They feast with David for three days, and their neighbors bring food on donkeys, camels, mules, and oxen, “for there was joy in Israel.”

**David Attempts to Bring the Ark to Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 13:1–14:17)** — David's first attempt to move the ark ends in Uzza's death.

David consults with his leaders and decides to bring the ark of God to Jerusalem: “for we enquired not at it in the days of Saul.” They bring the ark out of Kirjathjearim on a new cart, with David and all Israel playing music and singing with all their might. But when the oxen stumble, Uzza puts out his hand to steady the ark, and God strikes him dead. David is upset and afraid. He names the place Perezuzza, meaning outburst against Uzza. He leaves the ark at the house of Obededom, and God blesses Obededom's family for three months.

Meanwhile, Hiram king of Tyre sends cedar and workers to build David a palace. David knows that God has established his kingdom “because of his people Israel.” The Philistines attack twice in the valley of Rephaim. Both times David asks God what to do, and both times God gives him victory. The second time, God tells him to circle around and wait for “a sound of going in the tops of the mulberry trees,” and then attack, “for God is gone forth before thee.” “And the fame of David went out into all lands; and the LORD brought the fear of him upon all nations.”

**David Brings the Ark into Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 15:1–16:43)** — David moves the ark the right way, and Israel worships with music and thanksgiving.

This time David prepares carefully. He says, “None ought to carry the ark of God but the Levites.” He explains why Uzza died: “Because ye did it not at the first, the LORD our God made a breach upon us, for that we sought him not after the due order.” The Levites purify themselves and carry the ark on poles on their shoulders, “as Moses commanded.” David appoints singers and musicians with harps, lyres, and cymbals. As the ark comes into Jerusalem, there is shouting, horns, trumpets, and music. David dances and celebrates, and Michal watches from a window “and she despised him in her heart.”

They set the ark in the tent David had prepared and offer sacrifices. David gives every person bread, meat, and raisins. Then he gives Asaph and his relatives a song of thanks to sing. It is made from parts of Psalms 105, 96, and 106. “O give thanks unto the LORD, call upon his name, make known his deeds among the people.” “Seek the LORD and his strength, seek his face continually.” “Sing unto the LORD, all the earth; shew forth from day to day his salvation.” “Give unto the LORD the glory due unto his name... O worship the LORD in the beauty of holiness.” The song ends, “O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good; for his mercy endureth for ever.” And all the people say, “Amen.”

David assigns Asaph and the other musicians to minister before the ark every day. He leaves Zadok and the priests at the tabernacle in Gibeon to keep offering the daily sacrifices, with Heman and Jeduthun to give thanks there.

**God Promises David an Enduring Kingdom (1 Chronicles 17:1–27)** — God promises to build David a lasting house instead of letting David build him one.

Living in his cedar palace, David tells the prophet Nathan that he wants to build a house for the ark of God. That night, God tells Nathan that David will not build it. God took David from following the sheep to be ruler over Israel, and now God will build a house for David instead, a royal family. One of David's sons will build the temple, “and I will stablish his throne for ever. I will be his father, and he shall be my son: and I will not take my mercy away from him.” “But I will settle him in mine house and in my kingdom for ever: and his throne shall be established for evermore.”

David goes in and sits before the LORD. “Who am I, O LORD God, and what is mine house, that thou hast brought me hitherto?” He praises God: “O LORD, there is none like thee, neither is there any God beside thee.” He thanks God for choosing Israel and redeeming them from Egypt, and asks him to keep his promise. “For thou blessest, O LORD, and it shall be blessed for ever.”

**David Defeats Israel's Enemies (1 Chronicles 18:1–20:8)** — God gives David victory over the surrounding nations.

God gives David victory wherever he goes. He defeats the Philistines, Moabites, Syrians, and Edomites. He dedicates the silver, gold, and bronze he captures to the LORD. Solomon later uses that bronze to make the great basin, the pillars, and other furnishings for the temple. “So David reigned over all Israel, and executed judgment and justice among all his people.”

When the new Ammonite king, Hanun, humiliates David's messengers by shaving them and cutting their clothes, war breaks out. The Ammonites hire Syrian chariots and soldiers. Joab encourages his brother Abishai: “Be of good courage, and let us behave ourselves valiantly for our people.” Israel wins. The next spring, Joab captures the Ammonite capital of Rabbah, and David takes the king's heavy gold crown. Chronicles does not repeat the story of David and Bathsheba. Instead, it moves on to Israel's later battles with the Philistines. David's men kill several giants, including Lahmi, the brother of Goliath, whose spear was as thick as a weaver's beam, and a huge man with six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot.

**David's Census Brings Judgment Upon Israel (1 Chronicles 21:1–30)** — David's census brings judgment, but the place where the plague stops becomes the site of the temple.

“And Satan stood up against Israel, and provoked David to number Israel.” David orders Joab to count all the fighting men. Joab objects, asking why David would bring guilt on Israel. But David insists. Joab is so disgusted that he refuses to count the tribes of Levi and Benjamin. God is displeased and strikes Israel. David prays, “I have sinned greatly, because I have done this thing.”

The prophet Gad offers David three choices: three years of famine, three months of defeat by enemies, or three days of plague from the LORD. David says, “let me fall now into the hand of the LORD; for very great are his mercies.” A plague kills seventy thousand Israelites. As the angel reaches out to destroy Jerusalem, God says, “It is enough, stay now thine hand.” David looks up and sees the angel standing between earth and heaven with a drawn sword stretched over Jerusalem. David and the elders, dressed in sackcloth, fall on their faces. David says, “Is it not I that commanded the people to be numbered?... but as for these sheep, what have they done?”

Gad tells David to build an altar at the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite. Ornan is threshing wheat when he sees the angel, and his four sons hide. When David arrives, Ornan offers to give him everything for free. David says, “I will not take that which is thine for the LORD, nor offer burnt offerings without cost.” He pays six hundred shekels of gold for the site. He builds an altar, and God answers him with fire from heaven on the altar. The angel puts his sword away. David says, “This is the house of the LORD God.” This hill becomes the place where the temple will stand.

**David Prepares Solomon to Build the Temple (1 Chronicles 22:1–19)** — David gathers materials and charges Solomon to build the temple.

Right away, David begins preparing for the temple. He gathers stonecutters, huge amounts of iron for nails, more bronze than can be weighed, and countless cedar logs. He says, “Solomon my son is young and tender, and the house that is to be builded for the LORD must be exceeding magnifical, of fame and of glory throughout all countries.” So he prepares abundantly before his death.

David calls Solomon and explains why he himself could not build it. God had told him, “Thou hast shed blood abundantly, and hast made great wars.” Instead, God promised a son who would be “a man of rest,” and “his name shall be Solomon,” a name that sounds like the Hebrew word for peace. David tells Solomon, “Now, my son, the LORD be with thee; and prosper thou, and build the house of the LORD thy God.” He prays that God will give him wisdom to keep his law. “Be strong, and of good courage; dread not, nor be dismayed.” He has stored up huge amounts of gold and silver. Then he commands Israel's leaders to help Solomon: “Now set your heart and your soul to seek the LORD your God; arise therefore, and build ye the sanctuary.”

**David Organizes the Levites and Priests (1 Chronicles 23:1–24:31)** — David makes Solomon king and organizes the Levites and priests for temple service.

When David is old, he makes Solomon king. He gathers the leaders, priests, and Levites and counts the Levites who are thirty years old and older: 38,000 men. He assigns 24,000 to oversee the work of the temple, 6,000 to be officials and judges, 4,000 to be gatekeepers, and 4,000 to praise the LORD with musical instruments. Now that the ark has a permanent home, the Levites no longer need to carry the tabernacle. Instead, they help the priests in the temple courts, take care of the holy bread and offerings, and stand every morning and evening “to thank and praise the LORD.”

The descendants of Aaron, the priests, are divided into twenty-four groups by casting lots, so that each group takes a turn serving in the temple. One of these is the group of Abijah. More than a thousand years later, Zacharias, the father of John the Baptist, was serving with the course of Abijah when the angel Gabriel appeared to him in the temple (Luke 1:5).

**David Establishes Musicians, Gatekeepers, and Officials (1 Chronicles 25:1–27:34)** — David organizes musicians, gatekeepers, treasurers, and officials for the kingdom and temple.

David and his army leaders set apart the sons of Asaph, Heman, and Jeduthun “who should prophesy with harps, with psalteries, and with cymbals.” There are 288 skilled musicians, divided into twenty-four groups of twelve, chosen by lot, “as well the small as the great, the teacher as the scholar.” Worship through music is treated as a holy calling.

The gatekeepers are also divided by families and assigned by lot to guard the east, west, north, and south gates of the temple. Other Levites are put in charge of the temple treasuries and the gifts dedicated by David, Samuel, Saul, and others. Some serve as officials and judges throughout Israel.

Chapter 27 describes David's government. His army has twelve divisions of 24,000 men, each serving one month of the year under a commander, many of them from David's mighty men. There are leaders for each tribe, and officials in charge of the king's storehouses, farms, vineyards, olive groves, herds, and flocks. The chapter also lists David's advisers, including Ahithophel and Hushai, and Joab, the commander of the army.

**David Gives Solomon the Plans for the Temple (1 Chronicles 28:1–21)** — David publicly charges Solomon and gives him the temple plans.

David calls all the leaders of Israel to Jerusalem. He stands up and tells them that he had wanted to build a house for the ark. “But God said unto me, Thou shalt not build an house for my name, because thou hast been a man of war, and hast shed blood.” Instead, God chose Solomon to sit on the throne and build the temple.

Then David speaks to his son in front of everyone: “And thou, Solomon my son, know thou the God of thy father, and serve him with a perfect heart and with a willing mind: for the LORD searcheth all hearts, and understandeth all the imaginations of the thoughts: if thou seek him, he will be found of thee; but if thou forsake him, he will cast thee off for ever.” He tells him to take heed, “for the LORD hath chosen thee to build an house for the sanctuary: be strong, and do it.”

David gives Solomon detailed plans for the temple, its rooms and courts, the duties of the priests and Levites, and the exact weight of gold and silver for every object. He says, “All this, said David, the LORD made me understand in writing by his hand upon me.” He ends, “Be strong and of good courage, and do it: fear not, nor be dismayed: for the LORD God, even my God, will be with thee; he will not fail thee, nor forsake thee, until thou hast finished all the work.”

**David Dedicates the Kingdom's Gifts to God (1 Chronicles 29:1–30)** — David and Israel give generously for the temple, and David's reign comes to an end.

David tells the assembly that Solomon is young and the work is great, “for the palace is not for man, but for the LORD God.” He has given all he could for the temple, and now he gives his own personal treasure of gold and silver too. Then he asks, “And who then is willing to consecrate his service this day unto the LORD?” The leaders of Israel give generously: tons of gold, silver, bronze, and iron, and precious stones. “Then the people rejoiced, for that they offered willingly, because with perfect heart they offered willingly to the LORD: and David the king also rejoiced with great joy.”

David blesses the LORD in front of everyone: “Thine, O LORD, is the greatness, and the power, and the glory, and the victory, and the majesty: for all that is in the heaven and in the earth is thine; thine is the kingdom, O LORD.” He is humbled by their gifts: “But who am I, and what is my people, that we should be able to offer so willingly after this sort? for all things come of thee, and of thine own have we given thee.” “For we are strangers before thee, and sojourners, as were all our fathers: our days on the earth are as a shadow.” He prays that God will give Solomon “a perfect heart, to keep thy commandments.”

The next day they offer a thousand bulls, a thousand rams, and a thousand lambs, and they eat and drink before the LORD with great joy. They make Solomon king a second time, and all Israel obeys him. David reigned forty years, seven in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem. “And he died in a good old age, full of days, riches, and honour: and Solomon his son reigned in his stead.”

# 2 Chronicles
When it happened: From the beginning of Solomon's reign (about 970 BC) to the destruction of Jerusalem (586 BC), closing with the decree of Cyrus in 538 BC.
When it was written: Jewish tradition credits Ezra. It is the continuation of 1 Chronicles, written after the return from exile, likely in the 400s BC.
Where: Jerusalem and the kingdom of Judah.
The world at the time: Chronicles follows only the kings of Judah, while Egypt, Assyria, and then Babylon threatened from outside. Written in the Persian period, it ends with a Persian king inviting the exiles home.

**God Gives Solomon Wisdom and Wealth (2 Chronicles 1:1–17)** — Solomon seeks wisdom at Gibeon to govern Israel, and God gives him wisdom, riches, and honor.

Solomon, son of David, is firmly established as king, “and the LORD his God was with him, and magnified him exceedingly.” He gathers all Israel's leaders and goes to Gibeon, where the old tabernacle Moses built and the bronze altar still stand. There he offers a thousand burnt offerings.

That night God appears to him and says, “Ask what I shall give thee.” Solomon answers, “Give me now wisdom and knowledge, that I may go out and come in before this people: for who can judge this thy people, that is so great?” God is pleased that Solomon did not ask for riches, honor, the death of his enemies, or long life. “Wisdom and knowledge is granted unto thee; and I will give thee riches, and wealth, and honour, such as none of the kings have had that have been before thee, neither shall there any after thee have the like.” Solomon returns to Jerusalem and gathers chariots and horses, and he makes silver and gold as common in Jerusalem as stones.

**Solomon Builds the Temple in Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 2:1–5:1)** — Solomon gathers workers and materials and builds the temple on Mount Moriah.

Solomon decides to build the temple and a palace. He drafts thousands of laborers and sends a message to Huram, king of Tyre, asking for cedar, cypress, and a master craftsman. He explains why the temple must be great: “for great is our God above all gods. But who is able to build him an house, seeing the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain him?” Huram replies, “Because the LORD hath loved his people, he hath made thee king over them.” He sends a skilled craftsman named Huram, whose mother was an Israelite.

“Then Solomon began to build the house of the LORD at Jerusalem in mount Moriah, where the LORD appeared unto David his father, in the place that David had prepared in the threshingfloor of Ornan the Jebusite.” Mount Moriah is also the place where Abraham was told to offer Isaac. The temple is covered inside with pure gold and decorated with precious stones. In the most holy place stand two huge golden cherubims whose wings stretch across the whole room. A curtain of blue, purple, and crimson, embroidered with cherubims, hangs before it. In front of the temple stand the two bronze pillars, Jachin and Boaz. Solomon has the furnishings made: the bronze altar, the great bronze basin called the Sea resting on twelve oxen, ten washing basins, ten golden lampstands, and ten tables. Huram casts the bronze work in clay in the plain of Jordan. When all the work is finished, Solomon brings in the treasures his father David had dedicated.

**God's Glory Fills the Completed Temple (2 Chronicles 5:2–7:22)** — God's glory fills the temple, and God promises to hear his people when they turn to him.

Solomon gathers all Israel to bring the ark of the covenant into the temple. The Levites take it up, and the priests carry it into the most holy place, beneath the wings of the cherubims. Then the priests come out, and the Levite singers stand by the altar in white linen with cymbals, harps, and lyres, along with 120 priests blowing trumpets. “It came even to pass, as the trumpeters and singers were as one, to make one sound to be heard in praising and thanking the LORD,” singing, “For he is good; for his mercy endureth for ever: that then the house was filled with a cloud, even the house of the LORD.” The priests cannot stand to serve because of the cloud, “for the glory of the LORD had filled the house of God.”

Solomon stands on a bronze platform in the courtyard, then kneels before all Israel and spreads out his hands toward heaven. “O LORD God of Israel, there is no God like thee in the heaven, nor in the earth; which keepest covenant, and shewest mercy unto thy servants.” He prays for God to hear when his people pray toward this house in times of sin, defeat, drought, famine, and exile. “Now, my God, let, I beseech thee, thine eyes be open, and let thine ears be attent unto the prayer that is made in this place.”

When Solomon finishes praying, “the fire came down from heaven, and consumed the burnt offering and the sacrifices; and the glory of the LORD filled the house.” The people bow with their faces to the pavement and worship, saying, “For he is good; for his mercy endureth for ever.” They offer 22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep and celebrate the feast for fourteen days.

Then God appears to Solomon at night. He promises that if disaster comes because of sin, “If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land.” But he also warns that if they turn away and serve other gods, he will uproot them from the land and reject this temple, and it will become a byword among the nations.

**Solomon's Kingdom Reaches Great Prosperity (2 Chronicles 8:1–9:31)** — Solomon builds cities, orders worship, and receives the queen of Sheba.

Over twenty years, Solomon builds cities, store cities, and fortresses throughout his kingdom. He moves Pharaoh's daughter out of the city of David to a palace he built for her, saying that the places where the ark has been are holy. He organizes the priests and Levites according to David's plan, and offers sacrifices on every Sabbath, new moon, and feast. His ships bring back gold from Ophir.

The queen of Sheba hears of his fame and comes to test him with hard questions. She arrives with camels loaded with spices, gold, and precious stones. Solomon answers every question. When she sees his wisdom, his palace, his servants, and how he goes up to the house of the LORD, she is breathless with wonder. She tells him, “I believed not their words, until I came, and mine eyes had seen it: and, behold, the one half of the greatness of thy wisdom was not told me.” She praises God for making him king.

Solomon's riches are beyond counting: tons of gold every year, gold shields, a great ivory throne covered with gold with six steps and twelve lions, gold cups, and ships that bring gold, silver, ivory, apes, and peacocks. “And king Solomon passed all the kings of the earth in riches and wisdom.” Kings from all over the world come to hear the wisdom God has put in his heart. Solomon reigns forty years and dies, and his son Rehoboam becomes king. Chronicles does not repeat the story of Solomon's foreign wives and idols. It says the rest of his acts are written in the records of Nathan the prophet and others.

**Israel Divides Under Rehoboam (2 Chronicles 10:1–12:16)** — Rehoboam's harsh answer splits the kingdom, and Judah is humbled when it abandons God.

When Rehoboam becomes king, the northern tribes ask him to lighten the heavy load his father put on them. He rejects the advice of the older men and follows his young friends, telling the people, “My father made your yoke heavy, but I will add thereto: my father chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions.” The ten northern tribes break away and make Jeroboam their king. Rehoboam prepares to attack, but the prophet Shemaiah tells him not to fight against his brothers.

When Jeroboam sets up golden calves and appoints his own priests, the faithful priests and Levites from the north leave their homes and move to Judah, along with others who want to seek the LORD. They strengthen the kingdom of Judah for three years. But once Rehoboam feels secure, “he forsook the law of the LORD, and all Israel with him.”

In his fifth year, Shishak, king of Egypt, invades with twelve hundred chariots and a huge army and captures Judah's fortified cities. The prophet Shemaiah tells Rehoboam and his officials, “Ye have forsaken me, and therefore have I also left you in the hand of Shishak.” The leaders humble themselves and say, “The LORD is righteous.” God sees it and says he will not destroy them completely, but they will become Shishak's servants, “that they may know my service, and the service of the kingdoms of the countries.” They will learn the difference between serving God and serving a foreign king. Shishak carries off the temple and palace treasures, including Solomon's gold shields. Rehoboam reigns seventeen years. “And he did evil, because he prepared not his heart to seek the LORD.”

**Abijah Defeats Israel and Asa Seeks God (2 Chronicles 13:1–16:14)** — Judah prospers when it relies on God and suffers when it trusts in human help.

Rehoboam's son Abijah faces Jeroboam in battle with 400,000 men against 800,000. Standing on a mountain, Abijah shouts to Jeroboam's army that God gave the kingdom to David “for ever... by a covenant of salt,” a lasting covenant. He points out that Israel has driven out God's priests and has golden calves for gods. “O children of Israel, fight ye not against the LORD God of your fathers; for ye shall not prosper.” Meanwhile Jeroboam has set an ambush behind Judah. When Judah sees they are surrounded, they cry out to God, the priests blow trumpets, and the men give a battle shout, and God strikes Jeroboam's army. Judah wins “because they relied upon the LORD God of their fathers.”

Abijah's son Asa “did that which was good and right in the eyes of the LORD his God.” He removes foreign altars and idols and commands Judah to seek the LORD. Then Zerah the Ethiopian attacks with an enormous army. Asa prays, “LORD, it is nothing with thee to help, whether with many, or with them that have no power: help us, O LORD our God; for we rest on thee, and in thy name we go against this multitude.” God gives him a great victory. A prophet named Azariah meets him with a message: “The LORD is with you, while ye be with him; and if ye seek him, he will be found of you; but if ye forsake him, he will forsake you.” “Be ye strong therefore, and let not your hands be weak: for your work shall be rewarded.” Asa takes courage, removes the idols from the whole land, and leads the people in a covenant to seek God with all their heart. He even removes his grandmother Maachah from her position as queen mother because of her idol.

But later, when Baasha king of Israel threatens Judah, Asa pays the king of Syria with temple treasure to attack Israel instead of trusting God. The prophet Hanani tells him, “For the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him. Herein thou hast done foolishly.” Asa is so angry that he puts the prophet in prison and mistreats some of the people. In his old age, he gets a severe disease in his feet, “yet in his disease he sought not to the LORD, but to the physicians.”

**Jehoshaphat Strengthens Judah and Receives Deliverance (2 Chronicles 17:1–20:37)** — Jehoshaphat teaches God's law and leads Judah to trust God in a crisis.

Asa's son Jehoshaphat “walked in the first ways of his father David.” He removes the high places and sends officials, Levites, and priests throughout Judah to teach the people from the book of the law of the LORD. Fear falls on the surrounding nations, and they do not attack him. Some even bring him gifts.

But Jehoshaphat makes an alliance with wicked King Ahab by marrying his son to Ahab's daughter, and he joins Ahab in the battle where Ahab is killed. When he returns, the prophet Jehu son of Hanani asks him, “Shouldest thou help the ungodly, and love them that hate the LORD?” Still, he says there is good in Jehoshaphat. Jehoshaphat appoints judges in every city and warns them, “Take heed what ye do: for ye judge not for man, but for the LORD.” “For there is no iniquity with the LORD our God, nor respect of persons, nor taking of gifts.”

Then a huge army of Moabites, Ammonites, and others marches against Judah. Jehoshaphat is afraid, but he “set himself to seek the LORD, and proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah.” He stands before the people at the temple and prays, “O our God, wilt thou not judge them? for we have no might against this great company that cometh against us; neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee.” All Judah stands before the LORD with their wives and little children. Then the Spirit of the LORD comes on a Levite named Jahaziel, and he says, “Be not afraid nor dismayed by reason of this great multitude; for the battle is not yours, but God's.” “Ye shall not need to fight in this battle: set yourselves, stand ye still, and see the salvation of the LORD with you.”

Early the next morning, Jehoshaphat tells the people, “Believe in the LORD your God, so shall ye be established; believe his prophets, so shall ye prosper.” He puts singers at the front of the army, praising God and singing, “Praise the LORD; for his mercy endureth for ever.” As they begin to sing, God sets ambushes against the enemy, and the invading armies turn on each other until they are all destroyed. When Judah reaches the lookout point, they see nothing but dead bodies. It takes three days to gather all the spoil. On the fourth day they gather in a valley to bless the LORD, and they name it the valley of Berachah, meaning blessing. Later Jehoshaphat joins with another wicked king of Israel to build ships, and the ships are wrecked.

**Judah Suffers Under Jehoram and Ahaziah (2 Chronicles 21:1–22:12)** — Jehoram follows Ahab's house, bringing judgment on Judah and his family.

Jehoshaphat's son Jehoram becomes king, and his first act is to kill all six of his brothers. He is married to Ahab's daughter, and he walks in the ways of Ahab's family. Edom and Libnah rebel against him. Then a letter arrives from the prophet Elijah. Because Jehoram has not walked in the ways of his father but has led Judah into idolatry and murdered his better brothers, God will strike his family, and he himself will suffer a terrible disease of the bowels. The Philistines and Arabians invade, carry off his wives and all his sons except the youngest, and plunder the palace. Then the disease strikes, and after two years he dies in great pain. He “departed without being desired,” meaning no one was sorry. He is buried in the city of David, but not in the tombs of the kings.

His youngest son, Ahaziah, becomes king, and his mother Athaliah, Ahab's daughter, advises him to do wickedly. He joins his uncle, King Jehoram of Israel, and is killed by Jehu during his rebellion. When Athaliah sees her son is dead, she kills the rest of the royal family and takes the throne herself. But Jehoshabeath, the king's sister and wife of Jehoiada the priest, rescues the baby prince Joash and hides him in the temple for six years.

**Joash Restores the Temple but Later Turns Away (2 Chronicles 23:1–24:27)** — Joash serves God while Jehoiada lives but turns away after his death.

In the seventh year, Jehoiada the priest makes a covenant with the army captains and Levites and brings out young Joash. They crown him, give him a copy of the law, and anoint him, shouting, “God save the king.” Athaliah rushes in crying, “Treason, Treason,” and is taken out and executed. Jehoiada leads the people in a covenant to be the LORD's people. They tear down the temple of Baal, and Jehoiada restores the temple worship.

Joash is seven years old when he becomes king, and he does what is right all the days of Jehoiada the priest. He decides to repair the temple. He puts a chest at the temple gate, and the people joyfully bring their offerings until it is full again and again. The temple is restored. Jehoiada lives to be 130, and when he dies he is buried among the kings, “because he had done good in Israel, both toward God, and toward his house.”

But after Jehoiada dies, the officials of Judah come and bow to the king, and he listens to them. They abandon the temple and worship idols. God sends prophets to warn them, but they will not listen. Then the Spirit of God comes on Zechariah, Jehoiada's son, and he stands up and says, “Why transgress ye the commandments of the LORD, that ye cannot prosper? because ye have forsaken the LORD, he hath also forsaken you.” At the king's command, the people stone Zechariah to death in the temple courtyard. Joash forgets all the kindness Zechariah's father showed him. As he dies, Zechariah says, “The LORD look upon it, and require it.” Jesus later spoke of the blood of Zechariah, who “perished between the altar and the temple” (Luke 11:51). Within a year, a small Syrian army defeats Judah's much larger one. Joash is badly wounded, and his own servants kill him in his bed for the murder of Zechariah.

**Amaziah and Uzziah Experience Victory and Pride (2 Chronicles 25:1–26:23)** — Two kings prosper while they follow God and fall when pride and idolatry take hold.

Amaziah does what is right, “but not with a perfect heart.” He hires 100,000 soldiers from Israel for a war against Edom. A man of God warns him not to take them, “for the LORD is not with Israel.” Amaziah worries about the money he already paid. The prophet answers, “The LORD is able to give thee much more than this.” So Amaziah sends them home and defeats Edom with his own army. But then he brings home the gods of Edom, sets them up, and worships them. A prophet asks him why he would worship gods that could not even save their own people. Amaziah tells him to stop, and the prophet warns that God has decided to destroy him. Amaziah then challenges King Joash of Israel to battle, is defeated and captured, and later is assassinated.

Amaziah's son Uzziah becomes king at sixteen and reigns fifty-two years. “And he sought God in the days of Zechariah, who had understanding in the visions of God: and as long as he sought the LORD, God made him to prosper.” He wins battles against the Philistines and Arabians, builds towers, digs wells, and loves farming. He builds a strong army and invents machines for shooting arrows and large stones from the towers. “And his name spread far abroad; for he was marvellously helped, till he was strong.”

“But when he was strong, his heart was lifted up to his destruction.” He walks into the temple to burn incense on the altar, a job only priests could do. Azariah the high priest and eighty brave priests follow him in and stand up to him: “It appertaineth not unto thee, Uzziah, to burn incense unto the LORD, but to the priests.” Uzziah, holding the censer, becomes furious. As he rages at the priests, leprosy breaks out on his forehead. The priests hurry him out, and he himself rushes to leave, “because the LORD had smitten him.” He has leprosy until the day he dies and lives in a separate house, cut off from the temple, while his son Jotham rules. Isaiah saw his famous vision of God “In the year that king Uzziah died” (Isaiah 6:1).

**Jotham Remains Faithful While Ahaz Leads Judah Astray (2 Chronicles 27:1–28:27)** — Jotham grows strong through obedience, while Ahaz leads Judah into idolatry.

Uzziah's son Jotham does what is right, though the people still act corruptly. He builds up Judah's cities and defeats the Ammonites. “So Jotham became mighty, because he prepared his ways before the LORD his God.”

Jotham's son Ahaz is the opposite. He makes metal images for Baal and burns his own children in the fire in the Valley of Hinnom. So God hands him over to the king of Syria and to Pekah, king of Israel. Pekah kills 120,000 soldiers of Judah in one day, and Israel carries away 200,000 women and children as captives to Samaria.

But a prophet named Oded meets the army as it returns. He warns them that they have gone too far in their anger, and that they too have sinned against God. “Now hear me therefore, and deliver the captives again.” Some leaders of Ephraim agree. They take the captives, give clothes and sandals to those who need them, feed them, give them drink, put oil on their wounds, put the weak ones on donkeys, and bring them back to their families at Jericho, the city of palm trees. Jesus' parable of the Good Samaritan echoes this scene.

Meanwhile, Ahaz asks Assyria for help, but the Assyrian king only troubles him. “And in the time of his distress did he trespass yet more against the LORD: this is that king Ahaz.” He sacrifices to the gods of Damascus, saying, “Because the gods of the kings of Syria help them, therefore will I sacrifice to them.” He cuts up the vessels of the temple, shuts its doors, and sets up altars on every street corner of Jerusalem. When he dies, he is not buried in the royal tombs.

**Hezekiah Reopens the Temple and Restores Worship (2 Chronicles 29:1–36)** — Hezekiah reopens and cleanses the temple and restores its worship.

Hezekiah becomes king at twenty-five, and he does what is right, just as David did. In the very first month of his reign, he opens the doors of the temple that his father had shut and repairs them. He gathers the priests and Levites and tells them, “Hear me, ye Levites, sanctify now yourselves, and sanctify the house of the LORD God of your fathers, and carry forth the filthiness out of the holy place.” He explains that their fathers abandoned God, and that is why Judah has suffered. Now he wants to make a covenant with God, so that his anger will turn away. “My sons, be not now negligent.”

The Levites set to work. They carry all the unclean things out of the temple and throw them into the Kidron Valley. It takes sixteen days to cleanse the whole temple. Then Hezekiah gathers the city leaders and offers sin offerings for the kingdom, the temple, and Judah. He sets the Levites in their places with cymbals, harps, and lyres, as David had commanded, and the priests with trumpets. “And when the burnt offering began, the song of the LORD began also with the trumpets.” The whole assembly worships while the singers sing and the trumpets sound. They bow down and sing praises with gladness. Then the people bring so many offerings that there are not enough priests to prepare them, and the Levites help. “And Hezekiah rejoiced, and all the people, that God had prepared the people: for the thing was done suddenly.”

**Hezekiah Invites Israel to Celebrate Passover (2 Chronicles 30:1–31:21)** — Hezekiah calls all Israel to a great Passover in Jerusalem.

Hezekiah sends messengers with letters not only through Judah, but also to the survivors of the northern tribes, inviting everyone to come to Jerusalem to keep the Passover. The northern kingdom has already been destroyed by Assyria. The letter says, “turn again unto the LORD God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, and he will return to the remnant of you.” “For the LORD your God is gracious and merciful, and will not turn away his face from you, if ye return unto him.” In many towns, people “laughed them to scorn, and mocked them.” But some from Asher, Manasseh, and Zebulun humble themselves and come.

A huge crowd gathers in Jerusalem in the second month. They tear down the pagan altars in the city and throw them into the Kidron. Many people from the north have not purified themselves according to the law, yet they eat the Passover. Hezekiah prays for them: “The good LORD pardon every one that prepareth his heart to seek God... though he be not cleansed according to the purification of the sanctuary.” “And the LORD hearkened to Hezekiah, and healed the people.” They celebrate for seven days with great joy, and then decide to celebrate seven more. “So there was great joy in Jerusalem: for since the time of Solomon the son of David king of Israel there was not the like in Jerusalem.”

Afterward, the people go out and smash the sacred pillars and idol poles throughout the land. Hezekiah reorganizes the priests and Levites and commands the people to bring their tithes. They bring so much grain, wine, oil, and honey that it lies in heaps, and storerooms have to be built in the temple. “And in every work that he began in the service of the house of God... he did it with all his heart, and prospered.”

**God Delivers Jerusalem from Assyria (2 Chronicles 32:1–23)** — Hezekiah encourages Jerusalem to trust God, and God defeats Assyria.

After all Hezekiah's faithfulness, Sennacherib, king of Assyria, invades Judah, planning to capture Jerusalem. Hezekiah and his officials block the springs outside the city so the Assyrians will not find water. They repair the walls, build towers, and make many weapons and shields. Then Hezekiah gathers the people and encourages them: “Be strong and courageous, be not afraid nor dismayed for the king of Assyria, nor for all the multitude that is with him: for there be more with us than with him: With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God to help us, and to fight our battles.” The people are encouraged by his words.

Sennacherib sends servants to Jerusalem to mock Hezekiah and God. They shout in Hebrew to the people on the wall, trying to frighten them. They write letters insulting the LORD and say that no god of any nation has been able to save his people from Assyria. “And they spake against the God of Jerusalem, as against the gods of the people of the earth, which were the work of the hands of man.” Hezekiah and the prophet Isaiah cry out to heaven in prayer. God sends an angel, who destroys the mighty warriors and commanders in the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib goes home “with shame of face.” When he enters the temple of his god, some of his own sons kill him with the sword. God saves Hezekiah and Jerusalem, and many people bring gifts to the LORD and to Hezekiah.

**Hezekiah's Pride and Final Years (2 Chronicles 32:24–33)** — God heals Hezekiah, tests his heart, and grants him an honored burial.

Hezekiah becomes deathly sick. He prays, and God answers and gives him a sign. “But Hezekiah rendered not again according to the benefit done unto him; for his heart was lifted up.” Because of his pride, God's anger comes on him and on Judah. Then Hezekiah humbles himself, along with the people of Jerusalem, so the anger does not come in his lifetime.

Hezekiah becomes very wealthy and honored, with treasuries full of silver, gold, jewels, and spices, storehouses for grain and wine, and many flocks and herds. He also “stopped the upper watercourse of Gihon, and brought it straight down to the west side of the city of David.” His tunnel still exists in Jerusalem today. When ambassadors come from Babylon to ask about the sign God gave, “God left him, to try him, that he might know all that was in his heart.” When Hezekiah dies, he is buried in the most honored place among the tombs of David's sons, and all Judah and Jerusalem honor him.

**Manasseh Repents but Amon Rejects God (2 Chronicles 33:1–25)** — Manasseh's long rebellion ends in repentance, while his son refuses to humble himself.

Hezekiah's son Manasseh becomes king at twelve and reigns fifty-five years. He rebuilds the high places his father destroyed, sets up altars to Baal, and worships the stars. He builds pagan altars inside the temple courts. He burns his sons in the fire in the Valley of Hinnom and practices witchcraft and sorcery. He leads Judah to do more evil than the nations God destroyed before them. God speaks to Manasseh and his people, but they will not listen.

So God brings the commanders of the Assyrian army against Judah. They take Manasseh “among the thorns, and bound him with fetters, and carried him to Babylon.” Some translations say they put a hook in his nose. There, “when he was in affliction, he besought the LORD his God, and humbled himself greatly before the God of his fathers.” God hears his prayer and brings him back to Jerusalem as king. “Then Manasseh knew that the LORD he was God.”

After he returns, Manasseh removes the foreign gods and the idol from the temple and throws the altars he had built out of the city. He repairs the LORD's altar, offers sacrifices of thanks, and commands Judah to serve the LORD. Only Chronicles tells of Manasseh's repentance. His prayer was written in the book of the kings of Israel. But his son Amon does evil like his father did before he repented, and Amon does not humble himself. After two years, his servants assassinate him, and the people make his son Josiah king.

**Josiah Restores the Temple and Rediscovers God's Law (2 Chronicles 34:1–33)** — Josiah seeks God from his youth, purges idolatry, and renews the covenant.

Josiah becomes king at eight years old. At sixteen, “while he was yet young, he began to seek after the God of David his father.” At twenty, he begins to clean the land of its high places, idol poles, and idols. He breaks down the altars of Baal, grinds the idols to dust, and scatters the dust on the graves of those who worshiped them. He burns the bones of the false priests on their own altars. He does this not only in Judah but also in the old territory of the northern tribes.

At twenty-six, Josiah sends officials to repair the temple. While they are bringing out the money, Hilkiah the priest finds “a book of the law of the LORD given by Moses.” When the book is read to the king, he tears his clothes. He sends officials to the prophetess Huldah, who says that God will indeed bring all the curses written in the book on Jerusalem, because the people have abandoned him. But because Josiah's heart was tender and he humbled himself and wept before God, the disaster will not come in his lifetime.

Josiah gathers all the people to the temple and reads the whole book of the covenant to them. He stands in his place and makes a covenant before the LORD to follow him with all his heart and soul, and he has all the people join it. He removes every disgusting idol from the land. “And all his days they departed not from following the LORD, the God of their fathers.”

**Josiah Celebrates Passover and Dies in Battle (2 Chronicles 35:1–27)** — Josiah keeps a great Passover, then dies after ignoring a warning from God through Necho.

Josiah holds a great Passover in Jerusalem. He tells the Levites to put the ark back in the temple and to prepare the Passover lambs as the law of Moses commands. Josiah gives thirty thousand lambs and goats and three thousand bulls from his own flocks for the people, and his officials give generously too. The priests, Levites, singers, and gatekeepers all work in their places. “And there was no passover like to that kept in Israel from the days of Samuel the prophet.”

Some years later, Pharaoh Necho of Egypt marches north to fight at Carchemish on the Euphrates River, and Josiah goes out to stop him. Necho sends messengers: “What have I to do with thee, thou king of Judah? I come not against thee this day... for God commanded me to make haste: forbear thee from meddling with God, who is with me, that he destroy thee not.” But Josiah will not listen to the words of Necho, which came from God. He disguises himself and fights in the valley of Megiddo. Archers shoot him, and he tells his servants, “Have me away; for I am sore wounded.” They take him out of his chariot and bring him to Jerusalem, where he dies. All Judah mourns for him. The prophet Jeremiah writes a lament for Josiah, and singing men and women remember him in their laments for years afterward.

**Judah's Final Kings Bring the Nation to Ruin (2 Chronicles 36:1–21)** — Judah's last kings reject God's messengers until there is no remedy.

After Josiah, Judah's last four kings move quickly toward ruin. Pharaoh Necho removes Josiah's son Jehoahaz after three months. His brother Jehoiakim does evil and is taken in chains by Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon. Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin reigns three months and ten days before he too is taken to Babylon. Then Zedekiah becomes king. He does evil and “humbled not himself before Jeremiah the prophet speaking from the mouth of the LORD.” He rebels against Nebuchadnezzar and hardens his heart. The leaders of the priests and the people also become more and more unfaithful, following the disgusting practices of the nations and polluting the temple.

Yet God keeps calling them. “And the LORD God of their fathers sent to them by his messengers, rising up betimes, and sending; because he had compassion on his people, and on his dwelling place: But they mocked the messengers of God, and despised his words, and misused his prophets, until the wrath of the LORD arose against his people, till there was no remedy.”

So God brings the king of the Chaldeans, who kills their young men with the sword even inside the temple, with no pity for young or old. He carries away all the treasures of the temple and the palace to Babylon. The Babylonians burn the house of God, break down the walls of Jerusalem, and burn all its palaces. The survivors are taken as slaves to Babylon until the Persian kingdom rises. This fulfills “the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah, until the land had enjoyed her sabbaths: for as long as she lay desolate she kept sabbath, to fulfil threescore and ten years.” For all the Sabbath years Israel had ignored, the land finally rests.

**Cyrus Permits God's People to Return (2 Chronicles 36:22–23)** — God moves Cyrus of Persia to let his people return and rebuild the temple.

But the story does not end in ruin. In the first year of Cyrus, king of Persia, “that the word of the LORD spoken by the mouth of Jeremiah might be accomplished, the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia.” Cyrus sends a proclamation throughout his whole kingdom, in writing: “All the kingdoms of the earth hath the LORD God of heaven given me; and he hath charged me to build him an house in Jerusalem, which is in Judah. Who is there among you of all his people? The LORD his God be with him, and let him go up.”

In the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles comes last, so these words end the whole collection with an invitation to go home. The book of Ezra begins by repeating them.

# Ezra
When it happened: From the first year of Cyrus king of Persia (538 BC) to Ezra's arrival in Jerusalem in the seventh year of Artaxerxes (about 458 BC). The temple was finished in 515 BC, and about fifty-seven years pass between chapters 6 and 7.
When it was written: Jewish tradition credits Ezra himself. Chapters 7–9 are partly written in Ezra's own voice. Some official letters are preserved in Aramaic, the language of the Persian government (Ezra 4:8–6:18; 7:12–26). Ezra and Nehemiah were originally one book.
Where: Babylon in the Persian Empire, and Jerusalem and the towns of Judah.
The world at the time: Persia had conquered Babylon and allowed exiled peoples to return home. Judah was a small, poor province surrounded by neighbors who opposed the rebuilding.

**Cyrus Allows God's People to Return Home (Ezra 1:1–2:70)** — After the captivity, God moves Cyrus of Persia to let his people return to Jerusalem and rebuild the temple.

“Now in the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, that the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah might be fulfilled, the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia.” Persia has conquered Babylon, and Cyrus sends a proclamation throughout his kingdom: “The LORD God of heaven hath given me all the kingdoms of the earth; and he hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem.” Any of God's people who want to go back may go. Their neighbors should help them with silver, gold, goods, animals, and gifts for the temple.

The leaders of Judah and Benjamin, the priests, and the Levites get ready to go, “with all them whose spirit God had raised.” Cyrus even brings out the gold and silver vessels Nebuchadnezzar had taken from the temple in Jerusalem, more than five thousand of them, and gives them to Sheshbazzar, the prince of Judah, to carry home.

Chapter 2 lists the families who return with Zerubbabel and Jeshua. Many names are listed by family and hometown, such as the men of Bethlehem and the men of Anathoth. Some priests cannot find their family records, so they are not allowed to serve as priests until a priest with the Urim and Thummim can decide. All together, 42,360 people return, along with 7,337 servants and 200 singers. When they arrive in Jerusalem, some of the family leaders give freewill offerings to rebuild the house of God on its old site. Then everyone settles in their own towns.

**The Altar Is Restored and the Temple Foundation Laid (Ezra 3:1–13)** — The returned exiles restore the altar and lay the temple's foundation with mingled joy and weeping.

In the seventh month, “the people gathered themselves together as one man to Jerusalem.” Jeshua the priest and Zerubbabel the son of Shealtiel rebuild the altar of God on its old foundation. They are afraid of the people living around them, but they offer the morning and evening burnt offerings anyway, as the law of Moses commands. They celebrate the Feast of Tabernacles. Even though the temple's foundation has not been laid, worship has begun again.

They pay masons and carpenters and buy cedar from Lebanon, as Solomon once did. In the second year after their return, they begin to lay the foundation. When it is done, the priests stand in their robes with trumpets and the Levites with cymbals to praise the LORD, as David had arranged. “And they sang together by course in praising and giving thanks unto the LORD; because he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever toward Israel.” The people give a great shout of praise.

But not everyone shouts for joy. “Many of the priests and Levites and chief of the fathers, who were ancient men, that had seen the first house,” Solomon's temple before it was destroyed fifty years earlier, “wept with a loud voice.” The new foundation is so much smaller. Others shout for joy, “So that the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping of the people: for the people shouted with a loud shout, and the noise was heard afar off.”

**Opposition Brings the Work to a Halt (Ezra 4:1–24)** — Opponents of Judah discourage the builders and persuade Persian kings to stop the work.

The enemies of Judah and Benjamin hear that the exiles are building a temple. These are the mixed peoples the kings of Assyria had settled in the land. They come to Zerubbabel and the leaders and say, “Let us build with you: for we seek your God, as ye do.” But Zerubbabel and Jeshua answer, “Ye have nothing to do with us to build an house unto our God.” They say that King Cyrus commanded them to build it themselves.

After that, the people of the land do everything they can to discourage the Jews. They frighten them, hire officials to work against them, and keep them from building throughout the rest of Cyrus's reign and into the reign of Darius.

The chapter then jumps ahead to later years to show that this kind of opposition continued for a long time. Under a later king, Artaxerxes, officials named Rehum and Shimshai write a letter accusing the Jews of rebuilding “the rebellious and the bad city.” They warn that if Jerusalem's walls are rebuilt, the Jews will stop paying taxes. The king has the records searched and finds that “this city of old time hath made insurrection against kings.” He orders the work stopped, and the officials hurry to Jerusalem and force the Jews to stop by force. Returning to the temple story, the chapter ends: “Then ceased the work of the house of God which is at Jerusalem. So it ceased unto the second year of the reign of Darius king of Persia.” For about sixteen years, the temple stands unfinished.

**God's Prophets Encourage the Rebuilding (Ezra 5:1–6:12)** — Haggai and Zechariah stir the people to build again, and Darius confirms Cyrus's decree.

Then the prophets Haggai and Zechariah begin to preach to the Jews in Judah and Jerusalem in the name of the God of Israel. Zerubbabel and Jeshua start building the temple again, “and with them were the prophets of God helping them.”

Soon Tatnai, the Persian governor of the region west of the Euphrates, comes and asks, “Who hath commanded you to build this house, and to make up this wall?” He wants the names of the men in charge. “But the eye of their God was upon the elders of the Jews, that they could not cause them to cease,” until the matter could be reported to King Darius. Tatnai writes to Darius. In the letter, he reports the Jews' answer: “We are the servants of the God of heaven and earth, and build the house that was builded these many years ago.” They explain that their fathers angered God, so he gave them to Nebuchadnezzar, who destroyed the temple. But Cyrus had decreed that it should be rebuilt. Tatnai asks Darius to search the royal records to see whether this is true.

Darius orders a search. In the fortress at Achmetha, a city in Media, they find a scroll with Cyrus's decree. It ordered the temple rebuilt, with its size given, and the costs paid from the king's treasury. The gold and silver vessels were to be returned to their place. So Darius writes back to Tatnai: “Let the work of this house of God alone.” Not only that, the governor must pay the full cost of the building from the royal taxes, and give the priests whatever animals, wheat, salt, wine, and oil they need every day, “That they may offer sacrifices of sweet savours unto the God of heaven, and pray for the life of the king, and of his sons.” Anyone who changes this decree is to have a beam pulled from his house and be impaled on it. Darius ends, “I Darius have made a decree; let it be done with speed.” The very officials who tried to stop the work now have to pay for it.

**The Temple Is Completed and Worship Restored (Ezra 6:13–22)** — The temple is finished and dedicated, and the returned exiles keep the Passover with joy.

Tatnai and his officials obey the king's decree right away. “And the elders of the Jews builded, and they prospered through the prophesying of Haggai the prophet and Zechariah.” They finish the temple according to the command of God and the decrees of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes. The temple is completed in the sixth year of King Darius, in 515 BC, about seventy years after Solomon's temple was destroyed.

The people of Israel, the priests, the Levites, and the rest of the returned exiles dedicate the house of God with joy. They offer a hundred bulls, two hundred rams, and four hundred lambs, and twelve male goats as a sin offering for all Israel, one for each tribe. They appoint the priests and Levites to their divisions for the service of God, “as it is written in the book of Moses.”

In the spring, on the fourteenth day of the first month, they keep the Passover. The priests and Levites purify themselves, and the returned exiles eat it together, along with everyone who has separated himself from the pagan practices of the land to seek the LORD. They celebrate the Feast of Unleavened Bread for seven days with joy, “for the LORD had made them joyful, and turned the heart of the king of Assyria unto them, to strengthen their hands in the work of the house of God.”

**Ezra Is Sent to Teach God's Law (Ezra 7:1–28)** — Ezra, a priest and scribe descended from Aaron, receives the king's support to lead another return and teach God's law.

“Now after these things,” almost sixty years later, during the reign of King Artaxerxes of Persia, a man named Ezra comes up from Babylon. Ezra is a priest descended from Aaron, “and he was a ready scribe in the law of Moses.” A scribe is a scholar who copies and teaches God's law. The king gives him everything he asks for, “according to the hand of the LORD his God upon him.” The journey from Babylon to Jerusalem takes four months, and they arrive safely, “according to the good hand of his God upon him. For Ezra had prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, and to do it, and to teach in Israel statutes and judgments.”

The chapter includes a copy of the king's letter. Artaxerxes allows any Israelite in his kingdom who wants to go to Jerusalem to go with Ezra. He sends silver and gold from the king and his advisers for the temple, plus whatever the Jews in Babylon freely give. Ezra may use it to buy animals for sacrifices. The royal treasurers west of the Euphrates must give Ezra whatever he needs, up to large amounts of silver, wheat, wine, oil, and as much salt as he wants. No taxes may be charged to the priests, Levites, singers, or temple servants. And Ezra must appoint judges who know God's law and teach it to those who do not. Anyone who will not obey God's law and the king's law will be punished.

Ezra responds with praise: “Blessed be the LORD God of our fathers, which hath put such a thing as this in the king's heart, to beautify the house of the LORD which is in Jerusalem.” He is encouraged, because God's hand is on him, and he gathers leaders from Israel to go with him.

**Ezra Leads Another Group Home (Ezra 8:1–36)** — Ezra gathers families and Levites, seeks God's protection, and brings the temple gifts safely to Jerusalem.

Ezra lists the family leaders who go with him from Babylon. He gathers them at the river Ahava and camps there for three days. When he checks the group, he finds that no Levites have come. So he sends men to find Levites and temple servants, and “by the good hand of our God upon us” they bring back several families.

Then Ezra proclaims a fast there by the river, “that we might afflict ourselves before our God, to seek of him a right way for us, and for our little ones, and for all our substance.” The journey is long and dangerous, and they are carrying a fortune in silver and gold. But Ezra says, “For I was ashamed to require of the king a band of soldiers and horsemen to help us against the enemy in the way: because we had spoken unto the king, saying, The hand of our God is upon all them for good that seek him.” He had already told the king that God would protect them, so he would not ask for soldiers. “So we fasted and besought our God for this: and he was intreated of us.”

Ezra chooses twelve leading priests and carefully weighs out to them the silver, gold, and vessels given for the temple, about twenty-five tons of silver and more than three tons of gold. He tells them, “Ye are holy unto the LORD; the vessels are holy also.” They must guard it until they can weigh it again in the temple.

They set out on the twelfth day of the first month. “And the hand of our God was upon us, and he delivered us from the hand of the enemy, and of such as lay in wait by the way.” They arrive in Jerusalem, rest three days, and then weigh the treasure in the temple. Every piece is accounted for. The returned exiles offer burnt offerings to the God of Israel, and they deliver the king's orders to the governors, who help the people and the house of God.

**Ezra Confronts Israel's Unfaithfulness (Ezra 9:1–15)** — Ezra learns that the people have intermarried with the surrounding nations and confesses their sin before God.

After these things are done, some of the leaders come to Ezra with bad news. “The people of Israel, and the priests, and the Levites, have not separated themselves from the people of the lands.” They have married women from the surrounding nations, who worship other gods, the very thing God's law forbade. “The holy seed have mingled themselves with the people of those lands.” And the leaders and officials have led the way in this unfaithfulness.

When Ezra hears this, he tears his clothes and his cloak, pulls hair from his head and beard, and sits down in shock. Everyone who trembles at the words of the God of Israel gathers around him, and he sits there stunned until the evening sacrifice.

Then Ezra gets up, falls on his knees, spreads out his hands to the LORD, and prays. “O my God, I am ashamed and blush to lift up my face to thee, my God: for our iniquities are increased over our head, and our trespass is grown up unto the heavens.” Ezra says “our” sins, not “their” sins, though he himself has not done this. He reminds God that Israel's sins brought the exile. “And now for a little space grace hath been shewed from the LORD our God, to leave us a remnant to escape.” God has given them “a little reviving in our bondage” and moved the kings of Persia to let them rebuild the temple and give them “a wall in Judah and in Jerusalem.”

But now they have broken God's commands again. He admits that “thou our God hast punished us less than our iniquities deserve.” If they keep sinning, what hope do they have? He ends, “O LORD God of Israel, thou art righteous: for we remain yet escaped, as it is this day: behold, we are before thee in our trespasses: for we cannot stand before thee because of this.”

**The People Respond to Ezra's Prayer (Ezra 10:1–44)** — The people confess their sin and agree to send away their foreign wives.

While Ezra is praying and weeping, lying on the ground in front of the temple, a large crowd of men, women, and children gathers around him, “for the people wept very sore.” A man named Shechaniah speaks up: “We have trespassed against our God, and have taken strange wives of the people of the land: yet now there is hope in Israel concerning this thing.” He proposes that they make a covenant with God to send away their foreign wives and the children born to them, according to the law. He tells Ezra, “Arise; for this matter belongeth unto thee: we also will be with thee: be of good courage, and do it.” Ezra makes the leaders, priests, and Levites swear to do it. Then he goes into a room and fasts, mourning over the people's unfaithfulness.

A proclamation goes out: all the returned exiles must gather in Jerusalem within three days, or lose their property and be cut off from the community. On the twentieth day of the ninth month, in winter, all the men sit in the open square in front of the temple, “trembling because of this matter, and for the great rain.” Ezra stands up and tells them, “Ye have transgressed, and have taken strange wives, to increase the trespass of Israel.” He calls them to confess to the LORD, do his will, and separate themselves from the foreign wives. The whole assembly answers with a loud voice, “As thou hast said, so must we do.” But they point out that there are many people, it is raining hard, and this cannot be done in a day or two. So they agree that leaders will handle each case, town by town, with the elders and judges of each city. Only Jonathan and Jahaziah are named as “employed about this matter.”

It takes three months to examine every case. The book ends with a list of the men who had married foreign women, including priests, Levites, singers, and gatekeepers, more than a hundred names. The last verse says simply, “and some of them had wives by whom they had children.” Ezra ends on a hard and painful note. The people are back in their land and the temple is rebuilt, but they are still struggling to stay faithful. The story continues in Nehemiah.

# Nehemiah
When it happened: From the twentieth year of Artaxerxes king of Persia (445 BC) to some time after his thirty-second year (about 433 BC; Nehemiah 13:6).
When it was written: Nehemiah wrote it, by Jewish tradition finishing the book Ezra began. Most of the book is Nehemiah's own first-person account, introduced as "The words of Nehemiah" (Nehemiah 1:1). In the Hebrew Bible it was originally joined with Ezra.
Where: The Persian palace at Shushan (Susa), and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Nearly a century after the first exiles returned, Jerusalem's walls still lay in ruins. Local officials, Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite, and Geshem the Arabian, opposed any effort to make the city strong again.

**Nehemiah Prays for Jerusalem's Restoration (Nehemiah 1:1–2:20)** — Grieved by news of Jerusalem's ruined walls, Nehemiah prays, receives the king's support, and rallies the people to rebuild.

“The words of Nehemiah the son of Hachaliah.” Nehemiah is a Jew living in the Persian royal palace at Shushan. He serves King Artaxerxes as his cupbearer, the trusted official who tastes the king's wine to make sure it is not poisoned. One winter day, his brother Hanani arrives from Judah with news. Nehemiah asks about the Jews who returned from exile and about Jerusalem. The answer breaks his heart: “The remnant that are left of the captivity there in the province are in great affliction and reproach: the wall of Jerusalem also is broken down, and the gates thereof are burned with fire.” Almost a hundred years after the first exiles came home, the city still lies open and unprotected.

Nehemiah writes, “I sat down and wept, and mourned certain days, and fasted, and prayed before the God of heaven.” He prays, “O LORD God of heaven, the great and terrible God, that keepeth covenant and mercy for them that love him.” He confesses that Israel, including his own family, has sinned. Then he reminds God of his promise through Moses: if his scattered people return to him, he will gather them back. He ends by asking God to give him success when he speaks to the king.

Four months later, Nehemiah is serving wine to the king, and his face is sad, which was dangerous in the king's presence. The king asks, “Why is thy countenance sad, seeing thou art not sick? this is nothing else but sorrow of heart.” Nehemiah writes, “Then I was very sore afraid.” He explains that the city where his ancestors are buried lies in ruins. The king asks, “For what dost thou make request?” Nehemiah says, “So I prayed to the God of heaven,” a quick, silent prayer, and then asks to be sent to rebuild the city. The king agrees and gives him letters for safe travel and for timber from the royal forest, “according to the good hand of my God upon me.” He even sends army officers and horsemen with him.

But two local officials, Sanballat the Horonite and Tobiah the Ammonite, hear about it, and “it grieved them exceedingly that there was come a man to seek the welfare of the children of Israel.” Three days after arriving in Jerusalem, Nehemiah rides out secretly at night with a few men to inspect the walls. In some places, the rubble is so bad that his animal cannot get through. Then he gathers the leaders and says, “Ye see the distress that we are in, how Jerusalem lieth waste... come, and let us build up the wall of Jerusalem, that we be no more a reproach.” He tells them how God's hand has been with him and what the king said. They answer, “Let us rise up and build. So they strengthened their hands for this good work.” When Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem the Arabian mock them and accuse them of rebelling against the king, Nehemiah answers, “The God of heaven, he will prosper us; therefore we his servants will arise and build.”

**Jerusalem's Families Rebuild the Walls (Nehemiah 3:1–32)** — Families from every part of the community repair assigned sections of Jerusalem's gates and walls.

This chapter lists who rebuilt each part of the wall and its gates, moving around the city in a circle. The high priest Eliashib and his fellow priests begin by rebuilding the Sheep Gate and dedicating it. Then, again and again, the list says “next unto them” another group worked. Families, towns, and trade groups each take a section. There are men from Jericho and Gibeon, goldsmiths and perfume makers, merchants and temple servants. A ruler named Shallum works on his section, “he and his daughters.” Many people repair the part of the wall right in front of their own houses. But not everyone helps. Of the men from the town of Tekoa, it says, “their nobles put not their necks to the work of their Lord.” Still, nearly everyone in the city has a part, and section by section the wall begins to rise.

**Opposition Threatens the Rebuilding Effort (Nehemiah 4:1–23)** — The builders keep working under mockery and threats, with weapons close at hand.

When Sanballat hears the wall is going up, he is furious and mocks the Jews in front of the Samaritan army: “What do these feeble Jews? will they fortify themselves?... will they revive the stones out of the heaps of the rubbish which are burned?” Tobiah, standing beside him, adds, “Even that which they build, if a fox go up, he shall even break down their stone wall.” Nehemiah prays, “Hear, O our God; for we are despised.” And the work goes on. “So built we the wall; and all the wall was joined together unto the half thereof: for the people had a mind to work.”

When the enemies see the gaps closing, they plot to attack Jerusalem and cause confusion. Nehemiah writes, “Nevertheless we made our prayer unto our God, and set a watch against them day and night.” But the workers of Judah are worn out: “The strength of the bearers of burdens is decayed, and there is much rubbish; so that we are not able to build the wall.” Jews who live near the enemies warn them ten times that an attack is coming.

So Nehemiah posts families behind the lowest parts of the wall with swords, spears, and bows. He tells them, “Be not ye afraid of them: remember the Lord, which is great and terrible, and fight for your brethren, your sons, and your daughters, your wives, and your houses.” When the enemies learn that their plot has been discovered, they give up, and everyone goes back to work.

From then on, half of Nehemiah's men work while the other half stand guard with spears, shields, bows, and armor. The people carrying loads work with one hand and hold a weapon in the other, “every one with one of his hands wrought in the work, and with the other hand held a weapon.” Every builder wears a sword at his side. A trumpeter stays beside Nehemiah. If they hear the trumpet, everyone is to run to that spot, “our God shall fight for us.” They work from dawn until the stars come out. Nehemiah and his men never take off their clothes, except to wash.

**Nehemiah Confronts Injustice Among the People (Nehemiah 5:1–19)** — Nehemiah stops the wealthy from exploiting their poor neighbors and sets an example of generosity.

Then a new crisis comes, this time from inside. There is “a great cry of the people and of their wives against their brethren the Jews.” Because of a famine, some families have mortgaged their fields, vineyards, and houses to buy grain. Others have borrowed money to pay the king's taxes. Now they are forced to sell their own sons and daughters into slavery to their richer Jewish neighbors, and some of their daughters have already been taken. “Neither is it in our power to redeem them; for other men have our lands and vineyards.”

Nehemiah is “very angry.” He thinks it over, then confronts the nobles and officials: “Ye exact usury, every one of his brother.” He calls a large assembly and says that while he has been buying back Jews who were sold to foreigners, they are selling their own brothers. “It is not good that ye do: ought ye not to walk in the fear of our God because of the reproach of the heathen our enemies?” He demands that they give back the fields, vineyards, olive groves, and houses, and stop charging interest. They answer, “We will restore them, and will require nothing of them; so will we do as thou sayest.” Nehemiah makes the priests put them under oath. Then he shakes out the folds of his robe and says that God will shake out anyone who breaks this promise. The people say, “Amen,” and praise the LORD.

Nehemiah points to his own example. For twelve years as governor, he and his family never took the food allowance that governors were given. Earlier governors had placed heavy burdens on the people, “but so did not I, because of the fear of God.” Instead, he fed 150 officials and visitors at his own table every day, paying for it himself. He prays, “Think upon me, my God, for good, according to all that I have done for this people.”

**The Wall Is Completed Despite Opposition (Nehemiah 6:1–19)** — Nehemiah refuses every scheme to stop him, and the wall is finished in fifty-two days.

The wall is finished, except for hanging the doors in the gates. Sanballat and Geshem send a message inviting Nehemiah to meet them in a village on the plain of Ono, far from Jerusalem. “But they thought to do me mischief.” Nehemiah replies, “I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down: why should the work cease, whilst I leave it, and come down to you?” They send the same invitation four times, and he gives the same answer each time.

The fifth time, Sanballat's servant brings an open letter, meant to be read by everyone along the way. It claims there are rumors that the Jews plan to rebel and that Nehemiah wants to make himself king, and that the king of Persia will hear about it. Nehemiah answers, “There are no such things done as thou sayest, but thou feignest them out of thine own heart.” He knows they are trying to frighten the people into stopping the work. So he prays, “Now therefore, O God, strengthen my hands.”

Then a man named Shemaiah, who has been paid by Tobiah and Sanballat, warns Nehemiah that assassins are coming at night and urges him to hide inside the temple. Only priests were allowed there, so hiding would be a sin and would make him look like a coward. Nehemiah refuses: “Should such a man as I flee? and who is there, that, being as I am, would go into the temple to save his life? I will not go in.” He realizes that God did not send Shemaiah, and that a prophetess named Noadiah and other prophets are also trying to frighten him.

“So the wall was finished in the twenty and fifth day of the month Elul, in fifty and two days.” When their enemies hear about it, the surrounding nations are afraid, “for they perceived that this work was wrought of our God.” But Tobiah still has friends among the nobles of Judah, who exchange letters with him and keep praising him to Nehemiah, while Tobiah keeps sending letters to frighten him.

**Jerusalem's Gates and People Are Organized (Nehemiah 7:1–73)** — Nehemiah sets guards over the city and turns to the register of families who first returned.

Now that the wall is built and the doors are hung, Nehemiah appoints gatekeepers, singers, and Levites. He puts his brother Hanani and Hananiah, the ruler of the palace, in charge of Jerusalem, because Hananiah “was a faithful man, and feared God above many.” He orders that the gates not be opened until the sun is hot, and that they be shut and barred while guards are still on duty. Residents are assigned to stand watch near their own houses.

Jerusalem is large, but few people live there, and many houses have not been rebuilt. God puts it into Nehemiah's heart to gather the people and register them by families. He finds the record of those who first returned from exile with Zerubbabel, and the chapter repeats that list, much like Ezra 2. In all, the community numbers 42,360 people, besides servants and singers. Some leaders, the governor, and the people give gold, silver, and priestly garments for the work. Then the people settle in their towns. When the seventh month comes, the Israelites are living in their cities.

**Ezra Reads God's Law Before the People (Nehemiah 8:1–18)** — Ezra reads the law, the Levites explain it, and the people's mourning turns into joy.

In the seventh month, “all the people gathered themselves together as one man into the street that was before the water gate.” They ask Ezra the scribe to bring the book of the law of Moses. Ezra stands on a tall wooden platform built for the occasion. When he opens the book, all the people stand up. Ezra blesses the LORD, the great God, “And all the people answered, Amen, Amen, with lifting up their hands.” Then they bow their heads and worship with their faces to the ground.

Ezra reads from early morning until noon to the men, women, and all the children old enough to understand, “and the ears of all the people were attentive unto the book of the law.” Levites move among the people helping them understand: “So they read in the book in the law of God distinctly, and gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading.” Many had grown up in exile, and the Levites made sure everyone understood.

As they hear God's law, the people begin to weep. But Nehemiah, Ezra, and the Levites tell them, “This day is holy unto the LORD your God; mourn not, nor weep.” “Go your way, eat the fat, and drink the sweet, and send portions unto them for whom nothing is prepared: for this day is holy unto our Lord: neither be ye sorry; for the joy of the LORD is your strength.” So the people go away to eat, drink, share food with others, “and to make great mirth, because they had understood the words.”

The next day the family leaders gather with Ezra to study the law more closely. They discover that God commanded Israel to live in shelters during the Feast of Tabernacles. So the people go out, gather branches of olive, myrtle, and palm, and build booths on their roofs, in their courtyards, in the temple courts, and in the city squares. “For since the days of Jeshua the son of Nun unto that day had not the children of Israel done so. And there was very great gladness.” Every day of the feast, Ezra reads to them from the book of God's law.

**Israel Confesses Its Sins and Remembers God's Mercy (Nehemiah 9:1–38)** — The people fast and confess, recounting God's faithfulness through all of Israel's history.

On the twenty-fourth day of the same month, the Israelites gather again, this time fasting, wearing sackcloth, with dust on their heads. They separate themselves from foreigners and stand to confess their sins and the sins of their ancestors. For a quarter of the day they read from the book of the law, and for another quarter they confess and worship.

Then the Levites call out, “Stand up and bless the LORD your God for ever and ever.” They lead the people in a long prayer that retells Israel's whole history. “Thou, even thou, art LORD alone; thou hast made heaven, the heaven of heavens, with all their host, the earth, and all things that are therein.” God chose Abram and kept his promise to him. He saw his people suffering in Egypt and divided the sea before them. He led them with a pillar of cloud by day and fire by night, spoke to them at Sinai, and gave them bread from heaven and water from the rock.

But again and again, their ancestors rebelled. They made a golden calf and wanted to go back to slavery in Egypt. “But thou art a God ready to pardon, gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and forsookest them not.” For forty years in the wilderness, “their clothes waxed not old, and their feet swelled not.” God gave them the land, with houses full of good things. Still they rebelled, threw his law behind their backs, and killed the prophets who warned them. So God handed them over to their enemies. But when they cried out, he rescued them, “and many times didst thou deliver them according to thy mercies.”

The prayer ends in the present. Even now, they are servants in the very land God gave their ancestors, paying heavy taxes to foreign kings. “Behold, we are servants this day,” “and we are in great distress.” “And because of all this we make a sure covenant, and write it.” The leaders, Levites, and priests put their seals on it.

**The People Renew Their Covenant with God (Nehemiah 10:1–39)** — Leaders and families sign a covenant to keep God's law and support his house.

Nehemiah the governor signs the covenant first, followed by the priests, the Levites, and the leaders of the people. The rest of the people, along with their wives and children who are old enough to understand, join them in a solemn oath “to walk in God's law, which was given by Moses.”

They make specific promises. They will not let their sons and daughters marry the peoples of the land who worship other gods. They will not buy goods from traders on the Sabbath or holy days. Every seventh year, they will let the land rest and cancel debts. They will each give a third of a shekel every year to support the temple's offerings. They cast lots to decide which families will bring wood for the altar fire at set times. They will bring the firstfruits of their crops and trees, their firstborn sons and animals, and tithes of everything to the priests and Levites. The covenant ends with a promise that sums up all the others: “we will not forsake the house of our God.”

**Families Settle in Jerusalem and Judah (Nehemiah 11:1–12:26)** — Jerusalem is repopulated, and the families who serve in its worship are recorded.

Jerusalem has strong walls now, but not enough people to fill it or defend it. The leaders of the people already live there. The rest of the people cast lots so that one out of every ten will move into the holy city, while the other nine stay in their towns. Some volunteer, “And the people blessed all the men, that willingly offered themselves to dwell at Jerusalem.”

The chapter lists the families from Judah and Benjamin who settle in Jerusalem, along with the priests, the Levites, the gatekeepers, and the singers who lead worship at the temple. It also lists the towns and villages where the rest of the people live. Chapter 12 then records the priests and Levites who came back with Zerubbabel and Jeshua almost a century before, and their descendants down to Nehemiah's own day. These lists connect the people of Nehemiah's time to the faithful families who returned first.

**Jerusalem's Rebuilt Wall Is Dedicated to God (Nehemiah 12:27–47)** — The wall is dedicated with two great choirs, sacrifices, and joy heard far away.

Now it is time to dedicate the wall. The Levites are gathered from wherever they live to celebrate “with gladness, both with thanksgivings, and with singing, with cymbals, psalteries, and with harps.” The priests and Levites purify themselves, the people, the gates, and the wall.

Nehemiah brings the leaders up onto the wall and forms two great choirs to give thanks. One choir, led by Ezra the scribe, walks along the top of the wall to the right. The other, with Nehemiah and half the leaders, walks to the left. They march around the city in opposite directions, singing and playing trumpets, until they meet at the temple. There they offer great sacrifices and rejoice, “for God had made them rejoice with great joy: the wives also and the children rejoiced: so that the joy of Jerusalem was heard even afar off.” The wall that their enemies said a fox could knock down now carries a parade.

That day, men are appointed to take charge of the storerooms for the offerings, firstfruits, and tithes, because the people of Judah are glad to support the priests and Levites who serve. The singers and gatekeepers serve as David and Solomon had arranged long ago, and all Israel gives them their daily portions.

**Nehemiah Confronts Renewed Disobedience (Nehemiah 13:1–31)** — Returning after an absence, Nehemiah confronts a series of renewed violations of the covenant.

That day, they read from the book of Moses that no Ammonite or Moabite should ever enter the assembly of God, because those nations hired Balaam to curse Israel, “howbeit our God turned the curse into a blessing.” So the people separate from those of foreign descent.

But then Nehemiah has to go back to Persia to serve the king. After some time, he returns to Jerusalem and finds that things have slipped badly. Eliashib the priest, who is related to Tobiah, has given Tobiah, the same enemy who fought the wall, a large room inside the temple courts, the room where offerings were supposed to be stored. Nehemiah writes, “it grieved me sore: therefore I cast forth all the household stuff of Tobiah out of the chamber.” He has the rooms purified and the temple supplies put back.

He also learns that the Levites have not been given their portions, so they have gone back to farming to survive. He confronts the officials: “Why is the house of God forsaken?” He brings the Levites back, and the people bring their tithes again. Then he sees people treading winepresses on the Sabbath, loading donkeys, and bringing goods into Jerusalem to sell. Men from Tyre sell fish to the people of Judah on the Sabbath. Nehemiah confronts the nobles: “What evil thing is this that ye do, and profane the sabbath day?” He reminds them that this is exactly the kind of sin that brought disaster on their ancestors. He orders the city gates shut before the Sabbath begins and posts his own servants to guard them. When merchants camp outside the wall waiting, he warns them, “If ye do so again, I will lay hands on you.” After that they stop coming on the Sabbath.

Finally, Nehemiah sees Jewish men who have married women from Ashdod, Ammon, and Moab. Their children speak half in the language of Ashdod and cannot speak the language of the Jews. Nehemiah is furious: “I contended with them, and cursed them, and smote certain of them, and plucked off their hair, and made them swear by God.” He reminds them that even Solomon, the greatest king, was led into sin by foreign wives: “even him did outlandish women cause to sin.” He discovers that a grandson of Eliashib the high priest has married the daughter of Sanballat, “therefore I chased him from me.” He purifies the priests and Levites and assigns their duties.

Nehemiah ends his book the way he prayed throughout it: “Remember me, O my God, for good.” The walls are standing, but the work of keeping God's people faithful is never finished.

# Esther
When it happened: During the reign of King Ahasuerus of Persia, usually identified with Xerxes I (486–465 BC), from his third year (Esther 1:3) to his twelfth year and after.
When it was written: Jewish tradition credits the men of the Great Assembly, the leaders after the return from exile. The book notes that "Mordecai wrote these things" in letters to the Jews (Esther 9:20); it was likely written in the Persian period not long after the events.
Where: Shushan (Susa), the royal city of the Persian Empire.
The world at the time: Persia ruled 127 provinces "from India even unto Ethiopia" (Esther 1:1). Many Jews still lived scattered throughout the empire after the exile, and a royal law, once sealed, could not be reversed.

**King Ahasuerus Removes Queen Vashti (Esther 1:1–22)** — At a royal feast in Shushan, Queen Vashti refuses the king's summons, and Ahasuerus removes her.

“Now it came to pass in the days of Ahasuerus, (this is Ahasuerus which reigned, from India even unto Ethiopia, over an hundred and seven and twenty provinces:)” In the third year of his reign, the king of Persia throws an enormous party for his officials and military leaders. For 180 days he shows off the riches of his kingdom. Then he holds a seven-day feast in the palace garden at Shushan for everyone in the city, from the greatest to the least. The garden is decorated with white, green, and blue curtains, marble pillars, and couches of gold and silver on a pavement of colored stone. The wine flows freely from golden cups, and by the king's order no one is forced to drink. Meanwhile, Queen Vashti holds a separate feast for the women.

On the seventh day, “when the heart of the king was merry with wine,” he orders seven servants to bring Queen Vashti before him, wearing her royal crown, “to shew the people and the princes her beauty: for she was fair to look on.” “But the queen Vashti refused to come at the king's commandment.” “Therefore was the king very wroth, and his anger burned in him.”

The king asks his wise men what the law says should be done. One of them, Memucan, says that Vashti has wronged not only the king but every man in the empire. When the women of Persia hear about it, they will look down on their own husbands. He advises the king to issue a royal decree that Vashti may never again come before him, and to give her royal position to someone better. The king likes the idea. He sends letters to every province, in every language, declaring “that every man should bear rule in his own house.”

**Esther Becomes Queen of Persia (Esther 2:1–23)** — Esther, a Jewish orphan raised by her cousin Mordecai, becomes queen without revealing her people.

Later, when the king's anger cools, he remembers Vashti and what was decreed against her. His servants suggest a search for beautiful young virgins throughout the empire. They will be brought to the palace and given beauty treatments, “And let the maiden which pleaseth the king be queen instead of Vashti.” The king agrees.

In Shushan lives a Jew named Mordecai from the tribe of Benjamin. His family was carried into exile from Jerusalem with King Jeconiah. Mordecai has raised his orphaned cousin Hadassah, also called Esther, as his own daughter. “The maid was fair and beautiful.” Esther is taken to the palace with the other young women and placed in the care of Hegai, the official in charge of the women. Hegai likes her and gives her special treatment. Esther does not tell anyone that she is Jewish, because Mordecai told her not to. Every day Mordecai walks in front of the court of the women's house to find out how she is doing.

Each young woman goes through twelve months of beauty treatments, “six months with oil of myrrh, and six months with sweet odours,” before spending one night with the king. Afterward, she goes to a second harem and never sees the king again unless he calls for her by name. When Esther's turn comes, she asks for nothing except what Hegai advises. “And Esther obtained favour in the sight of all them that looked upon her.” In the king's seventh year, she is taken to him, “And the king loved Esther above all the women, and she obtained grace and favour in his sight... so that he set the royal crown upon her head, and made her queen instead of Vashti.” He throws a great feast in her honor.

One day, while Mordecai is sitting at the king's gate, he overhears two of the king's doorkeepers, Bigthan and Teresh, plotting to assassinate the king. He tells Queen Esther, and she tells the king in Mordecai's name. The plot is confirmed, and both men are hanged. The event is “written in the book of the chronicles before the king,” and then everyone forgets about it.

**Haman Plans to Destroy the Jews (Esther 3:1–15)** — When Mordecai refuses to bow, Haman obtains a decree to destroy every Jew in the empire.

Some time later, King Ahasuerus promotes a man named Haman, the son of Hammedatha the Agagite, and gives him a seat above all the other officials. Agag was the Amalekite king Saul spared long ago, and the Amalekites had been Israel's enemies since the days of Moses. Mordecai, like Saul, is a Benjamite descended from Kish. The king commands everyone at the gate to bow down to Haman. “But Mordecai bowed not, nor did him reverence.” The other servants ask him day after day why he disobeys the king's command, and he explains that he is a Jew.

When Haman sees that Mordecai will not bow, “then was Haman full of wrath.” But it is not enough for him to punish one man. Since he has learned Mordecai's people, he decides to destroy all the Jews throughout the whole kingdom. In the first month, they cast Pur, which means the lot, before Haman to choose the day for the slaughter. The lot falls on the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, Adar, almost a year away.

Haman goes to the king and says, “There is a certain people scattered abroad and dispersed among the people in all the provinces of thy kingdom; and their laws are diverse from all people; neither keep they the king's laws: therefore it is not for the king's profit to suffer them.” He offers to pay ten thousand talents of silver, a huge fortune, into the royal treasury if the king will order them destroyed. The king takes off his signet ring and gives it to Haman, saying the money and the people are his to do with as he likes.

The king's scribes write letters in every language and send them by couriers to every province. The order is “to destroy, to kill, and to cause to perish, all Jews, both young and old, little children and women, in one day,” and to take their property. Then comes a chilling line: “And the king and Haman sat down to drink; but the city Shushan was perplexed.”

**Mordecai Calls Esther to Act (Esther 4:1–17)** — Mordecai urges Esther to risk her life for her people, and she resolves to go to the king.

When Mordecai learns what has happened, he tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth and ashes, and goes out into the city, crying “with a loud and a bitter cry.” He goes as far as the king's gate, but no one in sackcloth is allowed inside. In every province, the Jews mourn, fast, weep, and lie in sackcloth and ashes.

Esther's servants tell her about Mordecai, and she is deeply troubled. She sends him clothes to wear, but he refuses them. So she sends Hatach, one of her attendants, to find out what is wrong. Mordecai tells him everything, even the exact amount of money Haman promised to pay, and gives him a copy of the decree to show Esther. He tells her to go to the king and beg for mercy for her people.

Esther sends back a message: everyone in the empire knows that anyone who goes to the king in the inner court without being called will be put to death, “except such to whom the king shall hold out the golden sceptre, that he may live.” And the king has not called for her in thirty days.

Mordecai answers, “Think not with thyself that thou shalt escape in the king's house, more than all the Jews. For if thou altogether holdest thy peace at this time, then shall there enlargement and deliverance arise to the Jews from another place; but thou and thy father's house shall be destroyed: and who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?” God's name never appears in the book of Esther, but Mordecai's words suggest that someone greater is at work.

Esther decides. She sends Mordecai this answer: “Go, gather together all the Jews that are present in Shushan, and fast ye for me, and neither eat nor drink three days, night or day: I also and my maidens will fast likewise; and so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish.” Mordecai goes and does everything she asked.

**Esther Invites the King and Haman to a Feast (Esther 5:1–14)** — The king receives Esther, and Haman, enraged by Mordecai, builds a gallows.

On the third day, Esther puts on her royal robes and stands in the inner court of the palace, facing the king's hall. The king is sitting on his throne. When he sees Queen Esther standing there, “she obtained favour in his sight: and the king held out to Esther the golden sceptre that was in his hand.” Esther comes near and touches the top of the scepter. The king asks, “What wilt thou, queen Esther? and what is thy request? it shall be even given thee to the half of the kingdom.”

Esther does not ask right away. Instead, she invites the king and Haman to a banquet she has prepared that day. The king brings Haman quickly. During the wine, the king asks again what she wants. Esther asks them both to come to another banquet the next day, and then she will answer.

Haman leaves the banquet “joyful and with a glad heart.” But at the king's gate he sees Mordecai, who will not stand up or even move for him, and he is filled with rage. He holds it in and goes home. He calls his friends and his wife Zeresh and boasts about his great wealth, his many sons, his promotions, and that he is the only guest the queen invited to her banquets with the king. Then he says, “Yet all this availeth me nothing, so long as I see Mordecai the Jew sitting at the king's gate.” His wife and friends suggest building a gallows fifty cubits high, about seventy-five feet, and asking the king in the morning to have Mordecai hanged on it. Then Haman can go merrily to the banquet. “And the thing pleased Haman; and he caused the gallows to be made.”

**Mordecai Receives an Unexpected Honor (Esther 6:1–14)** — On a sleepless night the king learns of Mordecai's loyalty, and Haman is made to honor him.

“On that night could not the king sleep.” He orders the book of the royal records brought and read to him. It happens to include the account of how Mordecai exposed the plot to assassinate the king. The king asks, “What honour and dignity hath been done to Mordecai for this?” His servants answer, “There is nothing done for him.”

Just then, Haman arrives in the outer court of the palace, coming to ask the king to hang Mordecai on the gallows he has built. The king asks who is in the court, and his servants say it is Haman. The king tells them to bring him in and asks him, “What shall be done unto the man whom the king delighteth to honour?” Haman thinks, “To whom would the king delight to do honour more than to myself?” So he describes the honor he wants for himself. The man should wear a royal robe the king has worn and ride a horse the king has ridden, with a royal crown on its head. One of the king's most noble officials should lead him through the city square, shouting, “Thus shall it be done to the man whom the king delighteth to honour.”

The king says, “Make haste, and take the apparel and the horse, as thou hast said, and do even so to Mordecai the Jew, that sitteth at the king's gate.” Haman has no choice. He dresses Mordecai in the king's robe, puts him on the king's horse, and leads him through the streets, calling out the words he had hoped would be said of himself.

Afterward, Mordecai goes back to the king's gate, but Haman hurries home in grief with his head covered. When he tells his wife and friends what happened, they say, “If Mordecai be of the seed of the Jews, before whom thou hast begun to fall, thou shalt not prevail against him, but shalt surely fall before him.” While they are still talking, the king's servants arrive and rush Haman off to Esther's banquet.

**Esther Exposes Haman's Plot (Esther 7:1–10)** — At the second banquet Esther reveals that she is a Jew, and Haman is hanged on his own gallows.

At the second banquet, as they drink wine, the king asks again, “What is thy petition, queen Esther? and it shall be granted thee.” This time Esther answers: “let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request: For we are sold, I and my people, to be destroyed, to be slain, and to perish.” If they had only been sold as slaves, she says, she would have kept quiet.

The king demands, “Who is he, and where is he, that durst presume in his heart to do so?” Esther points: “The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman.” Haman is terrified. The king gets up in a rage and goes out into the palace garden. Haman stays behind to beg Queen Esther for his life, because he can see the king has already decided his fate.

When the king comes back in, Haman has fallen onto the couch where Esther is reclining. The king shouts, “Will he force the queen also before me in the house?” As soon as the words leave his mouth, the servants cover Haman's face. One of them, Harbonah, mentions that Haman has built a gallows seventy-five feet high at his house for Mordecai, the man who saved the king's life. The king says, “Hang him thereon.” “So they hanged Haman on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai. Then was the king's wrath pacified.”

**Esther and Mordecai Secure a New Decree (Esther 8:1–17)** — Because the first decree cannot be revoked, a second decree lets the Jews defend themselves.

That same day, King Ahasuerus gives Haman's house to Queen Esther. Esther tells the king that Mordecai is her cousin, and Mordecai comes before the king. The king takes off his signet ring, which he had taken back from Haman, and gives it to Mordecai. Esther puts Mordecai in charge of Haman's house.

But the danger is not over. Haman's decree is still law, and the day for killing the Jews is coming. Esther falls at the king's feet, weeping, and begs him to stop Haman's evil plan. The king holds out the golden scepter again. Esther asks him to cancel the letters Haman wrote. “For how can I endure to see the evil that shall come unto my people?” But in Persia, a law sealed with the king's ring cannot be reversed: “for the writing which is written in the king's name, and sealed with the king's ring, may no man reverse.” So the king tells Esther and Mordecai to write a new decree in his name, however they wish.

Mordecai writes a new law and sends it by couriers on the king's fastest horses to every province, in every language. It gives the Jews in every city the right to gather together and defend themselves on the same day, the thirteenth of Adar, against anyone who attacks them, and to take the property of their attackers.

Mordecai leaves the king's presence wearing royal robes of blue and white, a great gold crown, and a purple linen cloak, and the city of Shushan rejoices. “The Jews had light, and gladness, and joy, and honour.” In every province where the decree arrives, the Jews celebrate with feasting. “And many of the people of the land became Jews; for the fear of the Jews fell upon them.”

**The Jews Defend Themselves and Establish Purim (Esther 9:1–32)** — On the day meant for their destruction, the Jews overcome their enemies and establish the feast of Purim.

The thirteenth day of Adar arrives, the very day the enemies of the Jews had hoped to destroy them. Instead, “the Jews had rule over them that hated them.” They gather in their cities to defend themselves, and no one can stand against them. Even the governors and officials help them, because they fear Mordecai, who has become powerful in the palace.

In Shushan, the Jews kill five hundred men, including Haman's ten sons. But the book stresses, “on the spoil laid they not their hand,” even though the king's decree allowed it. When the king tells Esther what has happened and asks what else she wants, she asks that the Jews in Shushan be allowed one more day to defend themselves, and that Haman's sons' bodies be hung on the gallows. The next day the Jews in Shushan kill three hundred more. In the other provinces, they kill seventy-five thousand of those who hated them, but again they take no plunder. Then they rest.

The Jews in the provinces rest and celebrate on the fourteenth of Adar, and the Jews in Shushan on the fifteenth. Mordecai sends letters to all the Jews in the empire, telling them to celebrate these two days every year, as days “of feasting and joy, and of sending portions one to another, and gifts to the poor.” Their sorrow had been turned to joy and their mourning into a holiday. The feast is named Purim, from Pur, the lot Haman cast to choose the day of their destruction. Queen Esther adds her authority in a second letter. The Jews agree to keep these days “throughout every generation, every family, every province, and every city.” Jewish people still celebrate Purim today, reading the book of Esther aloud and drowning out Haman's name with noisemakers whenever it is read.

**Mordecai Becomes a Great Leader in Persia (Esther 10:1–3)** — Mordecai rises to second in the kingdom and works for the good of his people.

King Ahasuerus places a tax on the land and the islands of the sea. All his acts of power, and the full account of how he honored Mordecai, are written in the official records of the kings of Media and Persia.

The book ends with Mordecai, the Jew who would not bow to Haman, raised to second in command of the whole empire: “For Mordecai the Jew was next unto king Ahasuerus, and great among the Jews, and accepted of the multitude of his brethren, seeking the wealth of his people, and speaking peace to all his seed.” God is never mentioned by name in the book of Esther. Yet through a sleepless night, a forgotten record, and a brave queen, the story shows him quietly protecting his people.
