# 1 Kings
When it happened: From the last days of King David (about 970 BC) through Solomon's reign and the division of the kingdom, to the reigns of Ahaziah in Israel and Jehoshaphat in Judah (about 850 BC).
When it was written: 1 and 2 Kings were originally one book, and Jewish tradition credits Jeremiah. The writer cites earlier sources such as "the book of the acts of Solomon" (1 Kings 11:41). Because 2 Kings ends in the Babylonian exile, the finished work dates to about 560 BC or later.
Where: Jerusalem, Samaria, the shrines at Bethel and Dan, Mount Carmel, Mount Horeb, Jezreel, and the Phoenician cities of Tyre, Sidon, and Zarephath.
The world at the time: Solomon traded with Hiram king of Tyre and married Pharaoh's daughter. After the kingdom split, Egypt's Shishak invaded Judah (1 Kings 14:25), Syria rose at Damascus, and Baal worship spread from Sidon through Ahab's wife Jezebel.

**Solomon Becomes King After David (1 Kings 1:1–2:46)** — As David approaches death, a struggle over the throne ends with Solomon becoming king and securing his position.

When David was old, his son Adonijah declared himself king with the support of Joab and Abiathar the priest. Nathan the prophet and Bathsheba reminded David of his promise that Solomon would reign. David ordered Solomon anointed at Gihon, and the people shouted, “God save king Solomon.” Adonijah's guests scattered, and he fled to the altar for safety.

Before he died, David charged Solomon, “be thou strong therefore, and shew thyself a man,” and told him to keep the law of Moses. After David's death, Solomon had Adonijah, Joab, and Shimei put to death, and he removed Abiathar from the priesthood, fulfilling God's word against the house of Eli. The kingdom was established in Solomon's hand.

**God Gives Solomon Wisdom (1 Kings 3:1–4:34)** — God grants Solomon extraordinary wisdom after he asks for understanding to govern Israel.

At Gibeon the Lord appeared to Solomon in a dream and invited him to ask for anything. Solomon asked, “Give therefore thy servant an understanding heart to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad.” God was pleased that he had not asked for long life or riches, and gave him wisdom along with riches and honor.

Two women came before Solomon, each claiming the same baby as her own. Solomon called for a sword to divide the child, and the true mother cried out, “give her the living child, and in no wise slay it.” Solomon gave the child to her, and all Israel feared the king because God's wisdom was in him. He spoke three thousand proverbs and a thousand and five songs, and people came from all nations to hear him.

**Solomon Begins Building God's Temple (1 Kings 5:1–6:38)** — Solomon prepares materials and workers for a permanent temple in Jerusalem.

Hiram king of Tyre supplied cedar and fir from Lebanon, and Solomon raised large crews of laborers and stonecutters. In the four hundred and eightieth year after Israel left Egypt, in the fourth year of his reign, Solomon began building the house of the Lord. The stones were prepared at the quarry, so that “there was neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron heard in the house, while it was in building.”

The inside was lined with cedar and overlaid with gold, and two golden cherubim stood in the most holy place. God told Solomon that if he walked in His statutes, He would dwell among Israel and not forsake them. The temple was finished after seven years.

**Solomon Completes the Royal Buildings and Temple Furnishings (1 Kings 7:1–51)** — Solomon builds his palace and completes bronze and gold temple furnishings.

Solomon spent thirteen years building his own palace, along with the house of the forest of Lebanon and a house for Pharaoh's daughter. Hiram, a skilled worker in brass from Tyre, cast the two great pillars named Jachin and Boaz, the molten sea resting on twelve oxen, ten bases, and lavers. Solomon made the golden altar, table, and lampstands, and brought in the silver, gold, and vessels his father David had dedicated.

**God's Glory Fills the Temple (1 Kings 8:1–66)** — Solomon brings the ark into the temple, dedicates it through prayer, and celebrates God's faithfulness.

The priests brought the ark, which held only the two tablets of stone, into the most holy place. A cloud filled the house, so that the priests could not stand to minister, “for the glory of the LORD had filled the house of the LORD.”

Solomon knelt before the altar and prayed, “behold, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee; how much less this house that I have builded?” He asked God to hear prayers made toward the temple, to judge justly, to forgive His people when they repented, to send rain, and to hear even foreigners who prayed there. Israel offered thousands of sacrifices and celebrated for fourteen days.

**God Warns Solomon to Remain Faithful (1 Kings 9:1–28)** — God accepts the temple but warns that disobedience and idolatry will bring judgment.

The Lord appeared to Solomon a second time and said, “I have hallowed this house.” If Solomon walked uprightly as David had, God would establish his throne forever. But if Israel turned to other gods, God would cut them off from the land and the temple would become a heap of ruins. Solomon also built cities, used foreign laborers, and sent ships to Ophir for gold.

**The Queen of Sheba Visits Solomon (1 Kings 10:1–29)** — Solomon's wisdom and wealth draw international attention.

The queen of Sheba came with hard questions, and Solomon answered them all. Seeing his wisdom, his house, and his worship, she said, “the half was not told me,” and praised the Lord for setting Solomon on Israel's throne. Solomon's wealth grew until silver was as common as stones in Jerusalem, and he gathered great numbers of horses and chariots, many brought out of Egypt.

**Solomon Turns Away from God (1 Kings 11:1–43)** — Solomon's foreign wives turn him to idolatry, and God announces the kingdom will be divided.

Solomon loved many foreign women and had seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines. In his old age “his wives turned away his heart after other gods,” and he built high places for Chemosh, Molech, and other gods. God declared that He would tear the kingdom away from Solomon's son, though for David's sake He would leave one tribe.

God raised up adversaries, including Hadad of Edom and Rezon of Damascus. The prophet Ahijah met Jeroboam, tore his new garment into twelve pieces, and told him to take ten, for God would give him ten tribes. Solomon sought to kill Jeroboam, who fled to Egypt. Solomon died after reigning forty years, and Rehoboam succeeded him.

**Israel Divides into Two Kingdoms (1 Kings 12:1–33)** — Rehoboam rejects the people's request for relief, and ten tribes establish Jeroboam as king.

The people asked Rehoboam to lighten the heavy burdens Solomon had placed on them. He rejected the older men's advice and followed the young men, answering, “my father hath chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions.” Israel cried, “To your tents, O Israel,” and the ten northern tribes made Jeroboam king, leaving Rehoboam with Judah and Benjamin. God told Rehoboam through Shemaiah not to fight his brothers.

Fearing that his people would return to Rehoboam if they worshiped in Jerusalem, Jeroboam made two golden calves and set them at Bethel and Dan, saying, “behold thy gods, O Israel.” He appointed priests who were not Levites and established his own feast.

**A Prophet Warns Jeroboam About His Altar (1 Kings 13:1–34)** — A man of God condemns Jeroboam's altar, but his own disobedience leads to his death.

A man of God from Judah cried against the altar at Bethel and foretold that a son of David's house, “Josiah by name,” would burn the bones of its priests upon it. When Jeroboam stretched out his hand to seize him, his hand withered, and the altar split as a sign. At the prophet's prayer, the king's hand was restored.

God had told the man of God not to eat or drink in Bethel, but an old prophet lied to him, claiming an angel had told him otherwise. The man of God ate with him, and on his way home a lion killed him. Even so, Jeroboam did not turn from his false worship, and this became the sin that would cut off his house.

**Judgment Falls Upon Jeroboam and Judah (1 Kings 14:1–31)** — Judgment comes on Jeroboam's house, while Judah falls into idolatry under Rehoboam.

When Jeroboam's son fell sick, he sent his wife in disguise to Ahijah, who was blind from age. God revealed who she was, and Ahijah announced that the child would die and that Jeroboam's house would be swept away. The child died as she entered her house.

In Judah, the people built high places and images and practiced the abominations of the nations. Shishak king of Egypt came against Jerusalem and took the treasures of the temple and palace, including Solomon's golden shields, which Rehoboam replaced with shields of brass. Rehoboam was succeeded by Abijam.

**Judah and Israel Follow Different Paths (1 Kings 15:1–16:34)** — Asa reforms Judah, while Israel sees violence, idolatry, and unstable leadership.

Asa did what was right in the eyes of the Lord, removed idols, and even removed Maachah from being queen mother because she had made an idol. In Israel, Baasha killed Jeroboam's son Nadab and destroyed Jeroboam's house. Baasha's son Elah was murdered by Zimri, who reigned only seven days before burning the palace over himself. Omri then took the throne, bought the hill of Samaria, and built his capital there.

Omri's son Ahab married Jezebel, daughter of the king of the Sidonians, served Baal, and built him a temple in Samaria. Ahab did more to provoke the Lord than all the kings of Israel before him. In his days Hiel rebuilt Jericho at the cost of his firstborn and youngest sons, as Joshua had warned.

**Elijah Announces a Drought and Receives God's Provision (1 Kings 17:1–24)** — God sustains Elijah during the drought he announced and raises a widow's son through his prayer.

Elijah the Tishbite told Ahab, “As the LORD God of Israel liveth, before whom I stand, there shall not be dew nor rain these years, but according to my word.” God hid Elijah by the brook Cherith, where ravens brought him bread and meat each morning and evening.

When the brook dried up, God sent Elijah to a widow at Zarephath, who had only a handful of meal and a little oil. She made him a cake first, and her barrel of meal and cruse of oil did not run out until the rain returned. When her son died, Elijah stretched himself on the child three times and cried to the Lord, and the child revived.

**Elijah Confronts the Prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18:1–46)** — At Mount Carmel, God answers Elijah with fire and ends the drought.

In the third year, God sent Elijah back to Ahab. On Mount Carmel Elijah challenged the people, “How long halt ye between two opinions? if the LORD be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him.” The four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal called on their god from morning until evening, cutting themselves, while Elijah mocked them, but no one answered.

Elijah repaired the altar of the Lord with twelve stones, dug a trench, and had water poured over the sacrifice three times. When he prayed, the fire of the Lord fell and consumed the sacrifice, the wood, the stones, the dust, and the water. The people fell on their faces and cried, “The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God.” The prophets of Baal were killed at the brook Kishon. Elijah prayed on Carmel until his servant saw “a little cloud out of the sea, like a man's hand,” and a great rain followed.

**God Encourages Elijah at Horeb (1 Kings 19:1–21)** — God strengthens a discouraged Elijah and gives him new work to do.

When Jezebel vowed to kill him, Elijah fled into the wilderness, sat under a juniper tree, and prayed, “It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life.” An angel fed him twice, and in the strength of that food he traveled forty days to Horeb, the mount of God.

There a great wind, an earthquake, and a fire passed by, but the Lord was not in them. After the fire came “a still small voice,” asking, “What doest thou here, Elijah?” God told him to anoint Hazael king of Syria, Jehu king of Israel, and Elisha as prophet in his place, and said that seven thousand in Israel remained “which have not bowed unto Baal.” Elijah found Elisha plowing and cast his mantle on him, and Elisha left everything to follow him.

**Ahab Battles the Syrians (1 Kings 20:1–43)** — God gives Ahab victories over Syria, but Ahab spares the king God had condemned.

Benhadad king of Syria besieged Samaria with thirty-two kings and made insulting demands. Ahab answered, “Let not him that girdeth on his harness boast himself as he that putteth it off.” A prophet promised victory, and Israel routed the Syrians. The next year the Syrians claimed that Israel's God was a god of the hills but not of the valleys, and God gave Israel another great victory to show that He is the Lord.

Instead of executing Benhadad, Ahab called him his brother and made a covenant with him. A prophet in disguise told Ahab a parable and declared that because he had let go the man God had appointed to destruction, Ahab's life would go for his.

**Ahab Takes Naboth's Vineyard (1 Kings 21:1–29)** — Jezebel has Naboth killed so Ahab can seize his vineyard, and Elijah announces judgment.

Ahab wanted the vineyard of Naboth beside his palace in Jezreel, but Naboth answered, “The LORD forbid it me, that I should give the inheritance of my fathers unto thee.” Ahab sulked, so Jezebel wrote letters in his name arranging for two worthless men to accuse Naboth falsely of cursing God and the king. Naboth was stoned, and Ahab went to take the vineyard.

Elijah met him there with God's word: “Hast thou killed, and also taken possession?” and, “In the place where dogs licked the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick thy blood.” Ahab said, “Hast thou found me, O mine enemy?” Elijah declared that Ahab's house would be cut off and that dogs would eat Jezebel by the wall of Jezreel. When Ahab tore his clothes and humbled himself, God delayed the disaster until his son's days.

**Ahab Dies in Battle and Ahaziah Succeeds Him (1 Kings 22:1–53)** — Ahab rejects Micaiah's warning and dies at Ramothgilead.

Ahab asked Jehoshaphat king of Judah to join him against Ramothgilead. Four hundred prophets promised victory, but Jehoshaphat asked for a prophet of the Lord. Micaiah, whom Ahab hated, vowed, “what the LORD saith unto me, that will I speak,” and said, “I saw all Israel scattered upon the hills, as sheep that have not a shepherd.” He described a lying spirit sent into the mouths of Ahab's prophets, and Ahab had him imprisoned.

Ahab disguised himself in battle, but “a certain man drew a bow at a venture” and struck him between the joints of his armor. He died that evening, and dogs licked his blood when his chariot was washed in Samaria, as the Lord had said. Jehoshaphat reigned in Judah and did what was right, though the high places remained, while Ahab's son Ahaziah served Baal in Israel.

# 2 Kings
When it happened: From about 850 BC to the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, ending with King Jehoiachin's release in Babylon in the thirty-seventh year of his exile, about 561 BC (2 Kings 25:27).
When it was written: The continuation of 1 Kings, with which it was originally one book. Jewish tradition credits Jeremiah; the finished work dates from the exile, about 560 BC or later.
Where: Samaria, Jerusalem, the Jordan valley, Damascus in Syria, Assyria, and Babylon.
The world at the time: The Assyrian Empire rose to dominate the region and destroyed Samaria and the northern kingdom in 722 BC. A century later Babylon replaced Assyria, and Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Jerusalem and the temple.

**Elijah Confronts King Ahaziah (2 Kings 1:1–18)** — After an injury, Ahaziah seeks a foreign god, and Elijah announces his death.

Ahaziah fell through a lattice in his upper room and sent messengers to ask Baalzebub, the god of Ekron, whether he would recover. Elijah met them and asked, “Is it not because there is not a God in Israel, that ye go to enquire of Baalzebub?” and told them the king would die. The king recognized the description of a hairy man with a leather girdle as Elijah.

Ahaziah sent two captains, each with fifty men, to seize Elijah, and fire from heaven consumed them. A third captain fell on his knees and begged for mercy, and Elijah went with him and repeated the message to the king in person. Ahaziah died, and because he had no son, his brother Jehoram became king.

**Elijah Is Taken to Heaven (2 Kings 2:1–25)** — Elijah is carried into heaven, and Elisha receives his mantle and continues his work.

Elisha refused to leave Elijah as they traveled from Gilgal to Bethel, Jericho, and the Jordan. Elijah struck the water with his mantle, and they crossed on dry ground. When Elijah asked what he could do for him, Elisha said, “let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me.” A chariot and horses of fire separated them, and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven as Elisha cried, “My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof.”

Elisha took up Elijah's mantle, struck the Jordan, and asked, “Where is the LORD God of Elijah?” The waters parted. He healed Jericho's bad water with salt. At Bethel a crowd of young people mocked him, saying, “Go up, thou bald head.” He cursed them in the name of the Lord, and “there came forth two she bears out of the wood, and tare forty and two children of them.”

**God Delivers Israel and Judah from Moab (2 Kings 3:1–27)** — Elisha promises water and victory when three kings march against Moab.

When Mesha king of Moab rebelled, Jehoram of Israel, Jehoshaphat of Judah, and the king of Edom marched against him and ran out of water. Elisha said he would not even look at Jehoram if not for Jehoshaphat. While a minstrel played, he told them to dig ditches, which God filled with water though no wind or rain came.

In the morning sunlight the water looked red, and the Moabites thought the kings had killed one another. They rushed in to take spoil and were defeated. When the battle turned against him, the king of Moab offered his eldest son as a burnt offering on the city wall. Then “there was great indignation against Israel,” and the armies withdrew to their own land.

**Elisha Provides for Those in Need (2 Kings 4:1–44)** — God works through Elisha to provide for a widow, a Shunammite family, and the prophets.

A prophet's widow was about to lose her two sons to a creditor. Elisha told her to borrow empty vessels, and her small pot of oil filled them all, enough to pay her debt and live on the rest. A wealthy woman of Shunem built Elisha a room on her house, and he promised her a son, who was born the following year.

Years later the boy died in the field. His mother laid him on Elisha's bed and rode to the prophet, answering everyone along the way, “It is well.” Elisha came, prayed, and stretched himself on the child, and the boy sneezed seven times and opened his eyes. When the prophets' stew was poisoned and they cried, “O thou man of God, there is death in the pot,” Elisha added meal and made it safe. He also fed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves, as the Lord had said: “They shall eat, and shall leave thereof.”

**Naaman Is Healed of His Leprosy (2 Kings 5:1–27)** — A Syrian commander is healed at Elisha's word, while Elisha's servant is struck for his greed.

Naaman, captain of the Syrian army, was a leper. A young Israelite girl serving his wife told of a prophet in Samaria who could heal him. Elisha sent word, “Go and wash in Jordan seven times.” Naaman was angry that the prophet had not come out to him and asked, “Are not Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel?” His servants persuaded him to obey, and “his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child.”

Naaman returned and declared, “now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel.” Elisha refused his gifts, but Elisha's servant Gehazi ran after Naaman and lied to obtain silver and clothing. Elisha confronted him, and Naaman's leprosy came upon Gehazi, white as snow.

**God Protects Elisha from the Syrians (2 Kings 6:1–23)** — God shows Elisha's servant His heavenly army and turns enemies into guests.

When a borrowed axe head fell into the Jordan, Elisha made the iron float. He repeatedly warned Israel's king where the Syrians planned to attack, so the king of Syria sent an army to surround Elisha at Dothan. Elisha's servant was terrified, but Elisha said, “Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them,” and prayed, “LORD, I pray thee, open his eyes, that he may see.” The servant saw that “the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha.”

God struck the Syrians with blindness, and Elisha led them into Samaria. When the king of Israel asked whether to kill them, Elisha told him to set food before them and send them home. Afterward “the bands of Syria came no more into the land of Israel.”

**God Ends the Famine in Samaria (2 Kings 6:24–7:20)** — During a terrible siege, God ends the famine overnight, as Elisha had promised.

Benhadad besieged Samaria until a donkey's head sold for eighty pieces of silver and some mothers ate their own children. The king blamed Elisha and swore to kill him. Elisha promised that by the next day fine flour and barley would be sold cheaply at the gate. A royal officer scoffed, “if the LORD would make windows in heaven, might this thing be?” Elisha answered, “thou shalt see it with thine eyes, but shalt not eat thereof.”

That night God made the Syrians hear the noise of a great army, and they fled, leaving their camp behind. Four lepers found it empty and said to one another, “this day is a day of good tidings,” and told the city. Food became plentiful, just as Elisha had said, and the doubting officer was trampled to death in the gate.

**Elisha's Influence Reaches Two Kingdoms (2 Kings 8:1–29)** — Elisha's words shape events in Israel, Syria, and Judah.

Elisha had warned the Shunammite woman to leave during a seven-year famine. When she returned to ask for her house and land, Gehazi was telling the king how Elisha had raised her son, and the king restored all her property. In Damascus, Elisha wept as he told Hazael that he would become king of Syria and do great evil to Israel. Hazael asked, “Is thy servant a dog, that he should do this great thing?” The next day he smothered the sick Benhadad with a wet cloth and took the throne.

In Judah, Jehoram married Ahab's daughter and walked in the ways of Ahab's house, and Edom revolted against him. His son Ahaziah also followed Ahab's ways and visited the wounded King Joram of Israel at Jezreel.

**Jehu Destroys the House of Ahab (2 Kings 9:1–10:36)** — Jehu carries out God's judgment on Ahab's house and Baal worship.

Elisha sent a young prophet to anoint Jehu king and charge him to strike down the house of Ahab. Jehu drove to Jezreel so fast that the watchman said, “he driveth furiously.” He shot Joram with an arrow and had his body thrown into Naboth's field, and his men also struck down Ahaziah king of Judah.

Jezebel painted her face and taunted Jehu from a window, “Had Zimri peace, who slew his master?” At Jehu's command servants threw her down, and when men went to bury her, dogs had eaten all but her skull, feet, and hands, as Elijah had foretold. The rulers of Samaria killed Ahab's seventy sons. Jehu gathered all the worshipers of Baal into Baal's temple by a trick, had them killed, and destroyed the temple. God promised that Jehu's sons would sit on Israel's throne to the fourth generation, yet Jehu did not turn from the golden calves of Jeroboam, and Hazael began to cut away Israel's territory.

**Joash Becomes King and Repairs the Temple (2 Kings 11:1–12:21)** — A hidden prince is crowned, and covenant worship is restored in Judah.

When Ahaziah died, his mother Athaliah killed the royal heirs and seized the throne. Ahaziah's sister Jehosheba hid his infant son Joash, who was kept hidden in the temple six years. In the seventh year Jehoiada the priest crowned the boy, and the people clapped and said, “God save the king.” Athaliah tore her clothes and cried, “Treason, Treason,” and was put to death. The people made a covenant with the Lord and tore down Baal's temple.

Joash did right “all his days wherein Jehoiada the priest instructed him.” He had a chest with a hole in its lid set beside the altar to collect money for repairing the temple. When Hazael threatened Jerusalem, Joash sent him the temple's sacred treasures. Joash was later murdered by his own servants.

**Elisha Dies as Israel Faces Continued Conflict (2 Kings 13:1–25)** — Even at his death, Elisha points Israel to God's deliverance.

When Syria oppressed Israel under Jehoahaz, he sought the Lord, and God gave Israel a deliverer. As Elisha lay dying, King Jehoash wept over him, “O my father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof.” Elisha had him shoot an arrow east, calling it “The arrow of the LORD'S deliverance,” then strike the ground with arrows. Jehoash struck only three times, and Elisha was angry, saying five or six strikes would have destroyed Syria completely.

After Elisha was buried, a dead man thrown into his tomb touched his bones and revived. Jehoash defeated Syria three times and recovered Israel's cities, as Elisha had said.

**Judah and Israel Experience Changing Fortunes (2 Kings 14:1–29)** — Amaziah's pride brings defeat, while Jeroboam II restores Israel's borders.

Amaziah of Judah executed the servants who had murdered his father but spared their children, obeying the law of Moses that “The fathers shall not be put to death for the children.” After defeating Edom, he challenged Jehoash of Israel to battle. Jehoash warned him with a parable of a thistle that challenged a cedar, but Amaziah would not listen. Judah was defeated, Jerusalem's wall was broken down, and the temple and palace treasures were taken. Amaziah was later killed at Lachish.

In Israel, Jeroboam II continued in the sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat, yet God used him to restore Israel's borders, “by the hand of his servant Jonah, the son of Amittai,” because He saw Israel's bitter affliction.

**Israel's Kings Lead the Nation Toward Destruction (2 Kings 15:1–38)** — Judah has steadier kings while Israel falls into a cycle of assassination.

Azariah, also called Uzziah, reigned long in Judah and did what was right, but the Lord struck him with leprosy, and he lived in a separate house until his death while his son Jotham governed. In Israel, Jehu's great-great-grandson Zachariah reigned six months before he was murdered, fulfilling God's word that Jehu's line would last four generations.

Shallum reigned one month before Menahem killed him. Menahem paid a heavy tribute to Pul king of Assyria. His son Pekahiah was murdered by Pekah, and during Pekah's reign Tiglathpileser of Assyria captured Gilead, Galilee, and Naphtali and carried their people away. Hoshea then killed Pekah and became king.

**King Ahaz Turns Judah Toward False Worship (2 Kings 16:1–20)** — Ahaz turns to idols and to Assyria instead of to God.

Ahaz walked in the ways of Israel's kings and even made his son pass through the fire. When Syria and Israel attacked Jerusalem, he sent the temple's silver and gold to Tiglathpileser with the message, “I am thy servant and thy son.” Assyria captured Damascus, and when Ahaz went there, he had a copy of a Damascus altar built for the temple and moved the bronze altar aside. He was succeeded by Hezekiah.

**Assyria Conquers the Northern Kingdom of Israel (2 Kings 17:1–41)** — Israel is carried into exile because of persistent idolatry and rejection of God's prophets.

Hoshea sought help from Egypt and stopped paying tribute, so Shalmaneser of Assyria imprisoned him and besieged Samaria for three years. In Hoshea's ninth year Samaria fell, and Israel was carried away to Assyria and settled in distant cities.

The account explains why: Israel had worshiped other gods, followed the customs of the nations, made idols and calves, and sacrificed their children, though God had warned them through every prophet and seer. They “would not hear, but hardened their necks.” The king of Assyria resettled Samaria with foreign peoples, who learned something of the Lord yet kept their own gods. “They feared the LORD, and served their own gods.”

**Hezekiah Restores Worship and Trusts God (2 Kings 18:1–19:37)** — Hezekiah trusts God when Assyria threatens Jerusalem, and God delivers the city.

Hezekiah removed the high places and broke the bronze serpent Moses had made, because Israel had been burning incense to it. “He trusted in the LORD God of Israel; so that after him was none like him among all the kings of Judah.” When Sennacherib king of Assyria captured Judah's fortified cities, Hezekiah first paid him a heavy tribute, even stripping the gold from the temple doors.

Sennacherib still sent his commander, the Rabshakeh, to mock Hezekiah's trust in God and urge Jerusalem to surrender. Hezekiah tore his clothes, sent to the prophet Isaiah, and spread Sennacherib's threatening letter before the Lord in prayer. Isaiah answered that the king of Assyria “shall not come into this city, nor shoot an arrow there.” That night the angel of the Lord struck 185,000 in the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib returned to Nineveh, where his own sons killed him as he worshiped his god.

**God Extends Hezekiah's Life (2 Kings 20:1–21)** — God answers Hezekiah's prayer for healing, but his pride toward Babylon brings a warning.

Isaiah told the sick Hezekiah, “Set thine house in order; for thou shalt die, and not live.” Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and wept, and God sent Isaiah back: “I have heard thy prayer, I have seen thy tears.” God added fifteen years to his life and gave a sign by moving the shadow ten degrees backward on the dial of Ahaz.

When envoys came from Babylon, Hezekiah showed them all his treasures. Isaiah foretold that everything in his house would one day be carried to Babylon and some of his descendants would serve there. Hezekiah answered, “Is it not good, if peace and truth be in my days?” He also built the pool and conduit that brought water into Jerusalem.

**Manasseh Leads Judah into Great Wickedness (2 Kings 21:1–26)** — Manasseh reverses Hezekiah's reforms and fills Jerusalem with idolatry and bloodshed.

Manasseh became king at twelve and reigned fifty-five years. He rebuilt the high places, made altars for Baal, placed altars to the stars in the temple courts, made his son pass through the fire, and consulted spirits. He shed so much innocent blood that he filled Jerusalem from one end to the other. God declared, “I will wipe Jerusalem as a man wipeth a dish.” His son Amon continued in evil and was murdered by his servants, and the people made Amon's son Josiah king.

**Josiah Rediscovers God's Law and Begins Reform (2 Kings 22:1–23:30)** — Josiah responds to the rediscovered law with humility, covenant renewal, and sweeping reform.

Josiah became king at eight. During temple repairs, the high priest Hilkiah said, “I have found the book of the law in the house of the LORD.” When it was read to Josiah, he tore his clothes. The prophetess Huldah declared that judgment would still come on Judah, but because Josiah's heart was tender and he humbled himself, he would be gathered to his grave in peace before it came.

Josiah read the book to all the people, and they made a covenant to keep it. He removed Baal's vessels from the temple, destroyed the high places, and defiled Topheth so no one would sacrifice children there. At Bethel he burned bones on Jeroboam's altar, fulfilling the word of the man of God from Judah. He kept a Passover unlike any since the days of the judges. “Like unto him was there no king before him, that turned to the LORD with all his heart,” yet God did not turn from His anger over Manasseh's sins. Josiah was killed by Pharaohnechoh at Megiddo.

**Judah's Final Kings Lead the Nation Toward Exile (2 Kings 23:31–24:20)** — Judah's last kings fall under Egypt and then Babylon.

Pharaohnechoh removed Josiah's son Jehoahaz after three months and made Eliakim king, renaming him Jehoiakim. Jehoiakim served Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon three years and then rebelled. His son Jehoiachin reigned three months before Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem and carried him, the princes, the soldiers, the craftsmen, and the temple treasures to Babylon, so that “none remained, save the poorest sort of the people of the land.” Nebuchadnezzar made Mattaniah king and renamed him Zedekiah, but Zedekiah also rebelled.

**Jerusalem Falls and the Temple Is Destroyed (2 Kings 25:1–21)** — Babylon destroys Jerusalem and the temple and carries Judah into captivity.

Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem until famine gripped the city. When the wall was broken, Zedekiah fled but was captured near Jericho. His sons were killed before his eyes, his eyes were put out, and he was taken to Babylon in chains. Nebuzaradan burned the temple, the palace, and every great house, broke down the walls, and carried away the bronze pillars and vessels. The chief priests and leaders were executed at Riblah. “So Judah was carried away out of their land.”

**The Survivors Scatter and Jehoiachin Receives Mercy (2 Kings 25:22–30)** — Gedaliah is murdered, but the book closes with mercy for David's line in Babylon.

Nebuchadnezzar appointed Gedaliah governor over the poor who remained, but Ishmael, a member of the royal family, murdered him, and the survivors fled to Egypt in fear of the Babylonians. In the thirty-seventh year of Jehoiachin's captivity, Evilmerodach king of Babylon released him from prison, spoke kindly to him, set his seat above the other captive kings, and gave him a place at the king's table and a daily allowance for the rest of his life.

# 1 Chronicles
When it happened: Through genealogies it reaches from Adam to the return from exile, but its story covers the death of Saul and the reign of David, ending about 970 BC.
When it was written: 1 and 2 Chronicles were originally one book, and its writer is often called the Chronicler. The genealogies run past the exile (1 Chronicles 3:17–24; 9:1–3), so it was written after the return—likely in the 400s BC.
Where: Centered on Jerusalem, with Hebron and the tribal lands of Israel.
The world at the time: When Chronicles was written, Judah was a small province of the Persian Empire, with a rebuilt temple but no king. Retelling David's story reminded the community of God's promises to David's house and the importance of the temple.

**Israel's Family History Begins with Adam (1 Chronicles 1:1–2:55)** — Israel's ancestry is traced from Adam through Noah, Abraham, Jacob, Judah, Jesse, and David.

The genealogies begin with Adam, Seth, and Enosh and pass through Noah's sons to Abraham. They trace the families of Ishmael, Esau, and Edom's kings before turning to the twelve sons of Israel. Judah's line receives special attention, including Achar, “the troubler of Israel,” who took what was devoted to God at Jericho. The record follows Judah's line through Boaz and Jesse to David, preserving the family that God would make royal.

**The Descendants of David and Israel's Tribes (1 Chronicles 3:1–5:26)** — The records follow David's royal descendants and the tribes of Judah, Simeon, and the east.

David's sons born in Hebron and Jerusalem are listed, followed by the kings of Judah from Solomon through Josiah, and descendants after the exile, including Zerubbabel. Among Judah's families, Jabez, who was more honorable than his brothers, prayed, “Oh that thou wouldest bless me indeed, and enlarge my coast,” and asked God to keep him from evil. “And God granted him that which he requested.”

Reuben, the firstborn, lost his birthright to Joseph's sons because he defiled his father's bed, while “Judah prevailed above his brethren, and of him came the chief ruler.” The eastern tribes cried to God in battle, and “he was intreated of them; because they put their trust in him.” Later they turned to other gods, and the Assyrians carried them into captivity.

**The Levites and Their Sacred Responsibilities (1 Chronicles 6:1–81)** — Levi's descendants are traced through priestly families and worship leaders.

The record traces the high priests from Aaron to Jehozadak, who went into captivity when the Lord carried Judah away by Nebuchadnezzar. It names the families of Gershom, Kohath, and Merari, and the singers Heman, Asaph, and Ethan, whom David set over the music in the house of the Lord after the ark came to rest. It closes with the cities and pasturelands given to the Levites among the tribes.

**The Remaining Tribes and Israel's Returned Families (1 Chronicles 7:1–9:34)** — Genealogies preserve the other tribes and the community that returned to Jerusalem after captivity.

The genealogies cover Issachar, Benjamin, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, including the line that led to Joshua, and Asher, and give Saul's family in detail. The record explains that Judah was carried away to Babylon for its transgression. It then lists the first families to return and live in Jerusalem: priests, Levites, gatekeepers who kept watch over the house of God, and those who cared for its vessels, its bread, and its music.

**Saul's Reign Ends in Defeat (1 Chronicles 9:35–10:14)** — Saul and his sons die at Mount Gilboa, and the kingdom passes to David.

The Philistines defeated Israel at Mount Gilboa, killing Jonathan and Saul's other sons. Saul was wounded and fell on his own sword, and the Philistines hung his head in the temple of Dagon. The Chronicler explains the meaning of his death: “So Saul died for his transgression which he committed against the LORD,” because he did not keep God's word and sought a medium rather than the Lord. Therefore God turned the kingdom to David.

**David Becomes King Over Israel (1 Chronicles 11:1–12:40)** — All Israel anoints David king at Hebron, and mighty men from every tribe support him.

All Israel came to Hebron and anointed David king, according to the word of the Lord by Samuel. When David marched on Jebus, he announced, “Whosoever smiteth the Jebusites first shall be chief and captain.” Joab went up first and became commander, and Jerusalem became the city of David.

The account honors David's mighty men, including the three who broke through the Philistine lines to bring him water from Bethlehem's well, which he poured out to the Lord. Warriors from every tribe came to make him king, including men of Issachar “that had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do.” There was great joy in Israel.

**David Attempts to Bring the Ark to Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 13:1–14:17)** — David's first attempt to move the ark ends in Uzza's death.

David and all Israel brought the ark from Kirjathjearim on a new cart, celebrating with music. When the oxen stumbled, Uzza put out his hand to steady the ark, and God struck him. David was afraid and asked, “How shall I bring the ark of God home to me?” He left it with Obededom, and God blessed his household for three months. Meanwhile, Hiram king of Tyre sent builders for David's house, and David twice defeated the Philistines after asking God's direction.

**David Brings the Ark into Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 15:1–16:43)** — David moves the ark the right way, and Israel worships with music and thanksgiving.

David declared, “None ought to carry the ark of God but the Levites,” and explained that God had broken out against them because they had not sought Him in the proper way. The Levites carried the ark on their shoulders with poles, as Moses had commanded, while singers and musicians played. David danced, and Michal despised him.

David appointed Asaph and the Levites to give thanks continually, and they sang, “O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good; for his mercy endureth for ever.” Zadok and the priests continued to serve at the tabernacle in Gibeon.

**God Promises David an Enduring Kingdom (1 Chronicles 17:1–27)** — God promises to build David a lasting house instead of letting David build Him one.

David wished to build a house for the ark, but God told Nathan that David would not build Him a house; instead God would build David a house. David's son would build the temple, and God promised to settle him in His house and kingdom forever, with a throne established for evermore. David sat before the Lord and prayed, “Who am I, O LORD God, and what is mine house, that thou hast brought me hitherto?”

**David Defeats Israel's Enemies (1 Chronicles 18:1–20:8)** — God gives David victory over the surrounding nations.

The Lord preserved David wherever he went, giving him victories over the Philistines, Moab, Zobah, Syria, Edom, and Ammon. David dedicated the silver, gold, and bronze he captured, which Solomon later used for the temple, and he executed judgment and justice among his people. After the Ammonite king Hanun humiliated David's messengers, Joab defeated Ammon and its Syrian allies and took Rabbah. Israel also killed Philistine giants, including the brother of Goliath.

**David's Census Brings Judgment Upon Israel (1 Chronicles 21:1–30)** — David's census brings judgment, but the place where the plague stops becomes the site of the temple.

“And Satan stood up against Israel, and provoked David to number Israel,” where 2 Samuel 24 says the Lord's anger moved David. Joab objected, but David insisted, and then confessed that he had sinned greatly. Given a choice of three judgments, David chose to fall into the Lord's hands, and a plague killed seventy thousand.

David saw the angel of the Lord standing between earth and heaven with a drawn sword over Jerusalem. He bought the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite for six hundred shekels of gold, built an altar, and God answered with fire from heaven. David declared that this would be the place of the house of the Lord.

**David Prepares Solomon to Build the Temple (1 Chronicles 22:1–19)** — David gathers materials and charges Solomon to build the temple.

David prepared iron, bronze, cedar, and stone in abundance because Solomon was young. He told Solomon that God had said, “thou hast shed blood abundantly, and hast made great wars,” so David would not build the temple. Solomon, whose name means peace, would build it in a time of rest. David charged him, “Be strong, and of good courage; dread not, nor be dismayed,” and commanded Israel's leaders to help him.

**David Organizes the Levites and Priests (1 Chronicles 23:1–24:31)** — David makes Solomon king and organizes the Levites and priests for temple service.

When David was old and full of days, he made Solomon king. He counted thirty-eight thousand Levites from thirty years old and up and assigned them to oversee the temple's work, serve as officers and judges, guard the gates, and praise the Lord with instruments. Since the tabernacle would no longer need to be carried, their duties shifted to the temple's courts and service. The descendants of Aaron's sons Eleazar and Ithamar were divided by lot into twenty-four priestly courses.

**David Establishes Musicians, Gatekeepers, and Officials (1 Chronicles 25:1–27:34)** — David organizes musicians, gatekeepers, treasurers, and officials for the kingdom and temple.

The sons of Asaph, Heman, and Jeduthun were set apart to prophesy with harps, psalteries, and cymbals, two hundred eighty-eight skilled musicians in twenty-four courses. Gatekeepers were assigned to each gate by lot, and Levites oversaw the treasuries of the temple and of dedicated gifts. Twelve army divisions of twenty-four thousand served one month each, and the account lists tribal leaders, royal stewards, and counselors such as Ahithophel and Hushai.

**David Gives Solomon the Plans for the Temple (1 Chronicles 28:1–21)** — David publicly charges Solomon and gives him the temple plans.

David gathered Israel's leaders and explained that God had chosen Solomon to sit on the throne and build the temple. He charged Solomon to serve God with a whole heart and a willing mind, for “if thou seek him, he will be found of thee; but if thou forsake him, he will cast thee off for ever.” David gave him the pattern of the temple, which he said the Lord had made him understand in writing, and told him to be strong and courageous, because God would not fail him until the work was done.

**David Dedicates the Kingdom's Gifts to God (1 Chronicles 29:1–30)** — David and Israel give generously for the temple, and David's reign comes to an end.

David gave his own treasure of gold and silver for the temple, and the leaders and people offered willingly and rejoiced. David blessed the Lord before the assembly: “Thine, O LORD, is the greatness, and the power, and the glory, and the victory, and the majesty,” and confessed, “all things come of thee, and of thine own have we given thee.”

Solomon was anointed king a second time and Zadok as priest, and all Israel obeyed Solomon. David had reigned forty years, seven in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem, “and he died in a good old age, full of days, riches, and honour.”

# 2 Chronicles
When it happened: From the beginning of Solomon's reign (about 970 BC) to the destruction of Jerusalem (586 BC), closing with the decree of Cyrus in 538 BC.
When it was written: The continuation of 1 Chronicles, written after the return from exile, likely in the 400s BC.
Where: Jerusalem and the kingdom of Judah.
The world at the time: Chronicles follows only the kings of Judah, while Egypt, Assyria, and then Babylon threatened from outside. Written in the Persian period, it ends with a Persian king inviting the exiles home.

**God Gives Solomon Wisdom and Wealth (2 Chronicles 1:1–17)** — Solomon seeks wisdom at Gibeon to govern Israel, and God gives him wisdom, riches, and honor.

Solomon went with all Israel to the tabernacle at Gibeon and offered a thousand burnt offerings on the bronze altar. That night God appeared to him, and Solomon asked, “Give me now wisdom and knowledge, that I may go out and come in before this people.” Because he had not asked for riches or the lives of his enemies, God gave him wisdom and also riches and honor beyond any king before or after him. Silver and gold became as common as stones in Jerusalem.

**Solomon Builds the Temple in Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 2:1–5:1)** — Solomon gathers workers and materials and builds the temple on Mount Moriah.

Solomon asked Huram king of Tyre for cedar and a skilled craftsman, and Huram sent both, praising the Lord for giving David a wise son. Solomon began building in the fourth year of his reign on Mount Moriah, where the Lord had appeared to David, at the threshing floor of Ornan. The most holy place was overlaid with fine gold, and two cherubim spread their wings across it. Huram's craftsman made the pillars Jachin and Boaz, the molten sea, ten lavers, ten lampstands, and ten tables. Solomon then brought in the treasures David had dedicated.

**God's Glory Fills the Completed Temple (2 Chronicles 5:2–7:22)** — God's glory fills the temple, and God promises to hear His people when they turn to Him.

When the priests set the ark in its place, the singers and a hundred twenty trumpeters praised the Lord together, “For he is good; for his mercy endureth for ever,” and the house was filled with a cloud of glory so that the priests could not minister. Solomon stood on a bronze platform, knelt, and prayed for God to hear His people's prayers toward the temple, to forgive them when they repented, and to hear even foreigners.

When he finished praying, fire came down from heaven and consumed the sacrifices, and the people bowed and worshiped. Later the Lord appeared to Solomon and promised, “If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land.” He warned that if they turned to other gods, He would uproot them and cast off the temple.

**Solomon's Kingdom Reaches Great Prosperity (2 Chronicles 8:1–9:31)** — Solomon builds cities, orders worship, and receives the queen of Sheba.

Solomon built store cities and fortified towns, moved Pharaoh's daughter out of the city of David because the places where the ark had been were holy, and arranged the priests and Levites as David had ordered. The queen of Sheba tested him with hard questions and said, “the one half of the greatness of thy wisdom was not told me.” Gold came to Solomon at six hundred sixty-six talents a year, and kings of the earth sought his presence. He reigned forty years.

**Israel Divides Under Rehoboam (2 Chronicles 10:1–12:16)** — Rehoboam's harsh answer splits the kingdom, and Judah is humbled when it abandons God.

Rehoboam rejected the people's plea for relief, saying, “my father chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions,” and the northern tribes broke away. He obeyed God's command through Shemaiah not to fight them. Priests and Levites from the north, rejected by Jeroboam, came to Judah, along with others who sought the Lord, and strengthened Rehoboam's kingdom for three years.

Once his kingdom was strong, Rehoboam and Judah forsook the law of the Lord. Shishak of Egypt invaded, and Shemaiah declared that because they had forsaken God, God had left them to Shishak. The princes and king humbled themselves, saying, “The LORD is righteous,” so God did not destroy them completely, but Shishak carried off the treasures of the temple and palace.

**Abijah Defeats Israel and Asa Seeks God (2 Chronicles 13:1–16:14)** — Judah prospers when it relies on God and suffers when it trusts in human help.

Abijah stood on Mount Zemaraim and warned Jeroboam's army, “fight ye not against the LORD God of your fathers.” When Judah found itself surrounded, the people cried to the Lord, the priests blew trumpets, and God struck Jeroboam's army.

Asa removed foreign altars and commanded Judah to seek the Lord. When Zerah the Ethiopian came against him with a huge army, Asa prayed, “LORD, it is nothing with thee to help, whether with many, or with them that have no power,” and God defeated the Ethiopians. The prophet Azariah told him, “The LORD is with you, while ye be with him; and if ye seek him, he will be found of you,” and Judah made a covenant to seek God.

Later, when Baasha of Israel threatened him, Asa sent temple treasures to buy help from Syria. The seer Hanani rebuked him: “the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him.” Asa angrily imprisoned Hanani. When his feet became severely diseased, “he sought not to the LORD, but to the physicians.” He died in the forty-first year of his reign.

**Jehoshaphat Strengthens Judah and Receives Deliverance (2 Chronicles 17:1–20:37)** — Jehoshaphat teaches God's law and leads Judah to trust God in a crisis.

Jehoshaphat sent princes, Levites, and priests through Judah's cities with the book of the law to teach the people. He joined Ahab in battle at Ramothgilead and barely escaped, and the seer Jehu rebuked him: “Shouldest thou help the ungodly, and love them that hate the LORD?” He appointed judges, warning them, “ye judge not for man, but for the LORD.”

When Moab, Ammon, and Mount Seir invaded, Jehoshaphat proclaimed a fast and prayed, “neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee.” The Spirit came on the Levite Jahaziel, who declared, “the battle is not yours, but God's,” and told them to “stand ye still, and see the salvation of the LORD with you.” Jehoshaphat sent singers ahead of the army, and as they praised God, the invaders turned and destroyed one another. Judah spent three days gathering the spoil and blessed the Lord in the valley of Berachah.

**Judah Suffers Under Jehoram and Ahaziah (2 Chronicles 21:1–22:12)** — Jehoram follows Ahab's house, bringing judgment on Judah and his family.

Jehoram killed all his brothers, married Ahab's daughter, and led Judah into idolatry. A letter came to him from Elijah the prophet announcing judgment for his sins. Edom and Libnah revolted, Philistines and Arabians carried off his household and all his sons except the youngest, and he died of a painful disease of the bowels. He “departed without being desired,” and was not buried in the tombs of the kings.

His son Ahaziah followed the counsel of his mother Athaliah and Ahab's family and was killed during Jehu's purge of Ahab's house. Athaliah then destroyed the royal family and seized power, but Ahaziah's sister Jehoshabeath, the wife of Jehoiada the priest, hid the infant Joash in the temple for six years.

**Joash Restores the Temple but Later Turns Away (2 Chronicles 23:1–24:27)** — Joash serves God while Jehoiada lives but turns away after his death.

Jehoiada crowned Joash, Athaliah was killed, and the people destroyed Baal's temple. Joash repaired the temple, collecting the tax Moses had commanded in a chest at the gate. Jehoiada died at one hundred thirty and was buried among the kings because he had done good in Israel.

After Jehoiada's death, Judah's princes persuaded Joash to serve idols. God sent prophets, and the Spirit came upon Jehoiada's son Zechariah, who said, “because ye have forsaken the LORD, he hath also forsaken you.” At the king's command, he was stoned in the court of the temple, and as he died he said, “The LORD look upon it, and require it.” A small Syrian army defeated Judah's great army, and Joash's servants murdered him in his bed for the blood of Jehoiada's son.

**Amaziah and Uzziah Experience Victory and Pride (2 Chronicles 25:1–26:23)** — Two kings prosper while they follow God and fall when pride and idolatry take hold.

Amaziah hired a hundred thousand soldiers from Israel, but a man of God told him to send them home, and Amaziah obeyed. After defeating Edom, he brought back Edom's gods and worshiped them, and he refused a prophet's rebuke. He challenged Israel to battle, was defeated, and later was killed by conspirators at Lachish.

Uzziah sought God in the days of Zechariah, who had understanding in the visions of God, and “as long as he sought the LORD, God made him to prosper.” He won wars, built towers, and loved farming, and his fame spread far. “But when he was strong, his heart was lifted up to his destruction.” He entered the temple to burn incense, which only priests could do. When Azariah and eighty priests withstood him, Uzziah raged at them, and leprosy broke out on his forehead beside the incense altar. He remained a leper until his death, living in a separate house, while his son Jotham governed.

**Jotham Remains Faithful While Ahaz Leads Judah Astray (2 Chronicles 27:1–28:27)** — Jotham grows strong through obedience, while Ahaz leads Judah into idolatry.

Jotham “became mighty, because he prepared his ways before the LORD his God,” though the people still acted corruptly. His son Ahaz made images for Baal and burned his children in the fire in the valley of Hinnom. God gave Judah into the hands of Syria and Israel, which killed a hundred twenty thousand in one day and carried off two hundred thousand captives.

The prophet Oded met Israel's army at Samaria and warned them not to enslave their brothers. Leaders of Ephraim clothed and fed the captives, put the weak on donkeys, and brought them home to Jericho. Ahaz sought help from Assyria, which only distressed him, and “in the time of his distress did he trespass yet more against the LORD: this is that king Ahaz.” He sacrificed to the gods of Damascus, cut apart the temple vessels, and shut the doors of the house of the Lord.

**Hezekiah Reopens the Temple and Restores Worship (2 Chronicles 29:1–36)** — Hezekiah reopens and cleanses the temple and restores its worship.

In the first month of his first year, Hezekiah opened the temple doors and repaired them. He told the priests and Levites that their fathers' unfaithfulness had brought God's wrath and called them to sanctify themselves. The Levites cleansed the temple over sixteen days. Hezekiah offered sin offerings for all Israel, and the Levites praised God with the instruments and songs of David and Asaph. Hezekiah and the people rejoiced, “for the thing was done suddenly.”

**Hezekiah Invites Israel to Celebrate Passover (2 Chronicles 30:1–31:21)** — Hezekiah calls all Israel to a great Passover in Jerusalem.

Hezekiah sent letters from Beersheba to Dan, inviting the northern tribes to return to the Lord and keep the Passover in Jerusalem. Many laughed the messengers to scorn, but some humbled themselves and came. Because many had not purified themselves, Hezekiah prayed, “The good LORD pardon every one” who sought God with his heart, and the Lord heard and healed the people. There had been no such joy in Jerusalem since Solomon's day.

Afterward the people destroyed idols and high places throughout the land and brought tithes in heaps to support the priests and Levites. In every work Hezekiah began, he sought God with all his heart and prospered.

**God Delivers Jerusalem from Assyria (2 Chronicles 32:1–23)** — Hezekiah encourages Jerusalem to trust God, and God defeats Assyria.

When Sennacherib invaded, Hezekiah stopped up the springs outside the city, rebuilt the wall, and encouraged the people: “With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God to help us, and to fight our battles.” Sennacherib's servants mocked the God of Israel as no better than the gods of other nations. Hezekiah and the prophet Isaiah prayed and cried to heaven, and the Lord sent an angel who cut off the mighty warriors and leaders in the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib returned home in shame, and his own sons killed him in the house of his god.

**Hezekiah's Pride and Final Years (2 Chronicles 32:24–33)** — God heals Hezekiah, tests his heart, and grants him an honored burial.

Hezekiah became deathly ill, prayed, and received a sign from the Lord, but he did not respond with gratitude, “for his heart was lifted up.” When he humbled himself, the wrath did not come in his days. He grew very wealthy and redirected the upper waters of Gihon to the west side of the city of David. When envoys from Babylon came, “God left him, to try him, that he might know all that was in his heart.” He was buried with honor in the highest of the tombs of David's sons.

**Manasseh Repents but Amon Rejects God (2 Chronicles 33:1–25)** — Manasseh's long rebellion ends in repentance, while his son refuses to humble himself.

Manasseh rebuilt the high places, worshiped the stars, set an idol in the temple, burned his children in the fire, and practiced sorcery, leading Judah to do worse than the nations. God spoke to him, but he would not listen, so Assyrian commanders captured him with hooks, bound him in chains, and carried him to Babylon.

In his distress Manasseh humbled himself greatly and prayed, and God heard him and brought him back to Jerusalem. “Then Manasseh knew that the LORD he was God.” He removed the foreign gods and idol, restored the altar of the Lord, and commanded Judah to serve Him. His son Amon did not humble himself as his father had, but sinned more and more, and his servants murdered him.

**Josiah Restores the Temple and Rediscovers God's Law (2 Chronicles 34:1–33)** — Josiah seeks God from his youth, purges idolatry, and renews the covenant.

In the eighth year of his reign, “while he was yet young, he began to seek after the God of David.” In his twelfth year he began purging Judah and Jerusalem of high places and idols, and even went into the former northern territories. In his eighteenth year, while the temple was being repaired, Hilkiah found the book of the law of the Lord given by Moses. When it was read, Josiah tore his clothes. The prophetess Huldah warned that judgment would come, but not in Josiah's days because he had humbled himself. Josiah made a covenant before the Lord, and all his days the people did not turn from following God.

**Josiah Celebrates Passover and Dies in Battle (2 Chronicles 35:1–27)** — Josiah keeps a great Passover, then dies after ignoring a warning from God through Necho.

Josiah kept a Passover with the priests and Levites in their courses, and there had been none like it since the days of Samuel the prophet. Later, when Pharaohnecho marched to fight at Carchemish, Josiah went out against him. Necho sent word, “forbear thee from meddling with God, who is with me, that he destroy thee not,” but Josiah disguised himself and fought, not listening to the words of God through Necho. Archers wounded him at Megiddo, and his servants carried him in another chariot to Jerusalem, where he died. All Judah mourned, and Jeremiah lamented for him.

**Judah's Final Kings Bring the Nation to Ruin (2 Chronicles 36:1–21)** — Judah's last kings reject God's messengers until there is no remedy.

Egypt removed Jehoahaz, and Nebuchadnezzar later carried Jehoiakim and then Jehoiachin to Babylon. Zedekiah did not humble himself before Jeremiah the prophet and rebelled against Nebuchadnezzar, who had made him swear by God. The priests and people multiplied idolatry and polluted the temple. God sent His messengers again and again because He had compassion on His people, but “they mocked the messengers of God, and despised his words, and misused his prophets, until the wrath of the LORD arose against his people, till there was no remedy.”

The Babylonians killed young and old, even in the sanctuary, burned the temple, broke down Jerusalem's walls, and carried the survivors to Babylon. This fulfilled the word of the Lord through Jeremiah, “until the land had enjoyed her sabbaths,” seventy years.

**Cyrus Permits God's People to Return (2 Chronicles 36:22–23)** — God moves Cyrus of Persia to let His people return and rebuild the temple.

In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, the Lord stirred up his spirit to fulfill the word spoken through Jeremiah. Cyrus proclaimed that the God of heaven had charged him to build Him a house in Jerusalem, and invited the exiles: “Who is there among you of all his people? The LORD his God be with him, and let him go up.”

# Ezra
When it happened: From the first year of Cyrus king of Persia (538 BC) to Ezra's arrival in Jerusalem in the seventh year of Artaxerxes (about 458 BC). The temple was finished in 515 BC, and about fifty-seven years pass between chapters 6 and 7.
When it was written: Chapters 7–9 are partly written in Ezra's own voice. Some official letters are preserved in Aramaic, the language of the Persian government (Ezra 4:8–6:18; 7:12–26). Ezra and Nehemiah were originally one book.
Where: Babylon in the Persian Empire, and Jerusalem and the towns of Judah.
The world at the time: Persia had conquered Babylon and allowed exiled peoples to return home. Judah was a small, poor province surrounded by neighbors who opposed the rebuilding.

**Cyrus Allows God's People to Return Home (Ezra 1:1–2:70)** — After captivity, God moved Cyrus of Persia to permit the people to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the temple. Cyrus returned temple vessels to Sheshbazzar. Zerubbabel, Jeshua, priests, Levites, and more than forty-two thousand people returned; family records determined eligibility for priestly service.

**The Altar Is Restored and the Temple Foundation Laid (Ezra 3:1–13)** — Jeshua and Zerubbabel rebuilt the altar and restored sacrifices and the Feast of Tabernacles. When the temple foundation was laid, praise, joy, and the tears of those remembering Solomon's temple mingled together.

**Opposition Brings the Work to a Halt (Ezra 4:1–24)** — Neighbors opposed the returned exiles, discouraged workers, and appealed to Persian rulers. A later order stopped Jerusalem's building, and the temple work remained halted until Darius's reign.

**God's Prophets Encourage the Rebuilding (Ezra 5:1–6:12)** — Haggai and Zechariah encouraged Zerubbabel and Jeshua to rebuild. Darius's search found Cyrus's decree; Darius ordered officials not to interfere and to provide support for sacrifices.

**The Temple Is Completed and Worship Restored (Ezra 6:13–22)** — The temple was completed in Darius's sixth year. The returned people dedicated it, organized priests and Levites, kept Passover and Unleavened Bread, and rejoiced that worship had been restored.

**Ezra Is Sent to Teach God's Law (Ezra 7:1–28)** — Ezra, a priest and skilled scribe descended from Aaron, received Artaxerxes's decree to lead another return, carry gifts, appoint judges, and teach God's law.

**Ezra Leads Another Group Home (Ezra 8:1–36)** — Ezra gathered families and Levites, proclaimed a fast at Ahava, and entrusted temple gifts to selected priests. God protected the travelers, who delivered the offerings and the king's orders in Jerusalem.

**Ezra Confronts Israel's Unfaithfulness (Ezra 9:1–15)** — Ezra learned that Israelites, priests, and Levites had intermarried with surrounding peoples contrary to the community's covenant obligations. He mourned, confessed Israel's sin, and acknowledged God's mercy and their guilt.

**The People Respond to Ezra's Prayer (Ezra 10:1–44)** — The people acknowledged wrongdoing and made a covenant to address the marriages. Leaders and judges examined the cases over time; Ezra ends with the names of those involved and the renewed seriousness of covenant obedience.

# Nehemiah
When it happened: From the twentieth year of Artaxerxes king of Persia (445 BC) to some time after his thirty-second year (about 433 BC; Nehemiah 13:6).
When it was written: Most of the book is Nehemiah's own first-person account, introduced as "The words of Nehemiah" (Nehemiah 1:1). In the Hebrew Bible it was originally joined with Ezra.
Where: The Persian palace at Shushan (Susa), and Jerusalem.
The world at the time: Nearly a century after the first exiles returned, Jerusalem's walls still lay in ruins. Local officials, Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite, and Geshem the Arabian, opposed any effort to make the city strong again.

**Nehemiah Prays for Jerusalem's Restoration (Nehemiah 1:1–2:20)** — After hearing Jerusalem's walls were broken, Nehemiah mourned, fasted, confessed Israel's sin, and prayed. Artaxerxes granted him authority and supplies to rebuild. Nehemiah inspected the walls, rallied the people, and answered opponents by trusting God.

**Jerusalem's Families Rebuild the Walls (Nehemiah 3:1–32)** — Priests, rulers, craftsmen, merchants, Levites, and families repaired assigned sections of Jerusalem's gates and walls. The record preserves their names, including those who worked near their homes; the nobles of Tekoa did not join.

**Opposition Threatens the Rebuilding Effort (Nehemiah 4:1–23)** — Sanballat and Tobiah mocked and threatened the builders. Nehemiah prayed, stationed guards, and had workers build with weapons nearby while a trumpeter stood ready to summon defense.

**Nehemiah Confronts Injustice Among the People (Nehemiah 5:1–19)** — Nehemiah confronted nobles exploiting poor families through debt and interest. They agreed to return property and stop the practice. He refused the governor's allowance and supported many people at his table.

**The Wall Is Completed Despite Opposition (Nehemiah 6:1–19)** — Nehemiah refused invitations, false accusations, and intimidation intended to stop him. Jerusalem's wall was completed in fifty-two days, and surrounding nations recognized God's help, though Tobiah continued his influence.

**Jerusalem's Gates and People Are Organized (Nehemiah 7:1–73)** — Nehemiah appointed guards, gatekeepers, singers, and Levites, then examined the register of families returning from captivity. Jerusalem remained sparsely populated, and genealogies determined priestly eligibility.

**Ezra Reads God's Law Before the People (Nehemiah 8:1–18)** — Ezra read Moses' law at the Water Gate while Levites explained it. The people were encouraged to rejoice, study the law, and restore the Feast of Tabernacles with great joy.

**Israel Confesses Its Sins and Remembers God's Mercy (Nehemiah 9:1–38)** — Israel fasted, confessed, and recalled God's faithfulness from creation, Abraham, Egypt, Sinai, wilderness provision, and the land. The Levites acknowledged repeated rebellion and God's mercy, then resolved to make a written covenant.

**The People Renew Their Covenant with God (Nehemiah 10:1–39)** — Leaders and families committed to obey God's law, honor Sabbath and sabbatical requirements, avoid unfaithful marriages, and support temple worship through offerings, firstfruits, wood, and tithes.

**Families Settle in Jerusalem and Judah (Nehemiah 11:1–12:26)** — One in ten people was chosen by lot to live in Jerusalem, with others volunteering. The record names residents, priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers, and the families responsible for restored worship.

**Jerusalem's Rebuilt Wall Is Dedicated to God (Nehemiah 12:27–47)** — Levites, priests, singers, and two great thanksgiving processions dedicated the wall with purification, music, sacrifice, and joy. Arrangements supported singers, gatekeepers, priests, and Levites.

**Nehemiah Confronts Renewed Disobedience (Nehemiah 13:1–31)** — Nehemiah returned to find Tobiah using temple space, Levites unsupported, Sabbath commerce, and unfaithful marriages. He removed Tobiah, restored offerings and guards, confronted wrongdoing, and asked God to remember him.

# Esther
When it happened: During the reign of King Ahasuerus of Persia, usually identified with Xerxes I (486–465 BC), from his third year (Esther 1:3) to his twelfth year and after.
When it was written: The book notes that "Mordecai wrote these things" in letters to the Jews (Esther 9:20); it was likely written in the Persian period not long after the events.
Where: Shushan (Susa), the royal city of the Persian Empire.
The world at the time: Persia ruled 127 provinces "from India even unto Ethiopia" (Esther 1:1). Many Jews still lived scattered throughout the empire after the exile, and a royal law, once sealed, could not be reversed.

**King Ahasuerus Removes Queen Vashti (Esther 1:1–22)** — At a royal feast in Shushan, Vashti refused the king's command to appear. Ahasuerus removed her as queen and issued a decree throughout his empire.

**Esther Becomes Queen of Persia (Esther 2:1–23)** — Esther, a Jewish orphan raised by Mordecai, became queen while concealing her identity. Mordecai uncovered a plot against Ahasuerus, which was recorded but not immediately rewarded.

**Haman Plans to Destroy the Jews (Esther 3:1–15)** — Haman became furious when Mordecai refused to bow. He cast Pur and secured the king's sealed decree ordering the destruction of Jews across the Persian Empire.

**Mordecai Calls Esther to Act (Esther 4:1–17)** — Mordecai urged Esther to plead for her people despite the danger of entering the king's court uninvited. Esther called for a three-day fast and resolved to go before the king.

**Esther Invites the King and Haman to a Feast (Esther 5:1–14)** — The king extended his golden scepter to Esther. She invited him and Haman to two feasts. Haman's hatred of Mordecai led him to prepare a gallows.

**Mordecai Receives an Unexpected Honor (Esther 6:1–14)** — The king's sleepless night revealed Mordecai's unrewarded loyalty. Haman, planning Mordecai's death, was instead ordered to lead him through the city in royal honor.

**Esther Exposes Haman's Plot (Esther 7:1–10)** — At Esther's second feast, she revealed that Haman planned the destruction of her people. The king ordered Haman hanged on the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai.

**Esther and Mordecai Secure a New Decree (Esther 8:1–17)** — Esther received Haman's estate and Mordecai the signet ring. Because the first decree could not be revoked, a new decree authorized Jews throughout the empire to gather and defend themselves.

**The Jews Defend Themselves and Establish Purim (Esther 9:1–32)** — The Jews prevailed over their enemies on the days Haman had chosen for their destruction, without taking spoil. Mordecai and Esther established Purim to remember their deliverance.

**Mordecai Becomes a Great Leader in Persia (Esther 10:1–3)** — Mordecai became second to Ahasuerus and sought the welfare and peace of his people. Esther closes with the Jews' deliverance from Haman's planned destruction.
